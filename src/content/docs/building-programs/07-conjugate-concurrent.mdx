## In one minute

**Concurrent** training develops several qualities (strength, speed, muscle, technique) in the same week, instead of one block at a time. The **Westside conjugate method** is a well-known version of it: a weekly **max-effort** day with a rotating special exercise, a **dynamic-effort** day of fast sets with light loads, and **repetition-method** accessories for muscle. It was built in multi-ply equipped lifting, so some of its tools need changing for raw lifters, who need much more practice of the competition lifts. Research supports some of its parts, such as planned exercise variation and lifting with maximal intent, but no controlled trials have tested the whole system in raw powerlifters.

## Two words that get mixed up

"Concurrent" and "conjugate" are used loosely, and they do not always mean the same thing.

| Term | What it usually means | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **Concurrent (complex-parallel) training** | Several qualities trained together in the same week or cycle, with the emphasis shifting slowly over the season | Traditional Soviet periodization |
| **Conjugate sequence system** | Short, concentrated blocks in a planned order, where each block's work supports the next | Verkhoshansky; described in Siff's *Supertraining* |
| **Westside "conjugate method"** | A concurrent weekly template: max effort, dynamic effort and repetition work every week, with special exercises rotated often | Louie Simmons, Westside Barbell (USA) |
| **Concurrent training (cardio + lifting)** | Endurance and strength training in the same program | A different topic, covered in Part 3, Chapter 7 |

<Evidence level="practice" /> The word "conjugate" in Verkhoshansky's work describes a **sequence of blocks**, which is closer to block periodization than to Westside (Siff). In this manual's reading, Westside borrowed the word from Soviet sources, but its weekly template is really a **concurrent** system: it trains maximal strength, speed and muscle at the same time. This chapter uses "conjugate" for the Westside-style method, because that is how most coaches use it today.

:::tip[In plain words]
"Concurrent" means training several things at once, every week. "Conjugate" usually refers to the Westside system, which does exactly that: heavy days, fast days and muscle-building work all in the same week.
:::

## The Soviet concurrent idea

In traditional Soviet periodization, an athlete trained many qualities in parallel. Early in the season the mix leaned towards general work and volume; closer to competition it leaned towards specific, heavy work. Nothing was dropped completely.

**Why it appealed:** no quality is left untrained for long, so nothing fades while another is being built.

**The critique.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Issurin (2010) argued that this "mixed" training has real limits for high-level athletes:

- Different qualities can produce **conflicting adaptations** when trained together.
- Long periods of training many things at once can create **excessive fatigue**.
- Spreading work across many targets gives each one a **diluted stimulus**.

These points are theory and coaching experience, not results from powerlifting trials. They are the reason block periodization was developed (see [Chapter 6](/lets-git-it/building-programs/06-block-periodization/)).

**The counterpoint.** Powerlifting has only three closely related lifts and one main quality: maximal strength. The "conflict" between qualities is much smaller than in a sport that needs, say, sprint speed and endurance. This is one reason concurrent approaches remain popular in powerlifting, including Sheiko's high-frequency system (Part 2, Chapter 12).

:::tip[In plain words]
The old Soviet approach trained everything a little bit, all year, and slowly shifted the focus. Critics say that spreads your effort too thin. In powerlifting, where you only need to get strong at three similar lifts, that problem is smaller.
:::

## The Westside conjugate method

<Evidence level="practice" /> The Westside system is coaching practice. It is described here as it is commonly taught; details vary between coaches who use it.

### The four-day week

| Day | Session | Main work |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Max-effort lower | Work up to a 1RM (sometimes a 3RM) in a squat, good morning or deadlift variation |
| 2 | Max-effort upper | Work up to a 1RM (or 3RM) in a bench variation (floor press, board press, close-grip) |
| 3 | Dynamic-effort lower | Many fast doubles of box squats, plus speed deadlifts |
| 4 | Dynamic-effort upper | Many fast triples of bench press |

Each session finishes with **repetition-method** accessories. About 72 hours separate the max-effort and dynamic-effort days for the same body region.

### The three methods

**1. Max-effort method.** Lift the heaviest load possible for 1–3 reps. The exercise is a **special exercise** (a variation), not the competition lift, and it is **rotated** often, commonly every 1–3 weeks.

The reasoning: true maximal loads are the best stimulus for maximal strength, but grinding the same lift to a max every week quickly stalls progress and wears the lifter down. Rotating the exercise is meant to keep the max-effort stimulus while avoiding that staleness.

:::tip[In plain words]
On max-effort days you work up to your heaviest single or triple for that day, but on a different exercise every week or few weeks. You get the heavy stimulus without hammering the same lift again and again.
:::

**2. Dynamic-effort method.** Light to moderate loads lifted **as fast as possible**, for many sets of low reps with short rests. Classic Westside prescriptions use roughly half of the lifter's max on the bar, often plus bands or chains, for around 8–12 sets of 2 (squat) or 3 (bench), with rests of about a minute.

The reasoning: moving a moderate load at maximal speed trains rate of force development and lets the lifter practise many technically clean reps with little fatigue.

:::tip[In plain words]
On dynamic-effort days the weight is light, but you push it as fast as you can, for lots of short sets. It's practice and speed work, not a grind.
:::

**3. Repetition method.** Submaximal loads for sets of moderate to high reps, taken close to failure. At Westside this is mostly accessory work: rows, triceps, hamstrings, low back and upper back. Its job is muscle growth and fixing weak muscle groups.

:::tip[In plain words]
The repetition method is ordinary bodybuilding-style work: moderate weights for 8–15 reps, hard sets, to build the muscles that move the big lifts.
:::

**4. Accommodating resistance.** Bands and chains make the load heavier as the bar rises. They match the strength curve of the lifts, where the lifter is weakest near the bottom or lower-middle of the lift and strongest near lockout.

:::tip[In plain words]
Bands and chains make the bar get heavier as you lift it up. That way the top of the lift, where you're strongest, gets harder too. They're an optional tool, not a must.
:::

## What the research says

There are **no controlled trials of the full Westside system in raw powerlifters** that this manual could verify. What exists is evidence on its separate parts.

**Rotating exercises (max-effort rotation).**

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 8 studies (241 young men) concluded that **some planned variation** of exercises can improve strength and regional muscle growth, but **excessive, random rotation** or rotating exercises that do the same job may hold gains back (Kassiano et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 49 physically active people training twice a week for 12 weeks, the group that **varied exercises while keeping intensity constant** gained the most squat strength, more than groups that kept the same exercise or varied the loading scheme (Fonseca et al., 2014).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 21 trained men, changing exercises **randomly every session** for 8 weeks gave similar bench and squat 1RM gains to a fixed program and improved motivation to train (Baz-Valle et al., 2019).

**Taken together:** varying exercises does not seem to cost strength, and planned variation may help. None of these studies tested weekly rotation of special exercises to a true max, in powerlifters, against a program built on the competition lifts.

**Speed and intent (dynamic effort).**

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 20 participants training the bench press for 6 weeks, lifting every rep at **maximal intended velocity** improved 1RM by about 18%, compared with about 10% when the same loads were lifted deliberately at half speed (González-Badillo et al., 2014).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 16 men and women doing an ankle-muscle exercise for 16 weeks, the **intention** to contract explosively produced the same high-velocity training effect whether or not the limb actually moved fast (Behm & Sale, 1993). Intent matters, not only bar speed.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 22 young men training the squat for 8 weeks, stopping each set after a **20% drop in bar speed** gave similar strength gains to allowing a 40% drop, with 40% fewer reps, and larger jump gains. The 40% group gained more muscle in two of the four quadriceps muscles (Pareja-Blanco et al., 2017). Short, fast sets build strength efficiently; longer, grindier sets add more size.

These studies support **lifting with maximal intent** and **stopping sets before they slow down a lot**. They do not show that very light dynamic-effort loads (around 50%) build more maximal strength than heavier work for a raw lifter.

**Bands and chains (accommodating resistance).**

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 44 resistance-trained college athletes, adding elastic bands to squat and bench for 7 weeks improved squat 1RM by about 16.5 kg vs 6.8 kg, and bench 1RM by about 6.7 kg vs 3.3 kg, compared with free weights alone (Anderson et al., 2008).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 24 trained athletes over 4 weeks, chain training improved lower-body maximal strength more than constant resistance, but not upper-body strength or power (Ataee et al., 2014).

Both are short studies in non-powerlifters, so treat bands and chains as an **optional tool**, not a requirement.

**Mixing qualities in the same week.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 18 college-aged male powerlifters training 3 days per week for 6 weeks, a weekly order of **hypertrophy, power, then strength** days gave greater bench press gains than hypertrophy, strength, then power, with no significant difference for squat or deadlift (Zourdos et al., 2016). Training different qualities in one week clearly works; the best order is not settled from one small study.

**What lifters actually do.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In a survey of 28 elite British powerlifters, most said they tried to lift both maximal and submaximal loads explosively (79% and 82%), 57% used chains and 39% used elastic bands (Swinton et al., 2009). Westside-style tools were common in that group, but a survey shows what lifters do, not what works best.

:::tip[In plain words]
Science supports some pieces of the conjugate method: changing exercises in a planned way, and pushing every rep as fast as you can. It hasn't tested the full system in raw lifters. Bands and chains helped in a couple of short studies, but they aren't essential.
:::

## What translates to raw IPF lifting, and what doesn't

<Evidence level="practice" /> Westside developed in **multi-ply equipped** federations, where supportive suits and shirts help most at the bottom of the lift and the lifter must be strong at lockout. Board presses, bands, chains and wide-stance box squats fit that sport very well. Raw (classic) lifting, including IPF classic, is different: the hardest part of most lifts is near the bottom, and there is no gear to help. The IPF also runs an equipped division, but with single-ply gear, which is not the same as multi-ply. None of this makes one sport better than the other; it means the tools must be matched to the lifter.

| Westside element | Keep for raw lifters? | Raw adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| Rotating max-effort variations | **Yes, with limits** | Choose close variations (pause squat, pin squat, Spoto press, deficit deadlift); rotate every 3–4 weeks, not weekly; put the competition lift in the rotation regularly |
| True 1RM every week | **Rarely** | Work up to a heavy single or triple at RPE 8.5–9 instead of RPE 10; add back-off sets on the competition lift |
| Dynamic effort at ~50% | **Modified** | Use ~60–75% on the competition lift, competition stance and depth, 6–10 sets of 1–3; stop if speed drops |
| Box squats | **Optional** | Use competition squats for most work; keep box squats as an accessory if they help |
| Bands and chains | **Optional** | Fine as a variation; don't let them replace bottom-end strength work |
| Repetition-method accessories | **Yes** | Choose accessories from weak points; count them in weekly volume |
| Competition-lift practice | **Must add** | Raw lifters need frequent practice of the exact competition lifts, which pure Westside programs often lack |

**Why competition-lift practice matters so much for raw lifters.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Specificity is the first training principle ([Part 1 · Chapter 3](/lets-git-it/foundations/03-core-training-principles/)). In equipped lifting, much of the specific practice happens in gear, at heavy loads, close to the meet. A raw lifter's competition lift is the same movement they can train every week, so most of their heavy work should be the competition lifts or very close variations.

:::tip[In plain words]
The conjugate method was built for lifters wearing very supportive suits and shirts. Raw lifters can borrow its best ideas, like heavy days on rotating variations and fast sets, but they need to practise the real squat, bench and deadlift much more often.
:::

## Who it suits

- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Good fit:** intermediate and advanced lifters who stall on fixed programs, who are motivated by variety, or who need to work around a sore lift with a variation.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Poor fit:** novices (they still progress on simple linear programs and need to learn the competition lifts), and lifters close to a meet (they need competition-lift specificity).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Common hybrid:** run a conjugate-style off-season, then switch to a block of competition-lift work before a meet (see [Chapter 6](/lets-git-it/building-programs/06-block-periodization/) and Part 4).

:::tip[In plain words]
Conjugate-style training works best for experienced lifters who like variety and are far from a competition. Beginners and lifters getting ready for a meet usually do better practising the competition lifts themselves.
:::

## Sample programs

Intermediate raw lifter: squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. RPE-to-kg conversions use the chart in [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/); dynamic-effort loads are percentages of the competition 1RM.

### Program 1: Raw-adapted conjugate week (4 days)

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 · ME lower | Pause squat (3 s) | Work up to 1 × 1 | RPE 8.5–9 |
| 1 · ME lower | Squat (back-off) | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 (~150 kg) |
| 1 · ME lower | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| 1 · ME lower | Back extension | 3 × 12 | RPE 8 |
| 2 · ME upper | Spoto press (pause just above chest) | Work up to 1 × 3 | RPE 9 |
| 2 · ME upper | Bench press (back-off) | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| 2 · ME upper | Dumbbell row | 4 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 2 · ME upper | Triceps pushdown | 3 × 15 | RPE 8 |
| 3 · DE lower | Squat (competition stance and depth) | 8 × 2 | ~65% (117.5 kg), rest 60–90 s |
| 3 · DE lower | Deadlift | 8 × 1 | ~70% (147.5 kg), rest 45–60 s |
| 3 · DE lower | Bulgarian split squat | 3 × 8 each leg | RPE 8 |
| 3 · DE lower | Hamstring curl | 3 × 12 | RPE 8 |
| 4 · DE upper | Bench press | 9 × 3 | ~60% (72.5 kg), rest 60 s |
| 4 · DE upper | Close-grip bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 4 · DE upper | Pull-up | 4 × 8 | RPE 8 |
| 4 · DE upper | Lateral raise | 3 × 15 | RPE 8 |

**Why it's built this way:** the max-effort days keep the heavy stimulus, but at RPE 8.5–9 rather than a true max, so the lifter can do it every week. Back-off sets on the **competition** squat and bench add the specific practice raw lifters need. Dynamic-effort work uses heavier loads than classic Westside, in competition form, and every rep is lifted with maximal intent. **If the bar slows visibly, stop the exercise for the day.** Weekly hard sets (counting ME singles and back-offs): squat ~4 plus 8 fast doubles, bench ~4 plus 9 fast triples and 4 close-grip sets, deadlift 8 fast singles plus Romanian deadlifts.

### Program 2: A 10-week max-effort rotation

Max-effort exercise and back-off sets for days 1 and 2 of Program 1. Days 3 and 4 stay the same, with dynamic-effort loads rising about 2.5% every 3 weeks. The one exception is weeks 4–6: day 1 is a deadlift variation, so on day 3 the dynamic-effort squat is replaced by heavy competition squat practice (see the last column).

| Weeks | ME lower (day 1) | Back-off | ME upper (day 2) | Back-off | Day 3 squat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | Pause squat (3 s), 1 × 1 | Squat 3 × 3 @ RPE 7 (~150 kg) | Spoto press, 1 × 3 | Bench 3 × 5 @ RPE 7 (~95 kg) | DE squat as in Program 1 |
| 4–6 | Deficit deadlift (3–5 cm), 1 × 2 | Deadlift 3 × 3 @ RPE 7 (~175 kg) | Close-grip bench, 1 × 3 | Bench 3 × 5 @ RPE 7 (~95 kg) | Squat 3 × 3 @ RPE 7 (~150 kg), replacing the DE squat |
| 7–9 | Competition squat, 1 × 1 | Squat 2 × 3 @ RPE 7.5 (~152.5 kg) | Competition bench, 1 × 1 | Bench 3 × 3 @ RPE 7.5 (~102.5 kg) | DE squat as in Program 1 |
| 10 | Deload: no max-effort work; all lifts 3 × 3 at ~70% | | | | |

**Within each 3-week wave:** week 1 top set at RPE 8, week 2 at RPE 8.5, week 3 at RPE 9. Write the load down and try to beat it next time the exercise comes round. **Why:** rotating every 3 weeks gives enough exposures to get better at each variation, consistent with planned rather than random variation (Kassiano et al., 2022). Weeks 7–9 put the **competition lifts** in the max-effort slot, so the lifter gets heavy, specific singles and an honest progress check. For the 180 kg squatter, an RPE 9 single in week 9 near 172.5 kg (95.5%) means the max is about where it was; 177.5 kg or more suggests real progress.

### Program 3: Concurrent week, Soviet style (3 days)

Each week trains muscle, speed and heavy strength, in the hypertrophy → power → strength order that did well in one small study of powerlifters (Zourdos et al., 2016).

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon · Hypertrophy | Squat | 4 × 8 | RPE 7 (~127.5 kg) |
| Mon · Hypertrophy | Bench press | 4 × 8 | RPE 7 (~85 kg) |
| Mon · Hypertrophy | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| Mon · Hypertrophy | Barbell row | 4 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| Wed · Power | Squat | 6 × 2 | 70% (125 kg), maximal intent, rest 90 s |
| Wed · Power | Bench press | 6 × 3 | 65% (77.5 kg), maximal intent, rest 90 s |
| Wed · Power | Deadlift | 6 × 1 | 70% (147.5 kg), maximal intent |
| Wed · Power | Pull-up | 3 × 8 | RPE 8 |
| Fri · Strength | Squat | 4 × 3 | RPE 8 (~155 kg) |
| Fri · Strength | Bench press | 5 × 3 | RPE 8 (~102.5 kg) |
| Fri · Strength | Deadlift | 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~180 kg) |

**Why it's built this way:** every week touches all three qualities, so nothing fades. The light power day sits between the two harder days and doubles as technique practice. Over a 4–6 week cycle, keep this structure and progress the strength day by small load increases at the same RPE; this is close to the undulating models covered in Part 2, Chapter 5.

## Common mistakes

- **Copying an equipped template for a raw lifter.** Board presses, high box squats and heavy bands train lockout strength a raw lifter may not need most.
- **Grinding true maxes every week.** RPE 10 attempts every week build fatigue fast; cap most max-effort work at RPE 8.5–9.
- **Rotating too often or too far.** Random weekly swaps, or variations that barely resemble the competition lift, make progress hard to measure and may hold gains back.
- **Lifting dynamic-effort sets slowly.** If the bar isn't moving fast, the set is just light, ordinary volume. Push every rep at maximal intent, or stop.
- **Leaving out the competition lifts.** Raw lifters need regular heavy practice of the exact squat, bench and deadlift.
- **Calling Westside "the conjugate sequence system."** They are different ideas; knowing the difference helps when reading older Soviet-based sources.

## Key takeaways

- Concurrent training develops several qualities in the same week; Westside's conjugate method is a well-known version of it.
- The method rests on max-effort work with rotating special exercises, fast dynamic-effort sets and repetition-method accessories.
- It developed in multi-ply equipped lifting; raw lifters should keep more competition-lift practice, use close variations and cap most max-effort work below RPE 10.
- Research supports planned exercise variation and lifting with maximal intent; bands and chains have only short, small studies behind them.
- No controlled trials have tested the full conjugate system in raw powerlifters, so treat it as one sound option, not the best one.
