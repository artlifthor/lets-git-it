## In one minute

A **deload** is a short, planned period of easier training, usually about a week, that lets accumulated fatigue clear so the lifter is ready for the next block. Strength athletes and coaches typically deload every 4–6 weeks by cutting sets and reps, lowering loads or effort, and keeping the same exercises and training days. Direct research on deloads is thin: surveys and expert consensus describe what coaches do, while experiments suggest that a short reduction is fine but stopping training entirely costs some strength. Good fatigue management **within** a block, using RPE caps, sensible volume, and smart exercise choice, means deloads work better and are needed less often. Learn to recognize real accumulated fatigue (performance at known loads falling over several sessions) and deload when it appears, even if the plan says otherwise.

## What a deload is, and why it works

<Evidence level="practice" /> An international panel of strength and physique coaches agreed on this definition: a deload is a **period of reduced training stress meant to reduce physical and mental fatigue, help recovery, and prepare the lifter for the training that follows** (Bell et al., 2023). It is not a holiday and not a taper for a meet.

<Evidence level="practice" /> The reasoning comes from the **fitness–fatigue model** ([Part 1 · Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/foundations/02-stress-recovery-adaptation/)): hard training builds fitness and fatigue together; fatigue fades faster than fitness, so a short easy period lets performance rise. This is well-established theory, but it has not been tested directly with deloads in powerlifters.

**Deload vs. taper.** A deload resets fatigue between training blocks so the next block can be hard. A taper is the planned reduction before a competition to peak performance; it is covered in Part 4, Chapter 1: The science of peaking and tapering.

:::tip[In plain words]
A deload is an easy week on purpose. Hard training makes you fitter and more tired at the same time. The tiredness wears off faster than the fitness, so a few easy days let your real strength show and get you ready to train hard again.
:::

## What the research says

### What coaches and athletes actually do

- <Evidence level="practice" /> In a survey of 246 competitive strength and physique athletes (about 8 years of training), everyone deloaded. A deload lasted about **6–7 days** and came about **every 5–6 weeks**. Athletes cut reps per set and sets per week, lowered the load, and stopped further from failure, but **kept the same training frequency and exercises**. Most planned deloads in advance, sometimes combined with taking one early when performance stalled or soreness or joint aches rose (Rogerson et al., 2024).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> In interviews with 18 experienced weightlifting, powerlifting and bodybuilding coaches, deloads typically lasted **5–7 days** and were programmed **every 4–6 weeks**, but timing varied a lot. There was **no single method**: coaches mainly reduced volume (fewer sets and reps) and effort (more reps in reserve, or lighter loads), sometimes changing exercises too (Bell et al., 2022).

These studies describe common practice. They do not show which method works best.

### Experiments on reducing or stopping training

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 39 trained men and women on a 9-week high-volume program, a **one-week complete break** from training in the middle gave similar muscle growth to training straight through, but **smaller lower-body strength gains** (Coleman et al., 2024). Note this tested stopping completely, not a reduced-load deload; it suggests a deload should reduce training, not remove it.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 8 trained men, 3.5 or 5.5 days off after a 4-week strength block did not reduce strength. Isometric bench press force and jump height were higher than before training, possibly because fatigue had cleared (Pritchard et al., 2018). Very short breaks do not seem to cost strength.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 14 previously untrained young men training the bench press for 24 weeks, three 6-week training periods separated by 3-week breaks produced similar overall muscle and strength gains to training continuously (Ogasawara et al., 2013).
- <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 103 studies found that stopping resistance training **reduces strength**, and the loss grows with the length of the break; losses were larger in older and inactive people (Bosquet et al., 2013).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> In interviews, elite New Zealand raw powerlifters said that tapers went poorly when they took **a week or more completely off** before a meet (Pritchard et al., 2016).

**Bottom line:** <Evidence level="practice" /> deloads are supported mainly by theory and wide practice, not by direct trials. The evidence that exists points to: reduce training rather than stop it; keep breaks from all lifting short (a few days); and expect no harm to muscle from a well-placed easy week.

:::tip[In plain words]
Almost every serious lifter takes easy weeks, usually about one week every five or six. Scientists haven't tested deloads much yet. What we do know is that a few days off can help, but a whole week off can cost some strength, so keep training during a deload, just lighter and less.
:::

## Types of deload

<Evidence level="practice" /> Choose the type by what kind of fatigue has built up and what comes next (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith; Bell et al., 2022).

| Type | What changes | What stays | Best when | Example (squat 180 kg lifter, usual 5 × 5 at ~145 kg) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Volume deload** | Sets cut by 40–60%; reps per set may drop | Load roughly the same or a little lower | Default choice; keeps skill and "feel" for heavy weights | 2 × 5 at ~140 kg |
| **Intensity deload** | Load cut by 10–20% | Sets roughly the same | Joint aches, after a very heavy block | 4 × 5 at ~120 kg |
| **Combined deload** | Sets and load both cut | Exercises, days | After a high-volume block that ended near MRV | 2 × 5 at ~125 kg |
| **Effort deload** | All sets capped at RPE 5–6 (4+ reps in reserve) | Exercises, days | Lifter tired but loads already modest | 3 × 5 at ~RPE 5–6 |
| **Exercise-change deload** | Swap stressful exercises for easier ones (e.g., deadlift → RDL; low-bar → high-bar) | Training rhythm | A specific joint or area is irritated | Squat replaced by belt squat or leg press |
| **Short break** | No lifting for 2–5 days | — | Illness, travel, life chaos, or very high fatigue | 3 days off, then a light session |

**Frequency during deloads.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Keep the usual training days if possible, as surveyed athletes do (Rogerson et al., 2024). Shorter sessions on the same days keep the habit and the skill practice.

:::tip[In plain words]
There are several ways to make a week easier: do fewer sets, use lighter weights, stop sets earlier, swap tough exercises for easier ones, or take a few days off. Most of the time, the best choice is doing fewer sets with similar weights, so you stay used to heavy loads.
:::

## Planned vs. reactive deloads

| | Planned (proactive) | Reactive (autoregulated) |
|---|---|---|
| **How** | Deload on a schedule, e.g., every 4th–6th week | Deload when fatigue signs appear |
| **Strengths** | Simple, predictable, prevents fatigue from piling up; fits block structure | Fits the individual; no wasted easy weeks if still fresh |
| **Weaknesses** | May come too early or too late for this lifter | Needs good monitoring and honest reporting; easy to delay too long |
| **Best for** | Most intermediates; programs that ramp volume to MRV | Experienced lifters with good logs; irregular schedules |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Most athletes in the survey used a planned approach, alone or combined with a reactive one (Rogerson et al., 2024). A sensible default is to **plan a deload at the end of each mesocycle** (every 4–6 weeks) and **allow an early one** if fatigue signs appear. Novices on linear progression rarely need full deload weeks; a reset of the load does the job ([Part 1 · Chapter 7](/lets-git-it/foundations/07-novice-linear-progression/)). Lifters with high life stress, poor sleep or a calorie deficit often need deloads more often.

:::tip[In plain words]
You can schedule easy weeks in advance, take them when you feel run down, or both. The simplest method is to plan one every four to six weeks and take one early if you clearly need it.
:::

## Recognizing accumulated fatigue

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 56 studies found that **athletes' own ratings** (mood, stress, perceived recovery) tracked training load more sensitively and consistently than common objective measures such as blood markers or heart rate (Saw et al., 2016). Asking the lifter good questions is a valid tool.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of overtraining in resistance training found that **no marker other than a sustained drop in performance** has been established as a reliable sign of overtraining (Grandou et al., 2020). Performance in the log is the most important signal.

<Evidence level="practice" /> **Three levels of fatigue.** These are the standard terms in the overtraining research (used, for example, by Grandou et al., 2020); the time frames are typical descriptions, not fixed cut-offs:

- **Functional overreaching:** a short dip in performance after hard training that recovers within days to a couple of weeks and is followed by improvement. This is what the last week of a mesocycle aims for.
- **Non-functional overreaching:** a longer dip (weeks to months) with no improvement afterwards. A sign the plan was too hard for too long.
- **Overtraining syndrome:** long-lasting performance loss (months), often with other symptoms. Rare in well-planned strength training; see a qualified health professional if a lifter's performance, mood or health stays poor for weeks despite rest.

<Evidence level="practice" /> **Signals to watch and what to do** (thresholds are coaching rules of thumb):

| Signal | How to check | Deload or adjust when… |
|---|---|---|
| RPE at a known load | Same load and reps as earlier in the block | RPE is 1+ higher than expected for 2 sessions in a row |
| Estimated 1RM from top sets | Calculate from the RPE chart ([Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/)) | Falls ~3–5% or more across 2–3 sessions of the same lift |
| Bar speed / grinding | Video or velocity device; or lifter's report | Usually fast reps become slow at the same load |
| Joint aches | Lifter's daily rating | New aches that persist across sessions, or aches that change technique |
| Sleep, motivation, stress | Quick 1–5 rating before each session | Two or more items low for most of a week |
| Missed reps | Log | Misses at loads that should be RPE 8 or below |

One bad session is not accumulated fatigue; look for **trends across several sessions**. For pain that is sharp, worsening or lasting, see a qualified health professional rather than just deloading.

:::tip[In plain words]
The clearest sign you need an easy week is that weights you know start feeling heavier and your lifts go down, session after session. How you feel matters too: bad sleep, low motivation and achy joints that stick around are warning signs. One bad day doesn't count; a bad trend does.
:::

## Managing fatigue within a block

A good block keeps fatigue under control so the deload only has to clear what's left.

**1. Cap effort on the competition lifts.**

- <Evidence level="strong" /> Training to failure gives no extra strength compared with stopping short (Grgic et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-regression found strength gains were similar across a wide range of reps in reserve, while muscle growth improved as sets got closer to failure (Robinson et al., 2024). The authors called the analysis exploratory.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 10 trained men, squat and bench sets taken to failure caused more fatigue than the same work stopped halfway to failure. Recovery stayed behind for up to 24–48 hours (Morán-Navarro et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Rule: competition lifts mostly at RPE 6–8.5, with RPE 9+ only for planned top sets; accessories can go to RPE 8–10 because they cost less recovery.

**2. Stop sets when quality drops.**

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 22 young men squatting for 8 weeks, stopping each set when bar speed had fallen 20% produced similar squat strength gains to stopping at 40%, with **40% fewer reps**; the 40% group gained more muscle in some measures (Pareja-Blanco et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Without a velocity device, use RPE caps and load drops: if a set exceeds its RPE cap, reduce the load 5% or stop the exercise. Fatigue-based methods such as load drops and repeats are covered in Part 3, Chapter 2: Fatigue-based autoregulation (Tuchscherer).

**3. Keep volume inside the landmarks.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Ramp sets toward MRV only at the end of a mesocycle, then deload ([Chapter 8](/lets-git-it/building-programs/08-volume-landmarks/)).

**4. Spread the work.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Split a lift's weekly sets across more sessions so no single session is crushing ([Chapter 9](/lets-git-it/building-programs/09-training-frequency/)). Keep heavy squat and heavy deadlift sessions 48–72 hours apart.

**5. Choose lower-fatigue exercises when fatigue is high.** <Evidence level="practice" />

| Higher fatigue cost | Lower fatigue alternative (similar purpose) |
|---|---|
| Deficit or conventional deadlift for volume | Block pull, RDL, back extension |
| High-rep back squat (8+) | Leg press, belt squat, hack squat |
| Heavy barbell rows from the floor | Chest-supported row, cable row |
| New, unfamiliar exercises late in a block | Familiar exercises (new ones cause more soreness) |

**6. Cut accessories first.** <Evidence level="practice" /> When fatigue rises mid-block, drop accessory sets before touching the competition lifts.

**7. Adjust for life stress.** <Evidence level="practice" /> During exams, work deadlines, travel, illness, or a weight cut, lower the volume target and RPE caps before fatigue shows up in the bar ([Part 1 · Chapter 10](/lets-git-it/foundations/10-recovery-sleep-stress/)).

:::tip[In plain words]
The best way to manage tiredness is to not create more than you need. Stop most sets with a couple of reps left, end a set when it starts to grind, spread your sets over the week, and drop extra exercises first when you're worn out. Then your easy weeks only need to clean up a little.
:::

## Sample programs

Default lifter: intermediate man, ~83 kg, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. Percentages are of 1RM; RPE loads from [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg.

### Program 1: Planned combined deload after a high-volume block

The deload week (week 5) for the mesocycle in [Chapter 8, Program 1](/lets-git-it/building-programs/08-volume-landmarks/), which ended near MRV (squat 14, bench 16, deadlift 8 sets). Same days, same main exercises; about one-third of the sets; loads about 10–15% below week 4.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 2 × 5 | ~70% (125 kg), RPE ≤6 |
| 1 | Bench press | 2 × 6 | ~70% (85 kg), RPE ≤6 |
| 2 | Deadlift | 2 × 4 | ~72% (150 kg), RPE ≤6 |
| 2 | Bench press | 2 × 6 | ~70% (85 kg) |
| 3 | Pause squat (2 s) | 2 × 3 | ~65% (117.5 kg) |
| 3 | Close-grip bench press | 1 × 6 | RPE ~6 |
| 4 | Leg press, back extension | 2 × 10 each | RPE ~6 |

**Weekly sets:** squat 4, bench 5, deadlift 2 (about MV). The deficit deadlift is removed entirely, because the deadlift recovers slowest. **Next week** starts the new mesocycle near MEV.

### Program 2: Volume deload with heavy doubles (keeping intensity)

For a lifter coming off a heavy strength block, or 6–8 weeks from a meet, who needs to clear fatigue but keep the feel of heavy loads. Volume drops about 50–60%; one lighter top set per lift stays.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 1 × 2, then 2 × 3 | RPE 7 (~155 kg), then ~80% (145 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 1 × 2, then 2 × 3 | RPE 7 (~102.5 kg), then ~80% (95 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 1 × 2, then 1 × 3 | RPE 7 (~180 kg), then ~80% (167.5 kg) |
| 2 | Bench press | 3 × 3 | ~80% (95 kg) |
| 3 | Squat | 2 × 3 | ~75% (135 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press | 2 × 5 | ~75% (90 kg) |

**Weekly sets:** squat 5, bench 8, deadlift 2. No accessories, or only light ones. **Why:** heavy-ish doubles at RPE 7 keep the lifter used to heavy weights (and confident), while the low volume lets fatigue clear. This is close to the "volume down, intensity kept" pattern used in tapers, but milder and shorter.

### Program 3: Reactive mid-block reset (3–4 days)

Week 3 of the [Chapter 8](/lets-git-it/building-programs/08-volume-landmarks/) block. On day 1 the lifter's squat at 145 kg × 5 (RPE 7.5 the week before) is RPE 8.5–9, bench feels heavy too, sleep has been poor for a week due to work, and a knee aches.

**Decision rules**

| If… | Then… |
|---|---|
| One session is bad but sleep and mood are fine | Finish the session within RPE caps; carry on |
| RPE 1+ higher than expected for **2 sessions in a row**, or the 1–5 check-in is low on 2+ items | Do a 3–4 day reset (below) |
| Performance still down after the reset, or symptoms persist | Take the full deload week now; start the next block lower |
| Sharp or worsening pain | Stop that exercise and get it assessed by a qualified health professional |

**The reset (rest of week 3)**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Deadlift | 2 × 4 | ~72% (150 kg), RPE ≤6 |
| 2 | Bench press | 3 × 6 | ~70% (85 kg) |
| 3 | Belt squat or leg press (instead of pause squat, knee-friendly) | 3 × 10 | RPE 6–7 |
| 3 | Close-grip bench press | 2 × 6 | RPE 6 |
| 4 | Deficit deadlift: skip; back extension | 2 × 12 | RPE 6 |

**After the reset:** repeat week 3's plan the following week instead of moving to week 4. If week 3 then goes well, continue to week 4 with one fewer set per lift than planned; the lifter's MRV for this block is lower than estimated. Record what happened, so the next mesocycle's volume ramp starts and finishes lower.

## Common mistakes

- **Deloading by stopping training completely.** A full week off can cost strength; reduce training instead, and keep complete breaks to a few days.
- **Making the deload a "test week".** Maxing out or hitting PRs during a deload defeats its purpose.
- **Deloading too rarely**, then losing weeks to a forced break when fatigue finally wins.
- **Deloading too often** for a lifter who is still progressing and fresh. Easy weeks have a cost too.
- **Reacting to one bad session.** Look for trends across several sessions.
- **Starting the next block where the last one ended.** After a deload, restart near MEV, not at MRV.
- **Ignoring the causes of fatigue.** If a lifter needs a deload every three weeks, fix the program (volume, RPE caps, exercise choice) or the recovery (sleep, food, stress).

## Key takeaways

- A deload is about a week of reduced training stress that lets fatigue clear before the next block.
- Strength athletes and coaches typically deload every 4–6 weeks, cutting sets, reps, load or effort while keeping the same exercises and days.
- Direct evidence is limited: a few days off may help, but a full week off can reduce strength gains; reduce training rather than stop.
- The most reliable sign of too much fatigue is a sustained drop in performance at known loads; the lifter's own ratings of sleep, mood and recovery are useful early warnings.
- Manage fatigue inside the block with RPE caps, stopping sets when quality drops, volume inside the landmarks, spread-out sessions and lower-fatigue exercise choices.
- Plan deloads by default and take an early one when the trend says so.
