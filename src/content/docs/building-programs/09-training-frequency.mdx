## In one minute

Frequency is how many sessions per week train a given lift. In equal-volume trials, training a lift anywhere from once to six times per week produced similar strength gains; one large meta-regression found a small, diminishing benefit of more frequent training. So frequency is mainly a **tool for distributing volume**: higher frequency gives shorter, fresher sessions, more practice of the competition skill, and room for more total volume. Each lift recovers at a different speed, so most intermediate lifters train the bench press 2–4 times, the squat 2–3 times and the deadlift 1–2 times per week, set by the weekly sets the lift needs, how quickly the lifter recovers, and the lifter's schedule.

## What counts as frequency

**Frequency per lift = the number of sessions per week with hard sets of that lift or a close variation.**

- A day with pause squats counts as a squat day. A day with only leg press does not.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Count only sessions with hard sets (about RPE 6 and above). A few light technique sets at the start of a bench day are useful practice but add little training stress.
- Frequency is counted **per lift**, not per week of gym visits. A 4-day program can train the bench press 4 times and the deadlift once.

:::tip[In plain words]
Frequency is how many days a week you train each lift. A 4-day program might have bench on all four days but deadlift on only one. Count the days you do real work sets of the lift or something very close to it.
:::

## What the research says

### When weekly volume is equal, frequency matters little for strength

- <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 22 studies found strength gains grew with frequency (effect sizes 0.74, 0.82, 0.93 and 1.08 for 1, 2, 3 and 4+ sessions per week). But when weekly volume was equated, **frequency had no significant effect**: the extra gains came from the extra volume that higher frequency usually brings (Grgic et al., 2018).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> In that analysis, frequency mattered more for multi-joint lifts and the upper body than for single-joint lifts and the lower body (Grgic et al., 2018). These were subgroup results, so treat them as hints, not rules.
- <Evidence level="strong" /> A later and larger meta-regression found that strength gains still rose as weekly frequency rose, **with diminishing returns**, while muscle growth showed little or no effect of frequency (Pelland et al., 2025).

### Trained lifters: 3 vs. 6, 1 vs. 5

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 28 trained men over 6 weeks, training **3 or 6 times per week**, with volume and intensity equated, produced almost identical squat, bench press, deadlift and powerlifting-total gains (squat about +17 kg in both groups; bench +7.8 vs +8.8 kg; deadlift +19 vs +21 kg) (Colquhoun et al., 2018).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 27 trained men over 6 weeks, 3 and 6 sessions per week with equal volume gave similar upper- and lower-body strength gains (Saric et al., 2019).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 23 well-trained men (about 7 years of training), training each muscle **once per week** or **five times per week**, with the same weekly sets, gave similar bench press and squat gains over 8 weeks (Gomes et al., 2019).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 18 trained men over 8 weeks, once-per-week and five-times-per-week routines gave similar 1RM gains, but the high-frequency group gained more muscle thickness in some sites (Zaroni et al., 2019).
- <Evidence level="strong" /> For muscle growth, a meta-analysis of 25 studies found no meaningful effect of frequency when volume is equated (Schoenfeld et al., 2019).

### Powerlifters specifically

<Evidence level="emerging" /> Research on competitive powerlifters is scarce. In a pilot study of 10 beginner-to-intermediate competitive powerlifters preparing for a national IPF-affiliate meet, daily singles at RPE 9–9.5 raised the total in 2 of 5 lifters and lowered it in 3. All 3 lifters on traditional periodized training held or improved their total. The authors suggested results "may be comparable", but the sample is far too small to recommend daily maxing; it mainly shows how individual the response is (Androulakis-Korakakis et al., 2018).

**What this means:** <Evidence level="practice" /> frequency on its own does not make a lifter stronger. Its value is that it lets the coach fit the right weekly volume into sessions the lifter can do well.

:::tip[In plain words]
If you do the same total number of sets each week, it makes little difference to your strength whether you split them over two days or six. How often you train mostly decides how the work is spread out: shorter, fresher sessions on more days, or longer sessions on fewer days.
:::

## Why frequency still matters

<Evidence level="practice" /> Even if equal-volume studies show no difference, coaches raise frequency for four practical reasons:

1. **Fresher sets.** A lifter doing 15 bench sets in two sessions does 7–8 sets per session; the later ones are slower and sloppier. Spread over four sessions, every set is closer to fresh.
2. **More skill practice.** Each session is another chance to rehearse competition technique, setup and commands. This is central to Sheiko's high-frequency approach (Sheiko; see [Chapter 12](/lets-git-it/building-programs/12-sheiko-style-programming/)).
3. **Room for more volume.** When a lift needs more weekly sets (approaching its MAV in [Chapter 8](/lets-git-it/building-programs/08-volume-landmarks/)), adding a session is easier to recover from than adding sets to existing sessions.
4. **Less soreness per session.** Smaller doses more often usually cause less soreness than large doses less often. This is widely observed in practice but has not been well tested in powerlifters.

The cost: more sessions take more time, and if total volume rises with frequency the lifter needs more recovery overall.

:::tip[In plain words]
Training a lift more often lets each session be shorter, so every set is better quality, and you get more practice at the lift. That's why many lifters bench three or four times a week, even though the total amount of work is what builds strength.
:::

## Frequency by lift: SRA

Each lift has its own **stimulus–recovery–adaptation (SRA)** curve: how long it takes to recover from a hard session and be ready to train it hard again ([Part 1 · Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/foundations/02-stress-recovery-adaptation/)). The heavier the lift, the more muscle involved, and the closer sets go to failure, the longer that curve.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 10 trained men, sets taken to failure on the bench press and squat slowed recovery of bar speed and jump height by about 24–48 hours compared with the same work stopped short of failure (Morán-Navarro et al., 2017). Effort affects how soon a lift can be trained again.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In interviews, 11 elite raw powerlifters from New Zealand reported that **deadlifting takes longer to recover from than the other lifts** (Pritchard et al., 2016). This is what the lifters said, not a measurement, but it matches common coaching experience.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Typical recommendations (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith; Sheiko):

| Lift | Typical frequency (intermediate) | Hard/heavy sessions | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bench press** | 2–4 per week (5–6 in high-frequency programs) | 1–2 | Smaller muscles, less whole-body fatigue, fast recovery; precise technique benefits from practice |
| **Squat** | 2–3 per week | 1–2 | Large muscles, more fatigue and soreness; recovers in about 2–3 days from moderate sessions |
| **Deadlift** | 1–2 per week (often 1 competition + 1 variation) | 1 | Heaviest loads, heavy load on the back and hips, overlaps with squat muscles; slowest to recover |

**Heavy and light days.** <Evidence level="practice" /> A lift trained 3+ times a week should not be trained hard every time. Give each session a role: one heavy (top sets at RPE 8–9), one moderate volume day (RPE 7–8), and one light or technique day (RPE 6–7, or a variation).

**Squat and deadlift share recovery.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Leave about 48–72 hours between the heaviest squat and the heaviest deadlift session, and don't follow a heavy deadlift day with a heavy squat day.

:::tip[In plain words]
Each lift needs a different amount of rest. The bench press bounces back fastest, so you can train it often. The squat needs a couple of days. Heavy deadlifts take the longest, so most lifters pull heavy once a week and do a lighter variation on another day.
:::

## Frequency vs. volume per session

<Evidence level="practice" /> Use the lift's weekly set target (from [Chapter 8](/lets-git-it/building-programs/08-volume-landmarks/)) to choose its frequency. A practical rule: **keep hard sets of one lift to about 3–6 per session (up to about 8 for bench press)** (7 is acceptable in a planned overreach week — see [Chapter 8](/lets-git-it/building-programs/08-volume-landmarks/)). When a lift needs more, add a session instead of more sets.

| Weekly hard sets for one lift | Suggested sessions per week | Sets per session |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 6 | 1–2 | 3–6 |
| 7–12 | 2–3 | 3–6 |
| 13–18 | 3–4 | 4–6 |
| 19 or more | 4–6 | 3–6 |

**When to raise a lift's frequency**

- It needs more weekly sets and sessions are already long.
- It is the lifter's weak lift or is being re-learned after a technique change.
- Quality falls off in the later sets of the session.

**When to lower it**

- The lifter can't recover between sessions (performance at a known load drops from one session to the next).
- The schedule forces fewer gym days; keep weekly volume close by making each remaining session slightly longer.
- During a deload or maintenance phase.

:::tip[In plain words]
If one lift needs lots of sets, spread them over more days instead of cramming them into one long session. As a rough rule, about three to six hard sets of a lift per session is plenty.
:::

## Sample programs

Default lifter: intermediate man, ~83 kg, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. Loads are approximate RPE equivalents from [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg. Weekly hard sets are similar in all three (squat ~11, bench press 14–15, deadlift ~7, counting close variations) so you can see how frequency changes the sessions, not the total work.

### Program 1: Bench 2×, squat 2×, deadlift 1× (3 days)

For a lifter with only three gym days.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 5 × 5 | RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) |
| Mon | Bench press | 7 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| Mon | Barbell row | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| Wed | Deadlift | 5 × 4 | RPE 7–7.5 (~170 kg) |
| Wed | Deficit deadlift (5 cm) | 2 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| Wed | Overhead press, pull-ups | 3 × 8 each | RPE 8 |
| Fri | Squat | 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~155 kg) |
| Fri | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| Fri | Bench press | 7 × 3 | RPE 8 (~102.5 kg) |
| Fri | Back extension | 3 × 12 | RPE 8 |

**Weekly sets:** squat 11, bench 14, deadlift 7. **Max sets of one lift in a session:** 7 (bench).

**Why it is built this way:** deadlift sits alone on Wednesday, two days from each squat day. Bench needs 7 sets per session to reach 14 per week, which is at the top of the useful range; watch for slowing bar speed in sets 6–7. If bench quality drops, this lifter needs a fourth day or fewer weekly bench sets.

### Program 2: Bench 3×, squat 3×, deadlift 2× (4 days)

The most common intermediate setup.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 5 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 4 × 3 | RPE 7.5 (~177.5 kg) |
| 2 | Bench press | 5 × 3 | RPE 8 (~102.5 kg) |
| 2 | Barbell row | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Pause squat (2 s) | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Close-grip bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Squat | 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~155 kg) |
| 4 | Deficit deadlift (5 cm) | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Back extension, triceps work | 3 × 12 each | RPE 8 |

**Weekly sets:** squat 11, bench 14, deadlift 7. **Max sets of one lift in a session:** 5.

**Why it is built this way:** each lift gets one heavier day (squat day 4, bench day 2, deadlift day 2) and one or two lighter or variation days. In a Mon–Tue–Thu–Sat schedule, the heavy squat (Saturday) and the heavy deadlift (Tuesday) are three to four days apart. Bench sessions are 4–5 sets, so every set stays crisp.

### Program 3: Bench 5×, squat 3×, deadlift 2× (5 days, short sessions)

For lifters with time to train most days, a weak bench press, or high bench volume needs. Schedule: Mon, Tue, Thu, Fri, Sat.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (Mon) | Squat | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~145 kg) |
| 1 (Mon) | Bench press | 3 × 5 | RPE 7.5 (~95 kg) |
| 2 (Tue) | Deadlift | 4 × 3 | RPE 7.5 (~177.5 kg) |
| 2 (Tue) | Bench press | 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~102.5 kg) |
| 3 (Thu) | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| 3 (Thu) | Bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) |
| 4 (Fri) | Bench press | 3 × 2 | RPE 8 (~107.5 kg) |
| 4 (Fri) | Deficit deadlift (5 cm) | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| 5 (Sat) | Squat | 4 × 2 | RPE 8 (~160 kg) |
| 5 (Sat) | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |

**Weekly sets:** squat 11, bench 15, deadlift 7. **Max sets of one lift in a session:** 4.

**Why it is built this way:** bench appears every day, but two sessions (Tuesday triples, Friday doubles) are heavier; the rest are moderate or variations. Sessions take roughly 45–60 minutes instead of 90+. The heavy deadlift (Tuesday) and heavy squat (Saturday) are four days apart. The trade-off is time: five gym days a week. Do not add sets just because there are more days; the weekly total is the same as Programs 1 and 2.

**Comparison**

| | Program 1 | Program 2 | Program 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Days per week | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| Frequency (S / B / D) | 2 / 2 / 1 (+ variation same day) | 3 / 3 / 2 | 3 / 5 / 2 |
| Weekly sets (S / B / D) | 11 / 14 / 7 | 11 / 14 / 7 | 11 / 15 / 7 |
| Max sets of one lift per session | 7 | 5 | 4 |
| Best for | Busy lifters, fewer days | Most intermediates | More time, weak bench, high volume needs |

## Common mistakes

- **Adding frequency and keeping the old sets per session.** That raises total volume, sometimes past MRV. Redistribute the existing sets first.
- **Training every session of a lift hard.** With 3+ sessions, make some light or use variations.
- **Heavy deadlifts the day after heavy squats.** The same muscles are still recovering.
- **Cramming a lift's whole weekly volume into one session.** The last sets become low-quality practice.
- **Believing high frequency is magic.** Equal-volume studies show similar strength gains; the benefits are practical, not automatic.
- **Copying a top lifter's six-day plan without their recovery and schedule.** Choose frequency to fit the lifter's life.

## Key takeaways

- With weekly volume equal, frequency has only a small, diminishing effect on strength gains in trained lifters; it is mainly a tool for distributing volume, and total volume matters more.
- Frequency's real job is to spread volume so each set is fresher and each session gives more skill practice.
- Typical intermediate frequency: bench 2–4×, squat 2–3×, deadlift 1–2× per week, set by each lift's recovery (SRA).
- Keep about 3–6 hard sets of a lift per session (up to ~8 for bench); add sessions, not sets, when more volume is needed.
- With 3+ sessions per lift, give each session a role: heavy, moderate or light.
- Research on competitive powerlifters is limited; adjust frequency to the individual's response and schedule.
