## In one minute

Linear progression ends when a lifter can no longer recover from one session and come back stronger for the next, even with good sleep, food and two well-run resets. This is expected: the stronger and more trained a lifter is, the bigger the stimulus needed to improve and the longer it takes to recover from it, so gains slow. The fix is to change the **unit of progress** from the session to the week, by giving the week a hard day, an easy day and sometimes a medium day. Four simple ways to do this are weekly heavy–light progression, a Texas-method-style volume–light–intensity week, simple undulation of reps across the week, and RPE-based top sets with back-off sets. Change one thing at a time, starting with how often the lifter tries to set a best, before adding volume or complexity.

## Signs linear progression has ended

**A stall is not the same as the end of the novice phase.** Most early stalls have a fixable cause. Rule those out before changing the program.

| Looks like the end of LP, but is probably… | Check | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Poor recovery** | Sleep under ~7 h, missed meals, losing weight, high stress | Fix recovery, then repeat the weight |
| **Jumps too big** | Still adding 5 kg to squat or 2.5 kg to bench late in LP | Smaller jumps (see [Part 1 · Chapter 7](/lets-git-it/foundations/07-novice-linear-progression/)) |
| **Started too heavy** | Stalled within the first few weeks | Reset and run LP again from a lighter start |
| **Technique breakdown** | Video shows a new fault at the stall weight | Fix technique at lower loads, then rebuild |
| **One bad day** | A single miss after a run of good sessions | Repeat the weight next session |

<Evidence level="practice" /> If none of these explain it, the novice phase is over for that lift when (Rippetoe & Baker):

- The lift has been **reset twice** and stalls again near the same weight.
- Stalls arrive every one to two weeks despite good recovery.
- The lifter can make the weight on a fresh day but not when it is the third heavy session that week.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Lifts end their novice phase at different times. Bench press usually ends first because it is the lightest lift and the jumps are relatively large. Move **only the stalled lift** to intermediate programming and keep the others on LP while they still progress.

:::tip[In plain words]
Missing a few reps doesn't mean you've outgrown your beginner program. First check your sleep, food and technique, and make the jumps smaller. If you've reset twice and still get stuck at the same weight, then you're ready for an intermediate program, and you can move one lift at a time.
:::

## Why progress slows

**Each gain makes the next one harder.** This is the principle of diminishing returns.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Trained lifters need a different dose.** A meta-analysis of 140 studies found maximal strength gains at a mean intensity of about 60% 1RM in untrained people but about 80% in trained people, with untrained people doing best training each muscle 3 days per week and trained people 2 days (Rhea et al., 2003). A second meta-analysis, in competitive athletes, found the largest gains at about 85% 1RM, 2 days per week and about 8 sets per muscle group (Peterson et al., 2004).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Trained lifters gain less from the same program.** Over 21 weeks of identical training, 8 untrained men increased maximal force by 20.9% and muscle size by 5.6%, while 8 strength athletes gained only 3.9% and −1.8% (Ahtiainen et al., 2003).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Gains slow over months and years.** In 14 young men on the same bench press program, the rate of strength and muscle gain slowed after the first 6 weeks (Ogasawara et al., 2013). In training records of 14,690 adults on a minimal program, strength had nearly levelled off after 1–2 years (Steele et al., 2023). In competitive powerlifters, the strongest men gained about half as fast as the least strong (Latella et al., 2020).
- <Evidence level="strong" /> **More volume helps, but less and less.** Across 67 studies, strength rose with weekly sets and with frequency, but with diminishing returns that were more pronounced for strength than for muscle size (Pelland et al., 2025).

**What this means mechanically** <Evidence level="practice" />: a novice's session is a big enough stress to drive adaptation, and small enough to recover from in 48–72 hours. As the lifter gets stronger, a session big enough to cause adaptation takes longer to recover from than the gap to the next session (the stimulus–recovery–adaptation idea in [Part 1 · Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/foundations/02-stress-recovery-adaptation/)). The solution is to spread the stress across a week: one or two hard sessions to drive adaptation, easier sessions to practise and recover (Rippetoe & Baker; Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith).

:::tip[In plain words]
At the start, one workout is enough to make you stronger by the next. As you get stronger, you need a bigger push to improve, and a bigger push needs more time to recover from. So instead of beating your last workout every time, you aim to beat last week.
:::

## What changes at the intermediate stage

<Evidence level="practice" /> Three things change (Rippetoe & Baker):

| | Novice (LP) | Intermediate |
|---|---|---|
| **Unit of progress** | Each session | Each week |
| **Session stress** | Every session similar and hard | Hard, light and medium sessions in the same week |
| **Variables changed** | Load only | Load, plus reps, sets and exercise variations |

**Does varying the week help?** <Evidence level="moderate" /> The evidence suggests it matters more for trained lifters. A meta-analysis of volume-matched studies found undulating periodization (varying loads and reps daily or weekly) produced larger 1RM gains than linear periodization in trained participants, but no difference in untrained participants (Moesgaard et al., 2022). An earlier meta-analysis of 17 studies found no overall difference between linear and undulating models, and suggested novelty and variety may matter for further strength development (Harries et al., 2015).

**Does training more often help?** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 28 resistance-trained men, training 6 days per week gave the same strength gains as 3 days per week when weekly volume and intensity were matched (Colquhoun et al., 2018). Adding days is a way to spread volume, not a fix in itself (see [Part 1 · Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/foundations/04-training-variables/)).

:::tip[In plain words]
As an intermediate, you stop treating every workout the same. Some days are hard, some are easy, and some are in between. That mix lets you push hard enough to improve while still recovering.
:::

## Four ways to make the transition

All four keep the competition lifts as the core of training. They differ in how the week is organized and how load is chosen.

### Option 1: Weekly heavy–light progression

**Keep the novice schedule, but only one session per lift per week is heavy.** The other sessions use the same lift at about 80–90% of the heavy day's load, or a lighter variation. The heavy day's load goes up each week.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> Rippetoe & Baker present several intermediate models built this way, with a heavy, light and medium day across the week.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> The simplest change from LP: the lifter keeps the same exercises and days.

:::tip[In plain words]
You keep training the same days, but only one day per lift is hard. The other days are lighter practice. You add weight to the hard day once a week.
:::

### Option 2: Texas-method style (volume, light, intensity)

**Monday is high volume at moderate load, Wednesday is light, Friday is a low-volume, heavy attempt at a new best.** Monday's volume drives adaptation; Friday shows the result.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> This is the best-known intermediate model in Rippetoe & Baker. When 5-rep bests on the intensity day stall, they rotate the intensity day through triples, doubles and singles, and adjust Monday's volume.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> It suits lifters who respond well to heavy work and like a clear weekly target. It is demanding: Friday is close to maximal every week, so it often stalls after a few months.

:::tip[In plain words]
Monday is your hard-work day with lots of sets, Wednesday is easy, and Friday you try to beat your best. The hard work on Monday is what makes you stronger; Friday is where you see it.
:::

### Option 3: Simple undulation (different reps on different days)

**Each training day uses a different rep range (for example 3s, 5s and 8s), and each day progresses on its own, usually weekly.** This is daily undulating periodization (DUP) in its simplest form.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 20 men training 3 days per week for 12 weeks, changing the rep target daily (8RM, 6RM and 4RM) increased bench press and leg press strength more than changing it every 4 weeks, with total volume and intensity matched (Rhea et al., 2002).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 18 college-aged male powerlifters training 3 days per week for 6 weeks, the order of the days mattered: hypertrophy, then power, then strength days gave a greater bench press increase than hypertrophy, strength, then power days, with no significant difference for squat or deadlift (Zourdos et al., 2016).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Put the lightest, highest-rep day the session after the heaviest one, or after a rest day, so the heavy day is done fresh.

:::tip[In plain words]
Each day of the week uses a different number of reps: heavy sets of 3 one day, medium sets of 5 another, lighter sets of 8 another. Each one gets a little heavier every week. The variety gives you different kinds of practice and helps you recover.
:::

### Option 4: RPE-based progression (top set plus back-off sets)

**The lifter works up to a top set at a target RPE, then does back-off sets at a set percentage below it.** Load rises when the lifter is stronger, falls on bad days, and the target RPE rises across a few weeks.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In trained men following the same 8-week daily undulating program, choosing loads by RPE and by percentage of 1RM both increased squat and bench press strength, with small, non-significant differences favouring RPE (Helms et al., 2018).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 14 studies found that all autoregulation methods tested, including RPE targets and flexible daily undulation, increased 1RM strength (Larsen et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 23 college football players over 6 weeks, an autoregulated progression that adjusted load to daily and weekly performance improved bench press and estimated squat 1RM more than a fixed linear progression (Mann et al., 2010).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> RPE-based programming needs a lifter who rates effort honestly and accurately. Teach the scale during LP and check ratings against video (see [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/)). Top sets and back-off sets come from the Reactive Training Systems approach (Tuchscherer).

:::tip[In plain words]
Instead of a fixed weight, you work up to a set that feels like a certain effort, for example "5 reps with 2 left in the tank". Then you do a few lighter sets based on that. On good days the weights go up, on bad days they come down, and over the weeks you aim a bit harder.
:::

### Choosing an option

| Option | Best for | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Weekly heavy–light** | Lifters who just left LP; busy schedules | Smallest change; easy to run | Slows quickly if the heavy day is too hard |
| **Texas-method style** | Lifters who like heavy work and clear targets; strong recovery | Clear weekly best; simple | Near-maximal every Friday; stalls after a few months |
| **Simple undulation** | Lifters who need more volume or muscle; enjoy variety | Builds volume and muscle; good evidence in trained lifters | More numbers to track; easy to make every day hard |
| **RPE-based** | Lifters who rate RPE well; shift workers; variable lives | Adjusts to daily readiness | Depends on honest ratings; can drift without a plan |

<Evidence level="practice" /> No option is clearly best. Choose what fits the lifter's assessment (see [Part 2 · Chapter 1](/lets-git-it/building-programs/01-assessing-the-lifter/)) and what they will follow consistently.

## What to change first

**Change one thing at a time and give it 3–4 weeks.** If several things change at once, the coach cannot tell what worked.

<Evidence level="practice" /> A sensible order:

1. **Fix recovery** (sleep, food, stress) and **shrink the jumps**. This is still part of LP.
2. **Change the unit of progress** to weekly: one heavy session per lift per week, others lighter. Keep exercises and total volume about the same.
3. **Add variation in rep ranges** across the week (heavy, light, medium days).
4. **Add a little volume**: 1–2 extra hard sets per lift per week, or a close variation (pause squat, close-grip bench). Weekly volume helps with diminishing returns (Pelland et al., 2025), so add gradually.
5. **Introduce RPE** for top sets once the lifter rates reliably.
6. **Move to block-based planning** (Part 2, Chapters 3–6) once weekly progress also stalls.

**Progress expectations.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Early intermediates often add about 2.5 kg per week to squat and deadlift and about 1–1.25 kg per week to bench press for a few months. Expect slower progress the longer the lifter trains.

**Track estimated maxes.** <Evidence level="practice" /> With rep ranges changing, compare estimated 1RMs (from the RPE chart) week to week, not just the weight on the bar.

:::tip[In plain words]
Don't change everything at once. First make sure you're sleeping and eating well. Then switch to setting one best per week. Only after that add different rep ranges, a few more sets, or RPE. Give each change a few weeks before you judge it.
:::

## Sample programs

**Lifter:** male, about 80 kg, just finished six months of LP. Squat 3 × 5 stalled twice at 135 kg, bench press 3 × 5 at 85 kg, deadlift 1 × 5 at 165 kg. Taking the final set of 5 as about RPE 9.5 (85% on the chart in [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/)), estimated 1RMs are about **squat 157.5 kg, bench 100 kg, deadlift 192.5 kg**. Warm-ups and most accessories are not shown. Loads show a lifter who progresses well; real lifters will stall sooner or later, and each program says what to do then.

### Program 1: Texas-method style, 3 days (8 weeks)

**Template**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon (volume) | Squat | 5 × 5 | About 90% of Friday's target |
| Mon (volume) | Bench press | 5 × 5 | About 90% of Friday's target |
| Mon (volume) | Barbell row | 3 × 8 | RPE 8 |
| Wed (light) | Squat | 2 × 5 | About 80% of Monday's load |
| Wed (light) | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| Wed (light) | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 6 | RPE 6–7 |
| Fri (intensity) | Squat | 1 × 5 (later 1 × 3) | New best |
| Fri (intensity) | Bench press | 1 × 5 (later 1 × 3) | New best |
| Fri (intensity) | Deadlift | 1 × 5 | New best |

**Week by week**

| Week | Squat Mon / Wed / Fri | Bench Mon / Fri | Deadlift Fri | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 122.5 / 97.5 / 137.5 × 5 | 77.5 / 86.25 × 5 | 167.5 × 5 | |
| 2 | 125 / 100 / 140 × 5 | 78.75 / 87.5 × 5 | 170 × 5 | |
| 3 | 127.5 / 102.5 / 142.5 × 5 | 80 / 88.75 × 5 | 172.5 × 5 | |
| 4 | 130 / 105 / 145 × 4 (missed) | 81.25 / 90 × 5 | 175 × 5 | Squat 5RM stalls |
| 5 | 130 / 105 / 147.5 × 3 | 82.5 / 91.25 × 5 | 177.5 × 5 | Squat Friday switches to triples |
| 6 | 132.5 / 105 / 145 × 5 (made) | 83.75 / 92.5 × 4 (missed) | 180 × 5 | Bench 5RM stalls |
| 7 | 132.5 / 105 / 150 × 3 | 83.75 / 95 × 3 | 182.5 × 5 | Bench Friday switches to triples |
| 8 | 135 / 107.5 / 147.5 × 5 | 83.75 / 92.5 × 5 (made) | 185 × 5 | Alternate 5s and 3s from now on |

**Why:** Monday's 25 reps at a moderate load drive adaptation, Wednesday keeps the squat pattern practised while the legs recover, and Friday shows the result. When a 5-rep best stalls, the Friday target alternates between 5s and 3s instead of resetting, as Rippetoe & Baker suggest. If Monday's 5 × 5 becomes the problem (sets 4 and 5 missed), drop it to 4 × 5 for a week. When both rep targets stall for 2–3 weeks, the lifter moves to block programming.

### Program 2: Heavy–light–medium with simple undulation, 3 days (two 4-week waves)

**Template**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (heavy) | Squat | 5 × 3 | See table |
| 1 (heavy) | Bench press | 5 × 3 | See table |
| 1 (heavy) | Chin-up | 3 × 6–10 | RPE 8 |
| 2 (light squat, deadlift) | Squat | 3 × 8 | See table |
| 2 (light squat, deadlift) | Deadlift | 4 × 3 | See table |
| 2 (light squat, deadlift) | Bench press | 4 × 8 | See table |
| 3 (medium) | Squat | 4 × 5 | See table |
| 3 (medium) | Bench press | 4 × 5 | See table |
| 3 (medium) | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |

**Week by week (kg)**

| Week | Squat heavy / light / medium | Bench heavy / light / medium | Deadlift 4 × 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 125 / 102.5 / 115 | 80 / 65 / 72.5 | 155 |
| 2 | 127.5 / 105 / 117.5 | 81.25 / 66.25 / 73.75 | 157.5 |
| 3 | 130 / 107.5 / 120 | 82.5 / 67.5 / 75 | 160 |
| 4 | 132.5 / 110 / 122.5 | 83.75 / 68.75 / 76.25 | 162.5 |
| 5 (deload) | 2 × 3 @ 115 / — / 2 × 5 @ 105 | 2 × 3 @ 72.5 / — / 2 × 5 @ 65 | 2 × 3 @ 140 |
| 6 | 130 / 107.5 / 120 | 82.5 / 67.5 / 75 | 160 |
| 7 | 132.5 / 110 / 122.5 | 83.75 / 68.75 / 76.25 | 162.5 |
| 8 | 135 / 112.5 / 125 | 85 / 70 / 77.5 | 165 |
| 9 | 137.5 / 115 / 127.5 | 86.25 / 71.25 / 78.75 | 167.5 |
| 10 | Test: work up to a triple at RPE 8–9 on each lift and recalculate estimated maxes | | |

**Why:** each day trains a different rep range, so the week mixes heavy practice with volume for muscle growth, and the light day after the heavy day aids recovery. Loads start at about 80% (heavy), 65% (light) and 73% (medium) of estimated max and rise 2.5 kg per week (1.25 kg on bench). Each wave restarts a little above the previous one, so progress is weekly but with a built-in lighter week. If a day's sets go above about RPE 9, hold that day's load for a week instead of adding.

### Program 3: RPE-based top sets and back-offs, 4 days (7 weeks)

**Template**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | Top set, then 3 back-off sets | See table |
| 1 | Bench press | Top set, then 3 back-off sets | See table |
| 2 | Deadlift | Top set, then 2 back-off sets | See table |
| 2 | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Pause squat (2 s) | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Bench press | Top set, then 3 back-off sets | Same targets as day 1 |
| 4 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |

Back-off sets use the same reps as the top set, at **top set −8%** in sets of 4–5 and **−6%** in sets of 3 (−10% after the week 7 single).

**Week by week: top set target (expected kg) → back-off load**

| Week | Squat (day 1) | Bench press (days 1 and 4) | Deadlift (day 2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 @ RPE 7 (~125) → 3 × 5 @ 115 | 5 @ RPE 7 (~77.5) → 3 × 5 @ 72.5 | 4 @ RPE 7 (~155) → 2 × 4 @ 142.5 |
| 2 | 5 @ RPE 7.5 (~125) → 3 × 5 @ 115 | 5 @ RPE 7.5 (~80) → 3 × 5 @ 72.5 | 4 @ RPE 7.5 (~157.5) → 2 × 4 @ 145 |
| 3 | 5 @ RPE 8 (~127.5) → 3 × 5 @ 117.5 | 5 @ RPE 8 (~80) → 3 × 5 @ 72.5 | 4 @ RPE 8 (~160) → 2 × 4 @ 147.5 |
| 4 | 3 @ RPE 8 (~135) → 3 × 3 @ 127.5 | 3 @ RPE 8 (~87.5) → 3 × 3 @ 82.5 | 3 @ RPE 8 (~165) → 2 × 3 @ 155 |
| 5 | 3 @ RPE 8.5 (~137.5) → 3 × 3 @ 130 | 3 @ RPE 8.5 (~87.5) → 3 × 3 @ 82.5 | 3 @ RPE 8.5 (~170) → 2 × 3 @ 160 |
| 6 (deload) | 2 × 3 @ ~115, no top set | 2 × 3 @ ~72.5, no top set | 2 × 3 @ ~145, no top set |
| 7 | 1 @ RPE 8 (~145) → 3 × 3 @ top −10% (~130) | 1 @ RPE 8 (~92.5) → 3 × 3 @ top −10% (~82.5) | 1 @ RPE 8 (~177.5) → 2 × 3 @ top −10% (~160) |

The expected loads come from the starting estimated maxes. **If the lifter is getting stronger, the target RPE arrives at a heavier load, and that is the point.** In week 7, an RPE 8 single gives a new estimated max (single ÷ 0.922). For example, a 150 kg squat single at RPE 8 means about 162.5 kg, 5 kg up in 7 weeks.

**Rules:**

| If the top set… | Then |
|---|---|
| Hits the target RPE | Do all back-off sets |
| Overshoots by 1 RPE or more | Do only 2 back-off sets |
| Comes in 1 RPE or more easier than expected | Record the heavier load; it is a new estimated max |
| Overshoots two sessions in a row | Check recovery; consider an early deload |

**Why:** the top set sets the day's load to the lifter's readiness, so bad days don't become missed reps, and the rising RPE target and falling reps across weeks 1–5 add overload. Bench is trained three times, using two top-set days and one volume day, because it recovers fastest. This structure leads directly into the RPE-based programming in Part 3, Chapter 1: Autoregulation.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving LP after one stall.** Rule out sleep, food, technique and jump size, and reset twice first.
- **Staying on LP after it has ended.** Resetting again and again to the same weight wastes months.
- **Moving every lift at once.** Transition only the lifts that have stalled.
- **Keeping every session heavy.** An intermediate week needs easy and medium days, or recovery cannot keep up.
- **Jumping straight to a complicated advanced program.** Early intermediates still progress weekly; long blocks and peaks come later.
- **Changing several things at once.** Change one variable, then judge it after 3–4 weeks.
- **Using RPE before the lifter can rate it.** Check ratings against video and performance first.
- **Adding training days instead of fixing the week's structure.** More days only help if they spread the work sensibly.

## Key takeaways

- LP has ended for a lift when it stalls again after two resets despite good recovery; lifts can end at different times.
- Progress slows because trained lifters need a bigger stimulus and more recovery time; research shows smaller gains in trained lifters and diminishing returns from extra volume.
- The intermediate fix is to progress weekly, with hard, light and medium sessions in the same week.
- Four simple options: weekly heavy–light, Texas-method style, simple undulation, and RPE-based top sets with back-offs. None is clearly best.
- Change the unit of progress first, then rep variation, volume and RPE, one change at a time.
