## In one minute

Block periodization divides training into short, focused blocks of a few weeks, each concentrating on a small number of qualities instead of training everything at once. The classic sequence, with each block building on the one before, is **accumulation** (higher volume, moderate loads, building work capacity and muscle), then **transmutation** (heavier, more specific strength work), then **realization** (low volume, very heavy, specific work to peak). Periodized training in general beats non-periodized training for strength. Whether block periodization beats other periodized models is still unclear, because few studies compare them directly.

## The idea behind block periodization

Traditional periodization trains many qualities at the same time and shifts the balance slowly across a long season. Block periodization was developed partly because experienced athletes need a large, concentrated stimulus to keep improving. Spreading training across many qualities at once can leave each one with too little stimulus. Block periodization instead **concentrates** work on a few qualities per block and **sequences** the blocks so the gains from one carry into the next (Issurin, 2010).

Two ideas make this work:

- **Residual training effects.** <Evidence level="practice" /> A quality trained in one block is not lost the moment training shifts focus; it declines gradually. Issurin (2010) proposed approximate durations, for example around a month for maximal strength and only days for maximal speed. These estimates come from coaching theory and have not been directly tested in powerlifters. Treat them as rough guides.
- **Phase potentiation.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Each block prepares the lifter for the next. Muscle and work capacity built in accumulation give the next block more to work with, and strength built in transmutation is what realization expresses on the platform (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith).

## The three blocks

| Block | Main goal | Typical intensity | Typical reps per set | Volume | Typical length |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Accumulation** | Work capacity, muscle size, technique practice | ~65–80% 1RM (RPE 6–8) | 6–10 | High | 3–6 weeks |
| **Transmutation** | Converting muscle and capacity into specific strength | ~78–90% 1RM (RPE 7–9) | 3–5 | Moderate | 3–5 weeks |
| **Realization** | Peaking: expressing strength at near-maximal loads | ~85–100% 1RM (RPE 8–10) | 1–3 | Low | 1–3 weeks |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Ranges vary between coaches and lifters; these are typical starting points, not rules. Exercise selection also narrows from block to block: accumulation uses more variations and accessories, while realization is almost entirely the competition lifts.

## How it compares with other models

- **Periodized beats non-periodized.** <Evidence level="strong" /> Two meta-analyses found larger 1RM gains with periodized training than with non-periodized training. The effect was moderate in one (Williams et al., 2017) and smaller but still present when total volume was equated (Moesgaard et al., 2022).
- **Block vs. daily undulating.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 10 weeks of training with 31 university track and field athletes, block and daily undulating periodization (DUP) produced statistically similar strength gains. Trends favoured the block group, and the block group got its results from less total work, which the authors described as more efficient (Painter et al., 2012). This is one study in non-powerlifters, so it cannot settle the question.
- **Linear vs. undulating.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> The research is mixed. One meta-analysis found no difference in strength between linear and undulating periodization (Harries et al., 2015). A later meta-analysis of volume-equated studies found a small advantage for undulating, mostly in trained participants (Moesgaard et al., 2022). The later review found too few studies comparing other models, including block periodization, to analyse them at all (Moesgaard et al., 2022).

**What this means for coaches:** the evidence strongly supports planned variation over doing the same thing all the time. It does not yet show that one periodization model is clearly best. Choose a model that fits the lifter's level, schedule, and competition calendar, and that you can run consistently.

## Block periodization and the Sheiko approach

The books in this manual's library show two different philosophies:

- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Sequential blocks** (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith): blocks with distinct goals run in order (a hypertrophy-focused phase, then strength, then a peak), and each prepares the next.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Concurrent, high-frequency training** (Sheiko): the competition lifts are trained often throughout the year, mostly at moderate intensities, with volume and intensity varied from session to session and week to week. Qualities are developed together, not one block at a time.

Both approaches have produced elite lifters. In practice many coaches blend them: they keep frequent practice of the competition lifts in every block, as Sheiko does, while shifting the emphasis of volume and intensity from block to block.

## Sample program

A 12-week example for an intermediate lifter with a squat of 180 kg, a bench press of 120 kg and a deadlift of 210 kg, training four days a week. It is an illustration of the structure, not a prescription. Adjust it using the RPE targets; the percentages use the same RPE chart as Part 1 · Chapter 5.

**Overview**

| Weeks | Block | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1–5 | Accumulation | Volume, muscle, technique; week 5 is lighter to shed fatigue |
| 6–9 | Transmutation | Heavier sets of 3–5 on the competition lifts |
| 10–12 | Realization | Heavy singles and doubles, volume cut; week 12 is the test or meet |

**Sample week: accumulation (week 2)**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 5 × 6 | RPE 7 (~76%, 137.5 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 5 × 8 | RPE 7 (~71%, 85 kg) |
| 1 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 10 | RPE 7 |
| 2 | Deadlift | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~76%, 160 kg) |
| 2 | Close-grip bench press | 4 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| 2 | Leg press | 3 × 12 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Pause squat | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~76%, 92.5 kg) |
| 3 | Barbell row | 4 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 4 | Bench press | 4 × 8 | RPE 7 (~71%, 85 kg) |
| 4 | Deficit deadlift | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Back extension | 3 × 12 | RPE 8 |

**Sample week: transmutation (week 7)**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 5 × 4 | RPE 7.5 (~82%, 147.5 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 5 × 4 | RPE 7.5 (~82%, 100 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 4 × 3 | RPE 7.5 (~85%, 177.5 kg) |
| 2 | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Squat | 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~86%, 155 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press | 4 × 3 | RPE 8 (~86%, 102.5 kg) |
| 4 | Pause bench press | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |

**Sample week: realization (week 11)**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 1 × 1, then 2 × 2 | RPE 8.5 (~94%, 170 kg), then ~87% (157.5 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 1 × 1, then 2 × 2 | RPE 8.5 (~94%, 112.5 kg), then ~87% (105 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 1 × 1, then 1 × 2 | RPE 8.5 (~94%, 197.5 kg), then ~86% (180 kg) |
| 3 | Squat | 2 × 2 | RPE 7 (~86%, 155 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press | 3 × 2 | RPE 7 (~86%, 102.5 kg) |

Week 12 is the test or competition. Peaking and attempt selection are covered in Part 4.

## Common mistakes

- **Dropping the competition lifts during accumulation.** Strength skill fades without practice; keep squat, bench and deadlift in every block, even if lighter.
- **Making blocks too long.** Residual effects fade, so qualities from an early block can be lost before the peak.
- **Cutting volume too early or too much.** Realization should reduce fatigue, not undo the work; keep enough heavy practice to stay sharp.
- **Copying percentages without adjusting.** Use RPE to fit the plan to the lifter's actual day.
- **Expecting block periodization to be "the best" model.** Its advantage over other periodized approaches is not established; consistency matters more.

## Key takeaways

- Block periodization concentrates training on a few qualities per block and sequences blocks so each builds on the last.
- The classic order is accumulation → transmutation → realization.
- Periodized training clearly beats non-periodized training for strength.
- Few studies compare block periodization directly with other models; current evidence does not show a clear winner.
- Sequential blocks (Israetel et al.) and Sheiko's concurrent, high-frequency approach are both proven in practice and are often blended.
