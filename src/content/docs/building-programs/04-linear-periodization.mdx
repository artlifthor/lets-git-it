## In one minute

**Linear periodization** moves through a training cycle in steps: early phases use more sets and reps at lighter loads, and each later phase uses fewer reps at heavier loads, ending in a peak for a meet or test. It is simple to write, easy to follow, and lines up naturally with a fixed competition date. Both linear and undulating periodization increase strength well. Meta-analyses disagree on whether undulating models have a small edge, and where an edge appears it is mainly in trained lifters. The main weaknesses of the classic version are long stretches without heavy practice and gains from early phases that may fade by the end, both of which can be fixed in the design.

## What linear periodization is

In the classic Western version, often called **strength–power periodization**, training passes through phases in a fixed order. Intensity rises in steps and volume falls as the cycle nears its peak (Hartmann et al., 2015):

| Phase | Typical length | Reps per set | Intensity | Volume | Goal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hypertrophy / base** | 3–6 weeks | 6–10 | ~65–75% 1RM (RPE 6–8) | High | Muscle, work capacity, technique practice |
| **Strength** | 3–5 weeks | 3–5 | ~77–87% 1RM (RPE 7–9) | Moderate | Heavier specific strength |
| **Peak** | 2–4 weeks | 1–3 | ~87–95% 1RM (RPE 8–9) | Low | Express strength with heavy singles and doubles |
| **Taper / meet** | 1 week | 1–2 | Openers, then rest | Very low | Arrive fresh |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Ranges are typical starting points, not rules (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith). "Linear" describes the **direction** of change across the cycle, not a straight line: most real programs move in steps, with deload weeks between phases.

### Not the same as linear progression

**Linear progression** ([Part 1 · Chapter 7](/lets-git-it/foundations/07-novice-linear-progression/)) means adding load **every session** while sets and reps stay the same. It is a novice method. **Linear periodization** changes reps, sets and intensity **from phase to phase** over many weeks, for lifters who can no longer set a best every session. The shared word "linear" causes much confusion.

:::tip[In plain words]
Linear periodization is a step-by-step plan. You start with more sets and reps at lighter weights, then each few weeks you do fewer reps with heavier weights, until you peak for a meet. It is different from the beginner plan of adding weight every workout.
:::

## Why it works: the rationale

- **Phase potentiation.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Muscle and work capacity built in the base phase give the strength phase more to work with. The strength phase builds what the peak phase expresses (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith). The idea is logical, but it has not been tested directly in powerlifters.
- **Specificity rises toward the meet.** <Evidence level="practice" /> The closer the meet, the more training looks like competition: heavy singles and doubles on the three lifts. This follows the specificity principle from [Part 1 · Chapter 3](/lets-git-it/foundations/03-core-training-principles/).
- **Fatigue falls toward the meet.** <Evidence level="practice" /> As volume drops, fatigue clears and the lifter's strength can show on the day.
- **Simplicity.** <Evidence level="practice" /> One emphasis at a time is easy to write, easy to understand and easy to track. Many lifters stick with simple plans better.

:::tip[In plain words]
The early weeks build muscle and the ability to handle work. The middle weeks turn that into strength. The last weeks practise heavy lifts while you rest up, so you are strongest on meet day.
:::

## Strengths and limits

| Strengths | Limits |
|---|---|
| Simple to write and follow | Heavy loads are rare in the early phases, so heavy-lift skill can rust |
| Fits one fixed meet date well | Muscle and work capacity from early phases may fade by the peak |
| Volume and fatigue fall naturally into the meet | Fits several meets a year poorly; each needs a full cycle |
| Easy to track progress within a phase | Each phase can feel monotonous |
| Works well with percentages for lifters who rate RPE poorly | Fixed percentages ignore good and bad days unless capped with RPE |

<Evidence level="practice" /> These limits match the criticisms Issurin (2010) made of traditional periodization: long, mixed phases can give too little stimulus to each quality and handle multiple peaks badly. They are design problems, and most can be reduced:

- **Keep heavy exposure in every phase.** One heavier single or triple per lift every week or two keeps the skill sharp even in a base phase.
- **Keep some volume late.** Do not cut all moderate-rep work in the peak; one or two back-off sets keep muscle and work capacity.
- **Keep phases short.** Three to five weeks each, so early-phase gains are still present at the peak.
- **Cap percentages with RPE.** "4 × 3 at 85%, but stop if a set goes above RPE 9" protects bad days.

:::tip[In plain words]
The weak spot of a step-by-step plan is that you go weeks without lifting heavy, and the muscle you built early may fade by the end. Fix it by doing a few heavy lifts every week or two, keeping some lighter volume near the end, and keeping each phase short.
:::

## What the evidence says

### Meta-analyses: linear vs. undulating

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **No difference in one analysis.** A meta-analysis of 17 studies (510 participants) found no difference between linear and undulating periodization for upper- or lower-body strength. Almost all studies found both approaches increased strength. The authors flagged short study lengths and participants' training history as confounders, and suggested that novelty or variety may be important for further strength gains (Harries et al., 2015).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **A small edge for undulating in another.** A later meta-analysis of volume-equated studies found larger 1RM gains with undulating periodization (effect size 0.31). In the subgroup analysis this advantage appeared in **trained** participants (effect size 0.61, with a wide confidence interval reaching zero) but **not untrained** ones (Moesgaard et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of periodized vs. non-periodized training also found that undulating programs were associated with larger strength gains as a moderator (Williams et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Muscle growth is similar.** A meta-analysis of 13 studies found essentially no difference between linear and daily undulating periodization for hypertrophy (Grgic et al., 2017).

### Individual studies

| Study | Who | Length | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rhea et al. (2002) | 20 men | 12 weeks | DUP gave larger strength gains than linear (phases changed every 4 weeks) |
| Buford et al. (2007) | 28 recreationally trained men and women | 9 weeks | No differences between linear, daily undulating and weekly undulating |
| Prestes et al. (2009) | 40 men, ≥1 year of training | 12 weeks | Both improved; DUP had larger percentage gains, but the group difference was not statistically significant |
| Kok et al. (2009) | 20 untrained women | 9 weeks | Linear and undulating equally effective |
| Apel et al. (2011) | 42 recreationally active men | 12 weeks | **Linear** gave larger strength gains (10RM tests) than weekly undulating by week 12; the authors linked this to more soreness and fatigue in the undulating group |
| Miranda et al. (2011) | 20 trained men | 12 weeks | No significant difference; effect sizes favoured DUP |
| Simão et al. (2012) | 30 untrained men | 12 weeks | Undulating (every two weeks, then daily) gave higher bench press and curl 1RM |

<Evidence level="emerging" /> **Rigid linear vs. autoregulated progression:** in 23 college football players over 6 weeks, a program that adjusted load to each day's performance improved bench press and estimated squat 1RM more than a fixed linear program (Mann et al., 2010). This is one short study in a non-powerlifting population, but it supports adding RPE or rep-based adjustments to linear plans.

### How to read this honestly

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Both models work.** In almost every study, both groups got stronger.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **The results are mixed.** Some studies favour undulating, at least one favours linear, and many find no difference. A possible small edge for undulating in trained lifters is the best current estimate, not a settled fact.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Study "linear" programs are often very rigid.** In several studies the linear group used a single rep range for four weeks at a time on one or two exercises. Real powerlifting programs built on a linear structure usually include variations, heavy singles and back-off work, so the studies may not reflect how coaches actually use the model.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Almost no studies used competitive powerlifters over a full meet cycle.** Treat the findings as direction, not as a verdict on your lifter.

:::tip[In plain words]
Both step-by-step plans and plans that change every session make people stronger. Some studies favour one, some the other, and many find no difference. If there is an advantage for changing things more often, it's small and mostly in experienced lifters.
:::

## How to build a linear cycle

<Evidence level="practice" /> A step-by-step method (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith; Rippetoe & Baker):

1. **Fix the end date.** Count back from the meet or test day. Ten to sixteen weeks suits most intermediates.
2. **Set the phases.** For 12 weeks, for example: base 4 weeks → strength 3–4 weeks → peak 3 weeks → meet week. Put a deload between phases if the lifter needs it.
3. **Choose starting loads.** Use a recent 1RM or an estimated max from an RPE 8–9 set ([Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/)). If the max is uncertain, use 95% of it as the training max.
4. **Step intensity up by about 2.5–5% per week** within a phase, and drop reps as each new phase begins.
5. **Let volume fall gradually.** Hold the number of sets fairly steady while reps per set drop; total reps fall naturally.
6. **Keep the competition lifts every week.** Variations and accessories are heaviest in the base phase and are trimmed toward the peak.
7. **Cap with RPE.** If a prescribed set goes above RPE 9, stop there and use that load next week instead of the next planned step.
8. **Review at each phase change.** If loads felt easy (RPE 6 where 8 was expected), raise the training max by 2.5–5 kg; if they felt too hard, lower it.

:::tip[In plain words]
Pick the meet date and count back. Split the weeks into building, strength and peak phases. Go up a little in weight each week, do fewer reps each phase, and keep squatting, benching and deadlifting every week. If a set feels much harder than planned, don't force the next jump.
:::

## Sample programs

Loads are rounded to 2.5 kg; with smaller plates (0.5–1.25 kg), use the exact percentage, which matters most for bench press and for lighter lifters. Warm-ups are not shown. RPE caps apply to every main set: if a set goes above RPE 9, stop there.

### Program 1: 12-week linear cycle to a meet (4 days per week)

For the intermediate lifter in this manual: squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg, male, about 83 kg bodyweight.

**Main lifts by week (Day 1 squat and bench, Day 2 deadlift)**

| Week | Phase | Squat | Bench press | Deadlift |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Base | 4 × 8 @ 67.5% (122.5 kg) | 5 × 8 @ 67.5% (80 kg) | 3 × 8 @ 65% (137.5 kg) |
| 2 | Base | 4 × 8 @ 70% (125 kg) | 5 × 8 @ 70% (85 kg) | 3 × 8 @ 67.5% (142.5 kg) |
| 3 | Base | 4 × 6 @ 72.5% (130 kg) | 5 × 6 @ 72.5% (87.5 kg) | 3 × 6 @ 70% (147.5 kg) |
| 4 | Base | 4 × 6 @ 75% (135 kg) | 5 × 6 @ 75% (90 kg) | 3 × 6 @ 72.5% (152.5 kg) |
| 5 | Strength | 4 × 5 @ 77.5% (140 kg) | 5 × 5 @ 77.5% (92.5 kg) | 3 × 5 @ 77.5% (162.5 kg) |
| 6 | Strength | 4 × 4 @ 80% (145 kg) | 5 × 4 @ 80% (95 kg) | 3 × 4 @ 80% (167.5 kg) |
| 7 | Strength | 4 × 3 @ 85% (152.5 kg) | 5 × 3 @ 85% (102.5 kg) | 3 × 3 @ 82.5% (172.5 kg) |
| 8 | Deload | 3 × 3 @ 75% (135 kg) | 3 × 3 @ 75% (90 kg) | 2 × 3 @ 72.5% (152.5 kg) |
| 9 | Peak | 4 × 2 @ 87.5% (157.5 kg) | 5 × 2 @ 87.5% (105 kg) | 3 × 2 @ 87.5% (185 kg) |
| 10 | Peak | 1 × 1 @ 92.5% (167.5 kg), 3 × 2 @ 87.5% (157.5 kg) | 1 × 1 @ 92.5% (110 kg), 3 × 2 @ 87.5% (105 kg) | 1 × 1 @ 90% (190 kg), 2 × 2 @ 85% (177.5 kg) |
| 11 | Peak | 1 × 1 @ 95% (170 kg), 2 × 2 @ 85% (152.5 kg) | 1 × 1 @ 95% (115 kg), 2 × 2 @ 85% (102.5 kg) | 1 × 1 @ 92.5% (195 kg), early in the week |
| 12 | Meet | Openers at about 90% early in the week, then light work | Same | No heavy deadlifts this week |

**Sample week: strength phase (week 6)**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 4 | 80% (145 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 5 × 4 | 80% (95 kg) |
| 1 | Barbell row | 4 × 8 | RPE 8 |
| 2 | Deadlift | 3 × 4 | 80% (167.5 kg) |
| 2 | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 2 | Back extension | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Bench press | 3 × 4 | 75% (90 kg) |
| 3 | Lat pulldown | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 4 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Larsen press (feet up) | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Split squat | 3 × 8 per leg | RPE 7 |

**How it is built and why:**

- The four main-lift sets stay steady while reps drop from 8 to 2, so **total volume falls** while **intensity rises**: the defining shape of linear periodization.
- A **heavier single appears in weeks 10–11**, so the lifter has handled near-meet loads before the platform. Peaking details are in Part 4, Chapter 1: The science of peaking and tapering.
- **Variations and accessories (days 3–4) are kept** through the strength phase and trimmed in the peak, which reduces the "early gains fade" problem.
- The **week 8 deload** separates the strength and peak phases, so the lifter starts heavy work fresh.

### Program 2: 12-week linear cycle, 3 days per week

For a female lifter in the 63 kg class: squat 120 kg, bench press 65 kg, deadlift 150 kg. Trains Monday, Wednesday and Friday.

**Main lifts by week (Monday squat and bench, Wednesday deadlift)**

| Week | Phase | Squat | Bench press | Deadlift |

| 2 | Base | 4 × 8 @ 70% (85 kg) | 4 × 8 @ 70% (45 kg) | 3 × 6 @ 72.5% (110 kg) |
| 3 | Base | 4 × 6 @ 75% (90 kg) | 4 × 6 @ 75% (50 kg) | 3 × 5 @ 77.5% (117.5 kg) |
| 4 | Deload | 3 × 5 @ 65% (77.5 kg) | 3 × 5 @ 65% (42.5 kg) | 2 × 5 @ 65% (97.5 kg) |
| 5 | Strength | 4 × 5 @ 77.5% (92.5 kg) | 4 × 5 @ 77.5% (50 kg) | 3 × 4 @ 80% (120 kg) |
| 6 | Strength | 4 × 4 @ 80% (95 kg) | 4 × 4 @ 80% (52.5 kg) | 3 × 3 @ 82.5% (125 kg) |
| 7 | Strength | 4 × 3 @ 85% (102.5 kg) | 4 × 3 @ 85% (55 kg) | 3 × 3 @ 85% (127.5 kg) |
| 8 | Deload | 3 × 3 @ 75% (90 kg) | 3 × 3 @ 75% (50 kg) | 2 × 3 @ 72.5% (110 kg) |
| 9 | Peak | 3 × 2 @ 87.5% (105 kg) | 4 × 2 @ 87.5% (57.5 kg) | 2 × 2 @ 87.5% (132.5 kg) |
| 10 | Peak | 1 × 1 @ 92.5% (110 kg), 2 × 2 @ 85% (102.5 kg) | 1 × 1 @ 92.5% (60 kg), 2 × 2 @ 85% (55 kg) | 1 × 1 @ 92.5% (140 kg) |
| 11 | Peak | 1 × 1 @ 95% (115 kg), 2 × 2 @ 80% (95 kg) | 1 × 1 @ 95% (62.5 kg), 2 × 2 @ 80% (52.5 kg) | 1 × 1 @ 90% (135 kg), Monday only |
| 12 | Meet | Openers early in the week, then rest | Same | — |

**The rest of each week:**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat, bench press | As in the table | — |
| Mon | Dumbbell row | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| Wed | Deadlift | As in the table | — |
| Wed | Bench press (second session) | Same sets × reps as Monday | 7.5% lighter than Monday |
| Wed | Back extension | 3 × 10 | RPE 7 |
| Fri | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × Monday's reps (max 5) | About 85% of Monday's squat load |
| Fri | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 6 (base), 3 × 4 (strength), skip in peak | RPE 7 |
| Fri | Lat pulldown | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |

**How it is built and why:** with only three days, each lift gets **one main session and one lighter session** per week. Phases are three weeks with a **deload after each**, which suits a lifter whose recovery is limited by work, study or family. <Evidence level="practice" /> The bench press is trained twice because, in common coaching practice, it tolerates frequent training well (see [Chapter 9: Training frequency for each lift](/lets-git-it/building-programs/09-training-frequency/)). **Microplates help here:** a 2.5 kg rounding is almost 4% of a 65 kg bench, so use 1.25 kg steps where available.

### Program 3: Linear cycle for a busy lifter (2 sessions per week, about 60 minutes)

For the 180/120/210 kg lifter in a heavy work season. Eight weeks to a test day. Each session uses a **top set** and **back-off sets** so the work fits in an hour. An optional third session (bench press and rows, 30 minutes) can be added on a good week.

| Week | Session A: squat | Session A: bench press | Session B: deadlift |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 × 6 @ 75% (135 kg), 2 × 6 @ 70% (125 kg) | 1 × 6 @ 75% (90 kg), 2 × 6 @ 70% (85 kg) | 1 × 5 @ 75% (157.5 kg), 1 × 5 @ 70% (147.5 kg) |
| 2 | 1 × 6 @ 77.5% (140 kg), 2 × 6 @ 72.5% (130 kg) | 1 × 6 @ 77.5% (92.5 kg), 2 × 6 @ 72.5% (87.5 kg) | 1 × 5 @ 77.5% (162.5 kg), 1 × 5 @ 72.5% (152.5 kg) |
| 3 | 1 × 5 @ 80% (145 kg), 2 × 5 @ 75% (135 kg) | 1 × 5 @ 80% (95 kg), 2 × 5 @ 75% (90 kg) | 1 × 4 @ 80% (167.5 kg), 1 × 4 @ 75% (157.5 kg) |
| 4 | Deload: 2 × 5 @ 65% (117.5 kg) | Deload: 2 × 5 @ 65% (77.5 kg) | Deload: 1 × 5 @ 65% (137.5 kg) |
| 5 | 1 × 4 @ 82.5% (147.5 kg), 2 × 4 @ 77.5% (140 kg) | 1 × 4 @ 82.5% (100 kg), 2 × 4 @ 77.5% (92.5 kg) | 1 × 3 @ 85% (177.5 kg), 1 × 3 @ 80% (167.5 kg) |
| 6 | 1 × 3 @ 87.5% (157.5 kg), 2 × 3 @ 80% (145 kg) | 1 × 3 @ 87.5% (105 kg), 2 × 3 @ 80% (95 kg) | 1 × 2 @ 87.5% (185 kg), 1 × 3 @ 80% (167.5 kg) |
| 7 | 1 × 1 @ 92.5% (167.5 kg), 2 × 2 @ 85% (152.5 kg) | 1 × 1 @ 92.5% (110 kg), 2 × 2 @ 85% (102.5 kg) | 1 × 1 @ 92.5% (195 kg) |
| 8 | Test: work up to a single at RPE 9 | Test: single at RPE 9 | Test: single at RPE 9 (separate day) |

**Each session in full:**

| Session | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Squat | As in the table | — |
| A | Bench press | As in the table | — |
| A | Chin-up or lat pulldown | 3 × 8 | RPE 8 |
| B | Deadlift | As in the table | — |
| B | Bench press | 3 × the week's reps | The week's back-off load |
| B | Pause squat (2 s), weeks 1–6 only | 2 × 4 | RPE 6–7 |

**How it is built and why:** two sessions cannot hold much volume, so the plan keeps **only what matters most**: the three lifts, heavy enough to drive strength, with a back-off set or two for practice. The steady rise in intensity makes progress easy to see when life is hectic. **If a week is missed, repeat the previous week rather than skipping ahead.** If the lifter's schedule later frees up, move to Program 1.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing linear periodization with novice linear progression.** One changes load every session; the other changes the training emphasis every few weeks.
- **Making phases too long.** Six or more weeks of only sets of 8 leaves heavy-lift skill rusty and lets early gains fade.
- **Dropping all heavy work in the base phase.** Keep an occasional heavier single or triple.
- **Following percentages through a bad week.** Use RPE caps; repeat a week when needed.
- **Cutting volume too early.** Keeping one or two back-off sets in the peak preserves work capacity.
- **Using it for several meets a year.** One linear cycle per meet leaves little time to build; block structures handle frequent meets better.
- **Overstating the research.** Linear periodization is not "outdated", and undulating is not proven superior. Both work.

## Key takeaways

- Linear periodization steps from higher volume and lighter loads to lower volume and heavier loads across a cycle, ending in a peak.
- It is simple, matches a single meet date well, and reduces fatigue into the meet.
- Its main weaknesses, rare heavy practice early and fading early-phase gains, can be reduced with short phases, regular heavy singles and some volume late.
- Meta-analyses are mixed: one finds no difference from undulating models; another finds a small edge for undulating, mainly in trained lifters. Both models clearly work.
- Cap percentages with RPE, and repeat weeks rather than forcing progress.
