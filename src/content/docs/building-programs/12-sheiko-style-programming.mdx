## In one minute

Sheiko-style programming, named after Russian coach Boris Sheiko, trains the **competition lifts often**, mostly at **submaximal loads** of about 70–85% of 1RM, for **many sets of 1–5 reps** that stop well short of failure. Volume is planned by counting **lifts** (reps at 50% and above) and spreading them across **intensity zones**, days, and weeks, so heavy, medium, and light sessions alternate. Research supports the main ingredients: strength rises with weekly volume and frequency, and sets do not need to go near failure to build strength. The approach suits lifters who need lots of technical practice and recover well, but fixed percentages need an effort check, and lifters who need more muscle may need extra hypertrophy work.

## Where it comes from

Boris Sheiko set out his approach in *Powerlifting: Foundations and Methods*. It grows out of Soviet sports science: plan the training load carefully, practise the sport's skill often, and develop qualities **together** rather than one block at a time. In the terms of [Chapter 7](/lets-git-it/building-programs/07-conjugate-concurrent/) it is a **concurrent** system, and in the terms of [Chapter 6](/lets-git-it/building-programs/06-block-periodization/) it is the main alternative to sequential blocks.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Everything in the next section describes the style as it is generally understood from Sheiko's book and the coaches who use it. The sample programs in this chapter are original examples written in that style. They are not Sheiko's own programs.

## The five features of the style

### 1. High frequency of the competition lifts

<Evidence level="practice" /> Each competition lift is trained several times a week, often with two lifts in one session and sometimes the same lift twice (for example squat, then bench, then squat again).

| Lift | Typical sessions per week | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Bench press | 3–4 | Highest frequency and most lifts; recovers fastest |
| Squat | 2–3 | One session often lighter or a variation |
| Deadlift | 2 | Often one full deadlift and one partial variation (to the knees, from blocks) |

**Evidence.**

- <Evidence level="strong" /> In a meta-analysis of 22 studies, strength gains grew with training frequency, but **when weekly volume was equal, frequency made no significant difference** (Grgic et al., 2018). A larger, later analysis of 67 studies found strength still rises with frequency, with diminishing returns (Pelland et al., 2025).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> Two 6-week studies in trained men compared 3 and 6 sessions per week with matched volume. One (28 men, testing squat, bench and deadlift) and the other (27 men) both found **similar strength gains** at either frequency (Colquhoun et al., 2018; Saric et al., 2019).

**What this means:** high frequency is not magic. Its value is that it lets a lifter **fit more total practice and volume** into the week while keeping each session's fatigue manageable.

:::tip[In plain words]
In Sheiko-style training you squat, bench and deadlift several times a week, bench most of all. Training more often doesn't make you stronger by itself; it lets you do more good-quality work without any single session wrecking you.
:::

### 2. Submaximal intensity

<Evidence level="practice" /> Most work sets fall between about **70% and 85%** of 1RM. Loads of 90% and above appear rarely, mainly before a competition, and true max testing is usually left for the meet itself. Counting every lift from 50% upwards, including the ramp-up sets, the average intensity of a week typically ends up in the high 60s.

**Evidence.**

- <Evidence level="strong" /> Training to failure gave **no significant extra strength** compared with stopping short; when volume was not matched, non-failure training gave slightly better strength gains (Grgic et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Across many studies, estimated reps in reserve had **little effect on strength gains**, while muscle growth improved as sets ended closer to failure (Robinson et al., 2024).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 22 young men, short squat sets that stopped after a small drop in bar speed produced similar strength gains to longer sets with 40% fewer reps; the longer sets produced more muscle growth (Pareja-Blanco et al., 2017).

**What this means:** submaximal sets are an efficient way to build strength. The cost is a smaller hypertrophy stimulus, which matters for lifters who need to gain muscle (see Common mistakes).

:::tip[In plain words]
Most of the work is with weights you could lift several more times. Research shows you don't need to grind to get stronger. You may need some harder accessory work if you also want to build muscle.
:::

### 3. Many sets of low reps

<Evidence level="practice" /> Instead of 3 × 8, a Sheiko-style session might use 5 × 3 or 6 × 2 at the same or higher intensity. Each rep is fresher, so technique stays consistent. Sessions usually **ramp up** (for example 50% × 5, 60% × 4, 70% × 3) before the main sets, and sometimes **come back down** for a final set or two at a lighter load. The ramp sets count as practice, not just warm-up.

:::tip[In plain words]
You do lots of short sets, like 5 sets of 3, so every rep looks the same and stays sharp. The lighter sets at the start count too: they're extra practice.
:::

### 4. Competition-lift practice and few variations

<Evidence level="practice" /> Most of the volume is the **competition squat, bench press and deadlift** with competition technique. Variations are close to the competition lift: pause squats, pause or close-grip bench, deadlifts to the knees, from blocks, or from a deficit. Accessories are fewer and lighter than in bodybuilding-style programs. The idea is the specificity principle ([Part 1 · Chapter 3](/lets-git-it/foundations/03-core-training-principles/)) taken seriously: the skill being tested is practised all year, not only in a peaking block.

:::tip[In plain words]
Most of your time goes into the real competition lifts, done exactly the way you'll do them at a meet. The variations you use stay very close to those lifts.
:::

### 5. Planned volume distribution

<Evidence level="practice" /> This is the heart of the method, and what is most often missed when people copy a template. The coach plans **how many lifts** are done, **at which intensities**, and **on which days and weeks**:

- **Number of lifts (NL):** count every rep at 50% of 1RM or more, per lift, per session, per week and per month.
- **Average intensity:** the mean percentage of all those lifts. Rising average intensity with similar NL means harder training.
- **Within the week:** sessions differ in load: one or two bigger days, one medium, one light.
- **Within the month:** weeks differ too. A typical pattern is medium → large → medium (but heavier) → small.

:::tip[In plain words]
Sheiko-style coaches count every rep above half your max and plan where those reps go: which weights, which days, which weeks. Some days and weeks are big, others small, so you never do the same hard week over and over.
:::

## Intensity zones

<Evidence level="practice" /> Sheiko-style planning sorts lifts into intensity zones. The shares below are a typical **off-season** pattern for an intermediate lifter, worked out from the sample programs in this chapter; they are a guide, not fixed rules, and shift towards heavier zones before a meet.

| Zone (% 1RM) | Main role | Typical share of weekly lifts |
|---|---|---|
| 50–59% | Ramp-up, technique, blood flow | ~15–20% |
| 60–69% | Technique under light load, volume | ~15–25% |
| **70–79%** | **Main developmental work** | **~45–55%** |
| 80–89% | Heavier strength work | ~10–15% |
| 90%+ | Competition preparation, rare heavy singles | 0–5% (higher before a meet) |

**Why the 70–85% band carries the load:** it is heavy enough to build strength and keep competition technique, yet light enough to repeat for many sets several times a week. Remember that reps available at a given percentage **vary a lot between people** (Nuzzo et al., 2024). For one lifter 80% × 3 is easy; for another it is close to hard. Use percentages as the plan and check them against effort.

:::tip[In plain words]
About half of all your reps sit between 70% and 80% of your max. That's heavy enough to get stronger and light enough to do often. The rest are lighter practice or the occasional heavier set.
:::

## Making percentages work for your lifter

1. **Use a realistic max.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Base percentages on a recent competition or a well-performed heavy single, not a best-ever lift from a good day long ago. If unsure, use about 95% of the best recent max.
2. **Add an effort cap.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Most main sets in this style should feel like RPE 6–8. If 75% × 3 regularly rates RPE 9, the max is too high or recovery is poor: reduce loads by 2.5–5%.
3. **Keep the zones, adjust the loads.** <Evidence level="practice" /> When reducing loads, keep the planned sets and reps so the volume distribution stays intact.
4. **Progress monthly, not weekly.** <Evidence level="practice" /> If the heaviest week moved well, raise the working max a little for the next month (for example +2.5 kg bench, +5 kg squat and deadlift).

:::tip[In plain words]
Percentages only work if the max behind them is real. Check how hard the sets feel: if your "easy" sets feel heavy, lower the max a little and keep going.
:::

## Who it suits

- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Good fit:** intermediate lifters with sound technique who can train 3–4 times a week for about 1.5–2 hours, recover well, and like structure; lifters whose technique breaks down under fatigue.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Less ideal:** lifters with little training time; lifters who need to gain a lot of muscle; lifters who get bored with the same lifts; novices who still progress on simple linear programs.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Ready-made templates:** many numbered Sheiko-style programs circulate online. They were written for particular lifters and situations, often stronger and more experienced ones. Check their weekly lifts against what your lifter is used to before running one.

## Sample programs

Intermediate lifter: squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. Loads are percentages of the competition 1RM, rounded to 2.5 kg. Variations (pause squat, deadlift to knees, block pulls) are also loaded as a percentage of the competition lift. **Sets × reps** and **Load** columns list each step of the ramp in order. Rest 2–3 minutes between work sets, 3–4 minutes for sets at 80% and above. Main sets should feel like RPE 6–8.

### Program 1: Sheiko-style 3-day week

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 1×5, 1×4, 2×3, 4×3 | 50%, 60%, 70%, 75% (90 / 107.5 / 125 / 135 kg) |
| Mon | Bench press | 1×5, 1×4, 2×3, 5×3 | 50%, 60%, 70%, 75% (60 / 72.5 / 85 / 90 kg) |
| Mon | Pause squat (2 s) | 1×3, 4×3 | 55%, 65% (100 / 117.5 kg) |
| Mon | Chest-supported row | 4 × 8 | RPE 8 |
| Wed | Deadlift | 1×3, 1×3, 2×3, 4×2 | 50%, 60%, 70%, 75% (105 / 125 / 147.5 / 157.5 kg) |
| Wed | Bench press | 1×5, 1×4, 5×4 | 50%, 60%, 70% (60 / 72.5 / 85 kg) |
| Wed | Deadlift to knees | 1×3, 4×3 | 55%, 65% (115 / 137.5 kg) |
| Wed | Triceps extension | 4 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| Fri | Squat | 1×5, 1×4, 1×3, 1×2, 3×2, 2×4 | 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, 85%, 75% (90 / 107.5 / 125 / 145 / 152.5 / 135 kg) |
| Fri | Bench press | 1×5, 1×4, 1×3, 4×3, 2×5 | 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, 70% (60 / 72.5 / 85 / 95 / 85 kg) |
| Fri | Good morning | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 |
| Fri | Lat pulldown | 4 × 8 | RPE 8 |

**Weekly totals (lifts at 50%+):**

| Lift | Lifts | Average intensity | Heaviest work |
|---|---|---|---|
| Squat (incl. pause squat) | 70 | ~68% | 85% × 2 |
| Bench press | 93 | ~68% | 80% × 3 |
| Deadlift (incl. to knees) | 35 | ~66% | 75% × 2 |
| **Total** | **198** | **~67%** | |

**Why it's built this way:** bench is trained three times and gets the most lifts. Squat has one medium day (Monday) and one heavier day (Friday) with a back-down set. Deadlift is trained once in full and once as a partial pull, because heavy pulls are the hardest to recover from. About half of all lifts land at 70–79%.

### Program 2: Sheiko-style 4-day week

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 1×5, 1×4, 2×3, 4×2 | 50%, 60%, 70%, 80% (90 / 107.5 / 125 / 145 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 1×5, 1×4, 2×3, 4×4 | 50%, 60%, 70%, 75% (60 / 72.5 / 85 / 90 kg) |
| 1 | Squat (second time) | 2 × 4 | 70% (125 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 1×3, 1×3, 1×3, 4×3 | 50%, 60%, 70%, 75% (105 / 125 / 147.5 / 157.5 kg) |
| 2 | Pause bench press (2 s) | 1×5, 1×4, 4×4 | 50%, 60%, 70% (60 / 72.5 / 85 kg) |
| 2 | Block pull (bar just below knee) | 1×3, 3×3 | 70%, 80% (147.5 / 167.5 kg) |
| 2 | Back extension | 3 × 10 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Bench press | 1×5, 1×4, 1×3, 2×2, 3×2, 2×5 | 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, 85%, 75% (60 / 72.5 / 85 / 95 / 102.5 / 90 kg) |
| 3 | Pause squat (2 s) | 1×3, 4×3 | 50%, 65% (90 / 117.5 kg) |
| 3 | Dips | 4 × 8 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Barbell row | 4 × 8 | RPE 8 |
| 4 | Squat | 1×5, 1×4, 5×4 | 50%, 60%, 70% (90 / 107.5 / 125 kg) |
| 4 | Bench press | 1×6, 1×5, 4×5 | 50%, 60%, 70% (60 / 72.5 / 85 kg) |
| 4 | Good morning | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 |

**Weekly totals (lifts at 50%+):**

| Lift | Lifts | Average intensity | Heaviest work |
|---|---|---|---|
| Squat (incl. pause squat) | 75 | ~66% | 80% × 2 |
| Bench press (incl. pause bench) | 119 | ~67% | 85% × 2 |
| Deadlift (incl. block pulls) | 33 | ~72% | 80% × 3 (block pull) |
| **Total** | **227** | **~67%** | |

**Why it's built this way:** four days spread more lifts with shorter sessions than Program 1. Day 1 uses the classic "squat, bench, squat" pattern: the second squat block is extra practice once the lifter is warm. Day 3 is the heavy bench day; day 4 is a lighter, higher-rep day for squat and bench. Deadlift volume stays low but has the highest average intensity, because a few heavy pulls go further than many light ones.

### Program 3: A 4-week overview

This month uses Program 2's structure, changing the number of lifts and the top loads week by week.

| Week | Load | Squat / bench / deadlift lifts | Total lifts | Heaviest work | Average intensity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Medium | 65 / 100 / 28 | ~193 | Squat 80% × 2 (145 kg); bench 80% × 3 (95 kg); deadlift 75% × 3 (157.5 kg) | ~66% |
| 2 | Large | 75 / 119 / 33 | ~227 | As Program 2 | ~67% |
| 3 | Medium, heavier | 66 / 104 / 30 | ~200 | Squat 2 singles at 90% (162.5 kg); bench 2 singles at 90% (107.5 kg); deadlift 2 doubles at 85% (177.5 kg) | ~70% |
| 4 | Small | 42 / 68 / 20 | ~130 | Nothing above 80%; sets cut by about 40% | ~64% |

**How to use it:**

- **Weeks 1–2** build volume. Week 2 is the biggest of the month.
- **Week 3** cuts lifts slightly but adds a few heavier singles and doubles. This is the check: if 90% singles move fast at RPE 7–8, the lifter is on track.
- **Week 4** is the recovery week. Volume drops so the fatigue from weeks 1–3 can clear.
- **Next month:** if week 3 went well, raise the working max (for example squat 180 → 185 kg, bench 120 → 122.5 kg, deadlift 210 → 215 kg) and repeat with similar lifts. If week 3 felt heavy, keep the maxes and repeat.

The monthly total is about 750 lifts, split roughly 26% / 30% / 27% / 17% across the four weeks. Before a meet, the same style shifts more lifts into the 80–90% zone and adds a few singles at 90–95%, then tapers (Part 4).

## Common mistakes

- **Using an inflated max.** Every percentage is wrong if the max is wrong. Use a recent, honest number.
- **Ignoring how the sets feel.** Percentages are a plan; if 75% feels like RPE 9, adjust.
- **Running an advanced template too early.** Online templates written for strong, experienced lifters can carry far more lifts than an intermediate is used to. Compare weekly lifts first.
- **Adding failure sets or extra heavy work.** The style works because fatigue stays low. Adding grinding top sets changes the whole program.
- **Making every day and week equally hard.** The planned big, medium and small loads are the point of the method.
- **Neglecting muscle growth.** Submaximal sets build strength efficiently but give a smaller hypertrophy stimulus. A lifter who needs more muscle should add harder accessory work or a hypertrophy-focused phase.

## Key takeaways

- Sheiko-style training uses high frequency, submaximal loads (mostly 70–85%), and many sets of low reps on the competition lifts.
- Volume is planned by counting lifts at 50%+ and distributing them across intensity zones, days and weeks.
- Frequency helps mainly by letting a lifter do more total quality volume; sets don't need to approach failure for strength.
- Base percentages on a realistic max and check them against RPE.
- It suits technically sound lifters with time to train 3–4 days a week; lifters who need muscle may need extra hypertrophy work.
