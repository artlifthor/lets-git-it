## In one minute

Accessories are the exercises that are not the competition lifts or close variations of them: rows, leg presses, triceps work, back extensions and so on. Each one should have a **job**: build a muscle that limits a lift, strengthen a weak point, balance the body, or keep joints tolerant of heavy training. Research shows accessories add little to strength in the short term but can build muscles that the big lifts train only partly. Program them after the main work, usually for 2–4 sets of 6–20 reps at RPE 7–9, progress them with double progression, and keep them for a full block before judging them. Most intermediate lifters need **2–4 accessories per session**, and fewer as a meet approaches.

## What accessories can and cannot do

Accessories sit at the bottom of the specificity ladder (see [Chapter 8: Exercise selection](/lets-git-it/foundations/08-exercise-selection/)). They are less like the competition lifts, so they transfer less directly, but they also cost less recovery and carry less technical risk.

**What the research shows:**

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 20 trained men over 8 weeks, adding single-joint arm exercises to a program of multi-joint exercises gave **no extra gain in arm strength or size** (de França et al., 2015). A similar study in 29 untrained men found the same (Gentil et al., 2013).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 36 active men with total work matched, a program of only single-joint exercises produced smaller gains in bench press and squat 1RM than a program of multi-joint exercises (Paoli et al., 2017). Accessories cannot replace the main lifts.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 10 untrained men, training one arm with biceps curls and the other with dumbbell rows for 8 weeks, the curl arm grew its elbow flexors about twice as much (about 11% vs 5%), while each arm got stronger mainly at the exercise it trained (Mannarino et al., 2021). Direct work grows a target muscle better than hoping a compound lift trains it enough.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 20 adults over 12 weeks, seated leg curls grew the hamstrings more than lying leg curls (Maeo et al., 2021). Accessory choice changes which muscles grow, and by how much.

**What this means.** <Evidence level="practice" /> In the short run, accessories will not add much to a squat, bench or deadlift by themselves. Their value is longer-term: building muscles that the competition lifts train only partly (hamstrings, lats, upper back, triceps, adductors), adding muscle-building volume without the fatigue of more heavy barbell sets, and keeping the lifter healthy enough to train consistently. Most of the studies above are small and short, so treat them as direction, not proof.

:::tip[In plain words]
Extra exercises won't make your big lifts jump in a few weeks. What they do is build the muscles your big lifts lean on, add training without wearing you out as much, and keep your joints happy. Over months and years, that adds up.
:::

## Choosing accessories by job

Every accessory should have a one-line reason written next to it. The reason is its **job**. There are four.

### 1. Weak point

**The job:** strengthen the muscle or position where a lift breaks down. A lifter whose bench stalls near lockout often needs stronger triceps; a lifter whose squat stalls out of the hole often needs stronger quads and adductors.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> First rule out technique. A bench press that fails at lockout because the bar drifts toward the feet is a bar-path problem, not a triceps problem. Film the lift before you choose the accessory (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Pair a **close variation** that loads the weak position (pin press, pause squat) with **one or two accessories** that grow the muscle behind it (triceps extensions, leg press). The variation trains the skill; the accessory builds the muscle.
- Weak-point diagnosis in detail is covered in Part 5, Chapter 7 (From sticking point to accessory) and Part 3, Chapter 5 (Advanced weak-point analysis).

:::tip[In plain words]
Watch where your lift slows down or falls apart. Make sure it isn't a technique problem first. Then pick an extra exercise that builds the muscle doing most of the work at that point.
:::

### 2. Muscle building

**The job:** add muscle to the groups that drive the lifts. This is the main job of accessories during hypertrophy phases (see [Chapter 10](/lets-git-it/building-programs/10-hypertrophy-for-powerlifters/)).

| Muscle | Why powerlifters need it | Accessory examples |
|---|---|---|
| Quads | Squat out of the hole, deadlift off the floor | Leg press, hack squat, split squat, leg extension |
| Adductors | Squat out of the hole, sumo deadlift | Wide-stance leg press, adductor machine, Copenhagen plank |
| Glutes and hamstrings | Hip extension in all three lifts, deadlift lockout | Romanian deadlift, seated leg curl, back extension, hip thrust |
| Spinal erectors | Holding position in the squat and deadlift | Back extension, good morning |
| Lats and upper back | Bar path in the bench, keeping the bar close in the deadlift, staying upright in the squat | Chest-supported row, lat pulldown, pull-up, one-arm row |
| Chest and front shoulders | Bench off the chest | Dumbbell bench press, incline press, dips |
| Triceps | Bench lockout | Overhead and lying triceps extensions, pushdowns, dips |

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 17 men, full-depth squats grew the adductors and glutes more than half squats (Kubo et al., 2019). Deep squatting already trains these muscles; extra direct work adds to it.

:::tip[In plain words]
Some muscles get plenty of work from squats, benches and deadlifts. Others, like your hamstrings, back and triceps, only get some. Extra exercises fill those gaps so every muscle that helps your lifts keeps growing.
:::

### 3. Balance

**The job:** reduce side-to-side differences and train movements the competition lifts leave out. Squat, bench and deadlift are all two-sided, mostly forward-and-back movements; they do little rotation, side bending or single-leg work.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 18 academy rugby players over 5 weeks, training only the rear-foot-elevated split squat improved back squat 1RM similarly to training the back squat (Speirs et al., 2016). Single-leg work can build two-legged strength, at least in athletes who are not specialist squatters.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Use single-leg and single-arm exercises (split squats, step-ups, one-arm rows and presses) and pulling to balance pressing. Expanding on this, Part 5, Chapter 6 (Planes of motion) covers side-to-side and rotational weaknesses and the accessories that address them.

:::tip[In plain words]
The competition lifts use both sides at once and mostly move forward and back. One-leg and one-arm exercises, and pulling to balance all the pressing, help even you out.
:::

### 4. Joint health and tissue tolerance

**The job:** keep tendons, joints and smaller muscles able to handle heavy training, so the lifter can keep training.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 25 trials in sports found that **strength training reduced sports injuries to less than one-third**, while stretching had no protective effect (Lauersen et al., 2014). These trials covered a range of sports, not powerlifting, so the size of the effect may not apply directly; but it supports building strength as a way to stay healthy.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Typical choices: rotator cuff and rear-shoulder work (external rotations, face pulls, reverse flyes) for lifters who bench often; Copenhagen planks or adductor work for lifters with wide squat or sumo stances; calf and tibialis work for lifters with sore shins or knees. These are low-fatigue, higher-rep exercises.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> If a lifter already has pain, an accessory is not a treatment. See a qualified health professional for pain that is persistent, severe or getting worse. Training around pain is covered in Part 5, Chapter 10 (Programming around injury).

:::tip[In plain words]
Getting stronger in general helps protect you from injury. A few light, higher-rep exercises for your shoulders, hips and knees help those joints cope with heavy training. They're for keeping you healthy, not for fixing an injury you already have.
:::

### A decision guide

| Question | If yes | If no |
|---|---|---|
| Does the lifter have a clear weak point that is not technique? | One variation + 1–2 accessories for that muscle | Go to the next question |
| Is the lifter in a hypertrophy or general phase? | 1–2 accessories per major muscle group across the week | Keep 1–2 accessories per session, mostly for joint health and balance |
| Is one side clearly weaker, or does pressing far outweigh pulling? | Add single-limb work and rows/pulldowns | Keep one balancing exercise per week |
| Is there a joint that gets irritated by heavy training? | One low-fatigue, higher-rep exercise for that area, 2–3× per week | Skip |
| Can you write a one-line reason for the exercise? | Keep it | Drop it |

## Sets, reps and RPE for accessories

Accessories can be pushed closer to failure than the competition lifts because each set costs less recovery and carries less technical risk.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Muscle growth increases as sets end closer to failure, while strength gains are similar across a wide range of reps in reserve (Robinson et al., 2024, an exploratory analysis). This favours pushing accessories (whose main job is muscle) harder than the competition lifts.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In a multi-part study of powerlifting athletes and coaches (interviews, surveys and two small training studies), lifters using a minimal-dose approach typically used **1–3 accessory exercises per competition lift**, at **RPE 7–9**, for about **6–10 reps** (Androulakis-Korakakis et al., 2021). That is a description of common practice, not proof that it is best.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Starting points by job:

| Job | Sets per exercise per session | Reps | Effort | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Weak point (muscle behind the weak position) | 3–4 | 6–12 | RPE 7–9 | 2–3 min |
| Muscle building | 2–4 | 8–15 (up to 20 for small muscles) | RPE 8–10 on single-joint, 8–9 on multi-joint | 1.5–2 min |
| Balance (single-limb, pulling) | 2–3 | 8–12 per side | RPE 7–8 | 1–2 min |
| Joint health | 1–3 | 12–25 | RPE 6–8 | 1 min, or superset |

**Counting accessory volume.** Accessory sets count toward the weekly volume for a **muscle**, not for a **lift**. A set of leg press adds to the quads' weekly total, but it is not a squat set. Track both: hard sets per lift (for strength and skill) and hard sets per muscle (for growth). See [Chapter 8: Volume landmarks](/lets-git-it/building-programs/08-volume-landmarks/).

:::tip[In plain words]
Extra exercises can be done harder than the big lifts, often with only one or two reps left, because they don't tire you out as much. Use medium to high reps, and rest just long enough to make the next set good.
:::

## Placement in the session and the week

### In the session

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of exercise order found that **strength increases most in the exercises done first** in a session, while muscle growth was similar whichever order was used (Nunes et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> So: **competition lift → close variation → multi-joint accessory → single-joint accessory.** Accessories go last because the lifts you most want to improve should be done fresh. Muscle growth from the accessory is not lost by doing it later.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Exceptions: light joint-health work (band pull-aparts, external rotations, a set of light pushdowns) can go in the warm-up, as long as it does not fatigue the muscles needed for the main lift. A lifter in a hypertrophy phase may occasionally put a priority accessory earlier on a day with no heavy competition-lift work.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Supersets** of accessories that use different muscles (a row with a leg curl; a triceps extension with a face pull) save time without hurting performance much. Don't superset anything with a heavy competition lift.

### In the week

<Evidence level="practice" /> Rules of thumb (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith):

1. **Don't tire tomorrow's main lift today.** Heavy Romanian deadlifts or back extensions the day before a heavy deadlift or squat session will hurt it. Put them on the deadlift day itself, or two days before.
2. **Spread each muscle's accessories over 2–3 days** rather than doing all of them in one session.
3. **Put triceps and front-shoulder work after bench sessions**, not before them, and leave at least one day before the next heavy bench.
4. **Grip-heavy work (rows, pulldowns, shrugs) goes after or away from deadlift sessions.**
5. **Lighter sessions are good homes for accessories.** A day with only a moderate bench session has room for 3–4 accessories.

:::tip[In plain words]
Do your main lifts first while you're fresh, then the extra exercises. Spread them across the week and avoid doing hard leg or back work the day before a heavy squat or deadlift day.
:::

## Progression

- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Double progression** is the default for accessories: pick a rep range (for example 3 × 10–12), add reps each week until all sets reach the top of the range at the target RPE, then add a small amount of load and start again at the bottom (see [Chapter 6: Progressive overload](/lets-git-it/foundations/06-progressive-overload/)).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Add sets across a block** during hypertrophy phases: for example 2 sets in week 1, 3 in weeks 2–3, 4 in week 4, then back down at the deload.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Change exercises on purpose, not at random.** Planned variation can help, while rotating exercises too often may hold back gains (Kassiano et al., 2022). Keep an accessory for at least one full block (4–6 weeks) so it can actually progress; change it when progress stalls, when it irritates a joint, or when the block's goal changes.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Judge it by its job.** After a block, ask: did the accessory progress (more reps or load)? Did the muscle grow or the weak point improve? Did the competition lift improve? If none of the three, replace it.

:::tip[In plain words]
Add a rep or two each week until you reach the top of your rep range, then add a little weight. Keep the same extra exercises for at least four to six weeks so they can actually improve, and swap them only for a reason.
:::

## How many accessories?

<Evidence level="practice" /> Typical numbers for an intermediate lifter (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith; see also the "How many exercises?" section of [Part 1 · Chapter 8](/lets-git-it/foundations/08-exercise-selection/)):

| Phase | Accessories per session | Total accessory sets per session | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypertrophy / general | 2–4 | 6–12 | 20–35 min |
| Strength | 2–3 | 4–9 | 15–25 min |
| Peaking (last 3–4 weeks) | 0–2 | 0–5 | 0–15 min |
| Deload | 1–2 | 2–4 | 10 min |

**A simple check.** If accessories make the lifter late for the next session, sore going into a heavy day, or too tired to hit their competition-lift RPE targets, there are too many. Cut the accessories before cutting the main work.

:::tip[In plain words]
Two to four extra exercises in a session is plenty for most lifters. Do more when you're building muscle and fewer as a competition gets close. If they're leaving you tired or sore for your main lifts, do fewer.
:::

## Sample programs

Intermediate lifter: squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. Each plan shows only the weak-point variation and accessories; they slot into a normal 4-day week of competition-lift training (for example the week in [Chapter 14](/lets-git-it/building-programs/14-building-a-mesocycle/)). Accessory loads are chosen in the session to hit the target RPE.

### Program 1: Weak bench press lockout

**Diagnosis.** Video shows the bar moving fast off the chest, slowing about two-thirds of the way up, with a straight bar path (so it is not a technique drift). The lifter's triceps are small relative to their chest and shoulders. Job: **weak point (triceps)** and **muscle building**.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load | Job |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Overhead cable triceps extension | 3 × 12–15 | RPE 9 | Triceps (long head), muscle |
| 2 | Close-grip bench press (variation, after the main lifts) | 3 × 6 | RPE 7–8 | Skill and strength near lockout |
| 2 | Chest-supported row | 3 × 10–12 | RPE 8–9 | Upper back, bar-path stability |
| 3 | Pin press from 5–8 cm below lockout | 3 × 3 | RPE 8 | Overload the weak range |
| 3 | Dumbbell lying triceps extension | 3 × 10–12 | RPE 8–9 | Triceps, muscle |
| 4 | Dips (add weight when easy) | 3 × 8–10 | RPE 8 | Triceps and chest, muscle |

**Progression:** double progression on the triceps exercises; add 2.5 kg to close-grip bench and pin press each week the target RPE is met. **Weekly direct triceps sets:** 9, plus the bench press and close-grip work. **Judge after 6 weeks:** has the close-grip bench moved up, and does the bench press slow down less near lockout on video?

### Program 2: Weak squat out of the hole

**Diagnosis.** The squat slows just above the bottom position; the lifter's hips shoot up first and the chest tips forward. Quads and adductors are the likely limiters. Job: **weak point (quads, adductors)** and **muscle building**.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load | Job |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Leg press (deep, feet mid-platform) | 3 × 10–12 | RPE 8–9 | Quads, muscle |
| 1 | Seated leg curl | 2 × 12 | RPE 9 | Hamstrings, balance |
| 2 | Bulgarian split squat | 3 × 8 each leg | RPE 8 | Quads and adductors, balance |
| 3 | Pause squat, 3 s at the bottom (variation) | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 | Strength and position in the hole |
| 3 | Leg extension | 3 × 12–15 | RPE 9 | Quads, muscle |
| 4 | Adductor machine or Copenhagen plank | 3 × 12–15 / 3 × 20–30 s per side | RPE 8 | Adductors, muscle and joint health |

**Progression:** pause squat +2.5–5 kg per week at the target RPE; double progression on the rest. **Weekly direct quad accessory sets:** 9, plus the squat and pause squat sets. **Watch:** leg press and split squats on day 1–2 are placed at least two days before the next heavy squat day. **Judge after 6 weeks:** is the pause squat stronger, and does video show the hips and chest rising together out of the hole?

### Program 3: Upper back and position

**Diagnosis.** The lifter's upper back rounds in the deadlift as the bar breaks the floor, the chest falls forward in heavy squats, and the shoulder blades drift during long bench sets. Job: **muscle building** (upper back, lats, spinal erectors) and **joint health** (rear shoulder, rotator cuff).

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load | Job |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Chest-supported row | 4 × 8–10 | RPE 8–9 | Upper back, muscle |
| 1 | Face pull | 2 × 15–20 | RPE 8 | Rear shoulder, joint health |
| 2 | Pull-up or lat pulldown | 3 × 8–10 | RPE 8–9 | Lats, muscle |
| 2 | Back extension (rounded upper back allowed, light) | 3 × 12–15 | RPE 7–8 | Spinal erectors, muscle |
| 3 | Front squat or safety-bar squat (variation) | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 | Upright squat position |
| 3 | One-arm dumbbell row | 3 × 10 each arm | RPE 8–9 | Lats and upper back, balance |
| 4 | Reverse dumbbell fly | 3 × 15 | RPE 9 | Rear shoulder and upper back |
| 4 | Cable external rotation (warm-up before bench) | 2 × 15–20 | RPE 6–7 | Rotator cuff, joint health |

**A note on "posture".** This plan aims at **positional strength under the bar**, not at fixing how the lifter stands day to day. Rounded shoulders at rest are not, on their own, a reason for alarm. **Progression:** double progression on all rows and flyes; add a fifth row set in week 3 if recovery allows. **Judge after 6 weeks:** do rows and pulldowns show steady progress, and does video show the upper back holding position better at the same RPE?

## Common mistakes

- **No job, no reason.** If you can't write a one-line reason, drop the exercise.
- **Too many accessories.** Six exercises after the main work leaves the lifter tired for the next heavy day without adding much.
- **Treating a technique problem with an accessory.** Fix the bar path first.
- **Changing accessories every week.** They never get a chance to progress, and you can't tell whether they worked.
- **Doing hard leg or back accessories the day before a heavy squat or deadlift.**
- **Keeping full accessory volume into a peak.** Cut them back in the last 3–4 weeks.
- **Using accessories to treat pain.** Persistent or worsening pain needs a qualified health professional.

## Key takeaways

- Every accessory needs a job: weak point, muscle building, balance, or joint health.
- In the short run accessories add little strength; their value is muscle, weak points and staying healthy over months.
- Do accessories after the main lifts; strength improves most in exercises done first.
- Most accessories: 2–4 sets of 6–20 reps at RPE 7–9; small single-joint exercises can go closer to failure.
- Progress with double progression and keep each accessory for a full block before judging it.
- Two to four accessories per session for most intermediates; fewer as a meet approaches.
