## In one minute

**Periodization** is planned variation: dividing training into phases with different goals, loads and volumes, and arranging them so the lifter is strongest when it matters. The idea grew out of Soviet and Eastern European sport science (Matveyev's traditional model, Verkhoshansky's concentrated loading, and later Issurin's block periodization) and was adapted to strength training in the West as linear and undulating models. Meta-analyses consistently find that periodized training builds more strength than non-periodized training, although the advantage shrinks when total volume is matched. No single model is clearly best, so the practical question is which model fits a given lifter's level, schedule and competition calendar.

## What periodization is (and isn't)

Periodization is a **plan for how training changes over time**. It answers three questions:

1. **What is the goal and when?** A meet date, a test day, or a general "get stronger this year".
2. **What should each stretch of training emphasise?** Muscle and work capacity, heavy strength, or peaking.
3. **How do volume, intensity and exercise selection shift to get there?**

It is **not** the same as progressive overload. Overload says "do a bit more over time"; periodization decides *what kind* of "more", *when*, and when to back off. It is also not a fixed recipe: the same principles produce very different programs for a novice, a busy masters lifter and a national-level competitor.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Periodization exists because intermediate and advanced lifters can no longer set personal bests every session. They need larger, more varied stimuli to keep adapting, and they need planned periods of lower fatigue so their strength can show (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith).

:::tip[In plain words]
Periodization is a plan for how your training changes over the weeks and months. Instead of doing the same workout forever, you have stretches where you build muscle, stretches where you lift heavier, and a short stretch where you rest up and get ready to lift your best.
:::

## A short history

### Matveyev and the traditional model

<Evidence level="practice" /> In the Soviet Union in the 1960s, Lev Matveyev organised the training of many sports into a single framework now called **traditional periodization**. The year is split into large **periods**:

- **Preparatory period**: high volume, moderate intensity, lots of general training. It starts with *general* preparation and moves to more *specific* preparation.
- **Competition period**: volume falls, intensity and specificity rise, and the athlete peaks for the main event.
- **Transition period**: a short phase of active rest before the next cycle.

The model trains **many qualities at the same time** ("mixed" training) and shifts their balance slowly over a long season.

Issurin (2010) argued that this model was built when sport science was young, and that it fits modern elite sport poorly. He listed four main limitations:

- different qualities trained together can produce **conflicting** responses;
- long periods of multi-targeted training cause **excess fatigue**;
- spreading work across many targets gives each one **too little stimulus** for well-trained athletes;
- it handles **several peaks per season** badly.

### The stress-adaptation idea behind it

<Evidence level="practice" /> Periodization theory has long leaned on Hans Selye's **general adaptation syndrome**: a stressor disturbs the body, the body adapts, and too much stress without recovery leads to exhaustion. This idea is debated. Kiely (2018) argued that stress science has moved on a great deal since Selye, and that periodization theory should be updated to match. Cunanan et al. (2018) replied that, despite its imprecisions, the model still explains the core link between training stress, fatigue management and adaptation. Both sides agree on the practical core used in this manual: training stress must be **dosed and followed by recovery**, which is the stimulus–recovery–adaptation idea from [Part 1 · Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/foundations/02-stress-recovery-adaptation/).

### Verkhoshansky and concentrated loading

<Evidence level="practice" /> Yuri Verkhoshansky, working mainly with speed–strength athletes, argued that highly trained athletes respond better to **concentrated** blocks of one type of strength work, followed by a period of lighter training in which the delayed gains show. He also stressed that special exercises must match the movement and force demands of the sport (Siff, *Supertraining*). His ideas fed directly into block periodization and into the special-exercise thinking covered in Part 3, Chapter 6: Special exercises and dynamic correspondence.

### Issurin and block periodization

<Evidence level="practice" /> Vladimir Issurin organised these ideas into **block periodization**: short, sequenced mesocycle blocks, each with **highly concentrated** work on a few targets, usually **accumulation → transmutation → realization**. The block model rests on two ideas: training **residues** (a quality fades slowly after you stop training it) and **sequencing** each block to set up the next (Issurin, 2010). It is covered fully in [Chapter 6: Block periodization](/lets-git-it/building-programs/06-block-periodization/).

### Western strength-training models

<Evidence level="practice" /> In the West, the traditional model was adapted to resistance training as **strength–power periodization**: a hypertrophy phase, then a strength phase, then a power or peaking phase, with intensity stepping up and volume coming down as the phases progress (Hartmann et al., 2015). This is what most strength coaches call **linear periodization** ([Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/building-programs/04-linear-periodization/)). **Undulating periodization** varies the same variables much more often: from session to session (daily undulating) or week to week (weekly undulating) ([Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/building-programs/05-undulating-periodization/)).

### Modern flexible approaches

<Evidence level="moderate" /> Research since the 2000s shows that people respond to the same training very differently. Kiely (2012) argued that rigid, rule-based plans fit this poorly and called for training systems that **respond** to the individual. In practice this has produced:

- **Autoregulation** with RPE/RIR targets, load drops and velocity (covered in [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/) and Part 3).
- **Flexible undulating models**, where the lifter chooses which planned session to do on a given day based on how they feel (McNamara & Stearne, 2010; Colquhoun et al., 2017).
- **Hybrid plans**: a long-term structure (blocks or phases toward a meet) with day-to-day adjustment inside it.

:::tip[In plain words]
The idea of planning training in phases started with Soviet coaches in the 1960s. Later coaches found that very strong athletes do better with shorter, more focused blocks. Today most good coaches use a long-term plan but adjust it day by day to how the lifter is actually doing.
:::

## The terms coaches use

| Term | Typical length | What it is | Powerlifting example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Macrocycle** | Several months to a year | The whole plan toward a major goal | From one national meet to the next |
| **Mesocycle** (block) | 3–6 weeks | A stretch with one main emphasis | A 4-week strength block |
| **Microcycle** | Usually 1 week | The repeating unit of sessions | Mon/Wed/Fri/Sat training week |
| **Session** | One workout | The individual training day | Heavy squat + bench volume |
| **Preparatory period** | Long, early in the plan | Building capacity and muscle; general → specific | Off-season hypertrophy and volume |
| **Competition period** | Weeks before the meet | Specific, heavy work; peaking | Heavy singles, then taper |
| **Transition period** | 1–2 weeks | Active rest and recovery | Light, varied training after a meet |
| **Deload** | Usually 1 week | Planned drop in volume and/or intensity | Same lifts, about half the sets |
| **Taper** | 1–3 weeks | Fatigue reduced while intensity is kept | Final weeks before the meet (Part 4) |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Lengths are typical, not fixed. In powerlifting the "macrocycle" is often simply **meet to meet**, so a lifter with two meets a year has two macrocycles.

:::tip[In plain words]
A macrocycle is the big plan, often from one meet to the next. A mesocycle is a block of a few weeks with one main goal. A microcycle is usually one training week. A deload is an easier week to let tiredness clear.
:::

## Comparing the main models

| Model | How load and volume change | Main idea | Who it suits | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Traditional** (Matveyev) | Long periods; volume high early, intensity rises toward the season's peak | Many qualities trained together, balance shifts slowly | Sports with one long season; historical base for the others | Too little stimulus per quality for advanced lifters; long build-up |
| **Linear** (strength–power) | Every few weeks: reps fall, load rises, volume falls | Build muscle, then strength, then peak | Intermediates with one clear meet date; lifters who like a simple plan | Heavy loads are rare early; early gains can fade by the meet |
| **Daily undulating (DUP)** | Changes session to session within the week | Train several rep ranges each week; frequent heavy practice | Intermediates training each lift 2–3×/week | Too many hard days if heavy, medium and light days are not truly different |
| **Weekly undulating (WUP)** | Changes week to week | A full week at one emphasis, then rotate | Lifters who prefer weekly themes; limited weekly frequency | Less studied; mixed results |
| **Block** (Verkhoshansky, Issurin) | 2–6-week blocks with concentrated goals in sequence | Concentrate, then sequence | Intermediate to advanced lifters; several meets a year | Qualities from early blocks fade if blocks run too long |
| **Flexible / autoregulated** | Plan set in advance, daily choice or load set by RPE | Match training to readiness | Lifters who rate RPE well; irregular schedules | Needs honest effort ratings; can drift without a long-term plan |

Conjugate methods and Sheiko-style high-frequency programming are periodized approaches too; they are covered in [Chapter 7: Conjugate and concurrent methods](/lets-git-it/building-programs/07-conjugate-concurrent/) and [Chapter 12: Sheiko-style programming](/lets-git-it/building-programs/12-sheiko-style-programming/).

## What the evidence says

### Periodized vs. non-periodized training

- <Evidence level="strong" /> **Periodized training builds more maximal strength.** An early meta-analysis found a large overall advantage for periodized programs (effect size 0.84) and concluded the benefit held for men and women, all ages and all training backgrounds (Rhea & Alderman, 2004). When the authors isolated the effect of **variation itself**, controlling for volume, frequency and intensity, it was much smaller (effect size 0.25).
- <Evidence level="strong" /> A later meta-analysis of 18 studies found a moderate advantage for periodized programs on 1RM (effect size 0.43). Improvements were greater among untrained participants, and longer studies and higher training frequency were linked to larger gains (Williams et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="strong" /> In 35 studies where **total volume was matched** between groups, periodized training still produced larger 1RM gains (effect size 0.31), but **not more muscle growth** (Moesgaard et al., 2022).

**The pattern:** part of the advantage in older studies came from periodized groups simply doing more or different work. With volume matched, the benefit is smaller but still present for strength.

### Comparing models

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Linear vs. undulating:** one meta-analysis of 17 studies (510 participants) found no difference for upper- or lower-body strength (Harries et al., 2015). A later volume-equated meta-analysis found a small advantage for undulating models, seen in trained but not untrained participants (Moesgaard et al., 2022). Details are in [Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/building-programs/04-linear-periodization/) and [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/building-programs/05-undulating-periodization/).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Block vs. other models:** only a few small studies exist, with mixed results. In 24 trained men over 15 weeks, block and traditional periodization gave similar lower-body strength, with block possibly better for bench press power and strength (Bartolomei et al., 2014). In 17 trained women over 10 weeks, a weekly undulating program increased lower-body strength more than a block program (Bartolomei et al., 2015). In 31 track and field athletes, block and daily undulating gave similar gains, with block using less total work (Painter et al., 2012). The 2022 meta-analysis found too few studies to compare models other than linear and undulating (Moesgaard et al., 2022).

### Limits of the research

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Most studies are **short** (a few weeks to a few months), use **untrained or recreationally trained** people, and many test exercises such as the leg press rather than the competition lifts. Harries et al. (2015) named short study length and participants' training history as key confounders.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A review noted that the apparent superiority of periodization might partly reflect how the studies are designed, and that the literature has several limitations (Evans, 2019).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Almost no studies follow competitive powerlifters through a full year with meets. Long-term planning therefore still rests mostly on coaching experience.

**What this means for coaches:** <Evidence level="strong" /> planned variation beats doing the same thing indefinitely. <Evidence level="moderate" /> Among periodized models the differences are small and inconsistent. Fit the model to the lifter, and run it consistently.

:::tip[In plain words]
Research clearly shows that planned changes in training beat doing the same workout all the time. But no single type of plan clearly wins. Pick one that fits the lifter's schedule and goals, and stick with it long enough to work.
:::

## How to choose a model

<Evidence level="practice" /> Use these questions in order (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith; Kiely, 2012):

| Question | If… | Lean toward… |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Is the lifter still a novice?** | Can still add load every session | Novice linear progression ([Part 1 · Chapter 7](/lets-git-it/foundations/07-novice-linear-progression/)), no formal periodization |
| **2. How far is the next meet?** | One meet, 10–16 weeks away | Linear or block; both end in a peak |
| | Two or more meets a year | Block; short blocks re-sequenced for each meet |
| | No meet planned | Undulating or flexible; peak only for occasional tests |
| **3. How often does the lifter train each lift?** | Each lift 2–3× per week | DUP fits naturally |
| | Each lift 1–2× per week | Linear or WUP |
| **4. How predictable is the lifter's life?** | Irregular sleep, shift work, travel | Flexible DUP or RPE-based loading |
| **5. How experienced is the lifter?** | Late intermediate or advanced, slow progress | Block or hybrid; concentrated stimuli |
| **6. What will the lifter actually follow?** | Prefers simple, fixed numbers | Linear with percentages |
| | Enjoys variety, rates RPE well | DUP or flexible |

<Evidence level="practice" /> **Most good programs are hybrids.** A common combination is block or linear structure across the macrocycle, undulating loads within each week, and RPE caps on every heavy set. The labels matter less than three things: a clear goal date, sensible fatigue management, and enough heavy, specific practice before the meet.

:::tip[In plain words]
Start with the lifter's next meet and their weekly schedule. One meet far away suits a simple step-by-step plan. Many meets suit short focused blocks. Lots of training days suit changing the workout every session. The best plan is the one the lifter will actually follow.
:::

## Sample programs

The two outlines below show a whole year at the level of mesocycles. They are skeletons: each block would be written out using [Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/building-programs/04-linear-periodization/), [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/building-programs/05-undulating-periodization/) or [Chapter 6](/lets-git-it/building-programs/06-block-periodization/). Loads are not shown. Meet dates are examples only; check the actual national federation calendar.

### Program 1: Year at a glance, two meets (block structure)

For an intermediate lifter (squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg) with two meets about six months apart. Trains 4 days per week.

| Weeks | Phase | Block | Main focus | Typical work on competition lifts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Transition | — | Recover from the previous meet; light, varied training | 2–3 sets at RPE 5–7; more variations |
| 3–8 | Preparatory | Accumulation 1 | Muscle, work capacity, technique | Sets of 6–10 at RPE 6–8; highest weekly volume |
| 9 | — | Deload | Shed fatigue | About half the sets |
| 10–14 | Preparatory | Transmutation 1 | Heavier specific strength | Sets of 3–5 at RPE 7–9 |
| 15–17 | Competition | Realization 1 | Peak | Singles and doubles at RPE 8–9; volume cut |
| 18 | Competition | **Meet 1** | Taper and compete | Openers early in the week, then rest |
| 19–20 | Transition | — | Recover; review the meet | Light training; plan the next cycle |
| 21–27 | Preparatory | Accumulation 2 | Address weak points found at Meet 1 | Sets of 6–10; weak-point variations |
| 28 | — | Deload | Shed fatigue | About half the sets |
| 29–33 | Preparatory | Transmutation 2 | Heavier specific strength | Sets of 2–5 at RPE 7–9 |
| 34–36 | Competition | Realization 2 | Peak | Singles and doubles at RPE 8–9 |
| 37 | Competition | **Meet 2** | Taper and compete | Openers early in the week, then rest |
| 38–52 | Off-season | Repeat or extend | Longer accumulation if muscle gain is the priority | As above |

**Why it is built this way:** two meets need two peaks, and block periodization handles repeated peaks better than one long linear build (Issurin, 2010). Each cycle starts with a transition so fatigue and minor aches clear before volume rises again. The second accumulation block is **driven by what the first meet revealed**: a missed squat lockout or a slow bench off the chest changes the variations chosen.

### Program 2: Year at a glance, one meet plus test days (linear and undulating)

For an intermediate lifter with a busy job who competes once a year and trains 3 days per week. For example, a female lifter in the 63 kg class (squat 120 kg, bench press 65 kg, deadlift 150 kg).

| Weeks | Phase | Model used | Main focus | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1–12 | Preparatory | Daily undulating (3-day) | Muscle and strength together | Heavy, medium and light day each week; deload every 4th week |
| 13 | Test | — | Estimate maxes | Work up to an RPE 9 single; no true max needed |
| 14–25 | Preparatory | Daily undulating (3-day), new variations | Strength; weak points | Same structure; percentages updated from week 13 |
| 26 | Test | — | Estimate maxes | Update numbers |
| 27–28 | Transition | — | Short mental break | Light training or other sport |
| 29–40 | Preparatory → competition | Linear (12 weeks) | Build to the meet | Volume falls and intensity rises, as in Chapter 4, Program 2 |
| 41 | Competition | **Meet** | Compete | — |
| 42–43 | Transition | — | Recover | — |
| 44–52 | Preparatory | Daily undulating | Rebuild volume | Start of the next year |

**Why it is built this way:** with only one meet, most of the year is spent **building**, and DUP lets a 3-day lifter practise heavy and moderate loads every week without a long run of sets of 10. Test days every three months check progress without a full peak. The 12 weeks before the meet switch to a **linear** structure because a simple step-by-step rise in intensity is an easy, reliable way to arrive at the platform fresh and practised with heavy singles. The model changes; the principles (overload, fatigue management, specificity) do not.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a model as a religion.** The research does not show one model is clearly best. Use the structure that fits the lifter.
- **Periodizing a novice.** A lifter who can still add weight every session doesn't need phases yet; complex plans slow them down.
- **Planning a year in detail and never adjusting.** Long plans are a map, not a contract. Review after every block.
- **Confusing variation with randomness.** Changing exercises and rep ranges every week without a goal is not periodization.
- **Ignoring the meet calendar.** Decide which meets matter most before writing the plan, then count backward.
- **Over-reading the studies.** Most were short and in non-powerlifters. Use them for direction, not exact numbers.

## Key takeaways

- Periodization is planned variation toward a goal: what to emphasise, when, and when to back off.
- It developed from Matveyev's traditional model through Verkhoshansky's concentrated loading to Issurin's block model, alongside Western linear and undulating models and modern flexible approaches.
- Macrocycle, mesocycle and microcycle describe the plan at the level of months, weeks and the training week.
- Periodized training clearly beats non-periodized training for strength, even when volume is matched, although the advantage is then smaller.
- Comparisons between models are mixed and mostly short-term; choose by the lifter's level, schedule, meet calendar and preference.
