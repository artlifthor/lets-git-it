## In one minute

Bigger muscles give a lifter a higher ceiling for strength. Among elite powerlifters, those with more lean mass lift more, though in the short term most strength gains come from skill and the nervous system. Muscle grows across a wide range of loads, from heavy sets of about 5 reps to light sets of 20–30 as long as sets end fairly close to failure and weekly volume is high enough. For powerlifters, the trick is to build muscle **without losing the skill and heavy practice that make that muscle show up on the platform**: keep the competition lifts in every week, add moderate-rep work and accessories, and keep the phase to a few weeks at a time. Weight class sets the limit on how much muscle a lifter can add, so plan hypertrophy phases far from a meet and with the target class in mind.

## Why muscle size matters for strength

Strength comes from two things: **how much muscle you have** and **how well you use it** (skill, coordination, and how hard the nervous system can drive the muscle). Both matter, but on different timescales.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 20 elite male powerlifters, fat-free mass was strongly related to competition squat, bench press and deadlift (correlations of about 0.86–0.95). Muscle thickness at many sites also tracked performance. The authors concluded that powerlifting performance may be limited by how much lean mass a lifter can build (Brechue & Abe, 2002). This is a cross-sectional study: it shows that bigger lifters lift more, not that adding muscle caused the strength.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 59 male classic (raw) powerlifters measured before a championship, a larger torso circumference (a measure of overall size, not muscle alone) was the main predictor of absolute strength in all three lifts and the total (Ferland et al., 2020).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 28 young men training the knee extensors isometrically for 12 weeks, the individual strength gains were explained partly by more nervous-system drive to the muscle (about 31% of the variation), partly by growth in quadriceps volume (about 19%), and partly by starting strength (Balshaw et al., 2017). Muscle growth contributed, but the nervous system mattered more in the short term.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 38 untrained people over 8 weeks, a group that only practised the 1RM test gained as much 1RM strength as a group doing four high-volume sets to failure, even though the high-volume group grew more muscle (Mattocks et al., 2017).

**The debate.** Researchers disagree about how much muscle growth *causes* strength gains. One group argues that growth of the muscle fibres is a real contributing cause (Taber et al., 2019); another argues the evidence for a causal link is weak (Loenneke et al., 2019).

**What this means for coaches.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Over weeks, strength gains are mostly skill and neural. Over years, an intermediate lifter who has squeezed out the easy skill gains needs more muscle to keep raising the ceiling. That is why hypertrophy phases appear in most long-term powerlifting plans (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith). Muscle is the engine; heavy, specific practice teaches the lifter to drive it.

:::tip[In plain words]
Strong lifters tend to carry a lot of muscle. In the short run you get stronger mostly by getting better at the lift, but sooner or later you need more muscle to keep improving. Building muscle raises your ceiling; practising heavy lifts lets you reach it.
:::

## How muscle grows (briefly)

<Evidence level="practice" /> The main driver of growth is **mechanical tension**: muscle fibres working hard against a load, especially near the end of a set when many fibres are recruited and contractions slow down. Metabolic stress and muscle damage have also been proposed as drivers, but their roles are less clear, and damage does not appear to be required for growth (Schoenfeld, *The Science and Development of Muscle Hypertrophy*). In practice, this means **hard sets** matter more than the pump or soreness.

Schoenfeld's book is the main reference for this chapter's programming ideas. The sections below set out each variable, what the research says, and how to apply it to a powerlifter.

:::tip[In plain words]
Muscles grow when they work hard against a load, especially in the last few tough reps of a set. Being sore or pumped is not the goal; hard sets are.
:::

## Hypertrophy training variables

### Load range

- <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 21 studies with all sets taken to failure found **similar muscle growth with light loads (60% 1RM or less) and heavy loads**, but **larger 1RM gains with heavy loads** (Schoenfeld et al., 2017). A later network meta-analysis agreed: growth was similar across load ranges, while heavy and moderate loads both beat light loads for 1RM strength, with a non-significant edge for heavy loads (Lopez et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 32 men (plus 10 controls) doing volume-matched bench press for 10 weeks, sets of 4RM, 8RM and 12RM produced similar chest muscle growth, but the 12RM group gained less 1RM strength than the other two (Kubo et al., 2021).

<Evidence level="practice" /> For a powerlifter, a sensible split:

| Exercise type | Typical reps | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Competition lifts in a hypertrophy phase | 5–10 | Builds muscle while keeping the load heavy enough to practise technique |
| Close variations (pause squat, close-grip bench) | 5–10 | Muscle plus skill at weak points |
| Multi-joint accessories (leg press, rows, split squats) | 8–15 | Volume with less fatigue and less technical risk |
| Single-joint accessories (leg curls, triceps, lateral raises) | 10–20 | Safe to push close to failure; small loads |

:::tip[In plain words]
Light and heavy weights both build muscle if the sets are hard. Heavy weights build more strength. So during a muscle-building phase, use medium-heavy weights on the big lifts and lighter, higher-rep sets on the extra exercises.
:::

### Volume

- <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 15 studies found a **graded dose–response**: more weekly sets per muscle produced more growth, and 10 or more sets per muscle per week tended to do best (Schoenfeld et al., 2017b).
- <Evidence level="strong" /> A larger, more recent analysis confirmed that both strength and size increase with weekly volume, with diminishing returns that flatten **more for strength than for size** (Pelland et al., 2025). In other words, extra sets keep paying off for muscle longer than they do for 1RM.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Start a hypertrophy phase at about **10–15 hard sets per muscle group per week** and add sets across the block while recovery allows, staying below the lifter's maximum recoverable volume. Volume landmarks are covered in [Chapter 8: Volume landmarks](/lets-git-it/building-programs/08-volume-landmarks/) (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith).

**Counting sets by muscle, not just by lift.** For hypertrophy, count hard sets per muscle group. A squat set counts for the quads and glutes; a Romanian deadlift counts for the hamstrings and glutes; a bench press counts for the chest, front shoulders and triceps.

:::tip[In plain words]
More hard sets build more muscle, up to the point where you can't recover. Start with about 10–15 hard sets per muscle each week and add a few as the weeks go on, as long as you still feel recovered.
:::

### Proximity to failure

- <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis found only a **trivial advantage** for training to failure for muscle growth, and none when studies using a strict definition of failure were analysed separately (Refalo et al., 2023).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A series of exploratory meta-regressions found that **muscle growth increased as sets ended closer to failure**, while **strength gains were similar across a wide range of reps in reserve** (Robinson et al., 2024). The authors stressed that reps in reserve were estimated, so the exact relationship is uncertain.
- <Evidence level="strong" /> For strength, training to failure gives no extra benefit over stopping short (Grgic et al., 2022).

<Evidence level="practice" /> Putting it together for powerlifters:

| Exercise | Target effort | Reps in reserve |
|---|---|---|
| Competition lifts | RPE 7–8.5 | 1.5–3 |
| Close variations | RPE 7–8.5 | 1.5–3 |
| Multi-joint accessories | RPE 8–9 | 1–2 |
| Single-joint accessories | RPE 9–10 on the last set | 0–1 |

Failure on a heavy squat or deadlift costs a lot of recovery and risks a technical breakdown. Failure on a leg curl or cable triceps extension costs little.

:::tip[In plain words]
For muscle, sets should be hard: stop with about one to three reps left. You don't need to grind to total failure on the big lifts. Save the all-out sets for small exercises like curls and triceps work, where it's safe and cheap.
:::

### Exercise selection

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 17 men training for 10 weeks, full squats grew the adductors and glutes more than half squats, and full-squat 1RM improved more (Kubo et al., 2019). Range of motion changes which muscles grow.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 20 adults training each leg differently for 12 weeks, seated leg curls (hamstrings trained at longer lengths) grew the whole hamstrings more than lying leg curls (about +14% vs +9%) (Maeo et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Planned variation of exercises can help growth, while too much random variation may hold it back (Kassiano et al., 2022).

<Evidence level="practice" /> Choose exercises that (1) train the muscles that limit the lifter's competition lifts, (2) use a full, controlled range of motion, and (3) the lifter can progress safely for several weeks. For most powerlifters the muscles that benefit most are the **quads, glutes, adductors, hamstrings, spinal erectors, upper back and lats, chest, and triceps**. Big muscles that the competition lifts already train hard (quads, chest) still respond to more direct work; muscles the lifts train only partly (hamstrings, lats, upper back) usually need accessories. Detailed accessory programming is in [Chapter 13: Programming accessories](/lets-git-it/building-programs/13-programming-accessories/).

:::tip[In plain words]
Pick exercises that train the muscles your big lifts depend on, through a full range of motion. Keep them for a few weeks so you can add weight, then change them on purpose, not at random.
:::

### Rest periods

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review found that both short (60 seconds or less) and longer rests can build muscle, with a possible advantage for longer rests in trained lifters (Grgic et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 21 trained men, 3-minute rests produced more thigh muscle growth and more squat and bench strength than 1-minute rests over 8 weeks (Schoenfeld et al., 2016).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Rest **2–4 minutes on the competition lifts and multi-joint exercises**, and **1–2 minutes on small single-joint exercises**. Pairing two non-competing accessories (for example a row and a leg curl) saves time without hurting either.

:::tip[In plain words]
Rest long enough that your next set is still a good, hard set. That is about two to four minutes on squats, benches, deadlifts and big exercises, and one to two minutes on small ones.
:::

### Frequency

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> In a large meta-regression, training frequency had no clearly identifiable effect on muscle growth, while strength did rise with frequency (Pelland et al., 2025). For hypertrophy, frequency is mainly a way of spreading the weekly volume.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Train each major muscle group **at least twice per week**. Above about 6–8 hard sets for one muscle in a single session, later sets tend to be lower quality, so split the work across days.

:::tip[In plain words]
It doesn't matter much how many days you train a muscle, as long as the weekly total of hard sets is there. Two or more sessions a week makes it easier to keep every set good.
:::

## Managing hypertrophy phases without losing skill

A hypertrophy phase is a risk for a powerlifter: weeks of sets of 8–12 can make heavy singles feel unfamiliar, and the 1RM may dip. The fix is to keep the skill alive while the muscle is built.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Strength is specific to the load and task trained: heavy-load training produced larger 1RM gains than light-load training (Schoenfeld et al., 2017; Lopez et al., 2021), and practising the test alone matched the strength gains of high-volume training in beginners (Mattocks et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 70 adults, cutting training to one-third or one-ninth of the previous dose for 32 weeks kept the muscle gained in young adults (but not in adults aged 60–75), and strength was largely kept even with no training (Bickel et al., 2011). Muscle and strength are easier to *keep* than to *build*, which is what makes the trade-off between phases work.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Rules for keeping skill during hypertrophy phases:

1. **Keep all three competition lifts in every week**, with competition technique and commands.
2. **Keep one heavy-ish exposure per lift per week**, such as a top set of 2–3 at RPE 7–8 before the volume work. This is the "skill set".
3. **Keep most competition-lift work at 5–8 reps (occasionally up to 10)**, so loads stay around 65–80% 1RM rather than 12–15-rep sets.
4. **Keep the phase short**: 4–8 weeks, then move to a strength phase (see [Chapter 6: Block periodization](/lets-git-it/building-programs/06-block-periodization/)).
5. **Expect a small dip in 1RM-type performance** late in a hard volume block. Judge the phase by rep performance and estimated 1RM at the end of the following strength block, not by a max test in week 6.

:::tip[In plain words]
When you build muscle, don't stop practising the competition lifts heavy. Do one fairly heavy set of each lift every week, keep most reps in the 5–8 range on the big lifts, and keep the muscle phase to a couple of months before switching back to heavier training.
:::

## Weight-class considerations

Powerlifting is scored within bodyweight classes. Muscle gain increases the ceiling for strength but also moves bodyweight, and a lifter cannot keep adding muscle and stay in the same class forever.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis found that training in an energy deficit **impaired gains in lean mass but not strength**, and a deficit of about 500 kcal per day was enough to prevent lean-mass gain (Murphy & Koehler, 2022). Building muscle while dieting hard does not work well.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Some energy surplus helps muscle gain, but the ideal size of the surplus has never been properly tested in lifters (Slater et al., 2019). A small surplus (see [Chapter 11: Nutrition](/lets-git-it/foundations/11-nutrition/)) limits fat gain.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Women and men gain muscle at a similar *relative* rate on the same program (Roberts et al., 2020). Female lifters benefit from hypertrophy phases in the same way; there is no reason to give them lighter, "toning" programs.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Decision guide for the coach:

| Situation | Hypertrophy approach |
|---|---|
| Lifter well below the top of their class, or planning to move up | Hypertrophy phase with a small surplus; accept slow bodyweight gain (about 0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per week is a common guideline) |
| Lifter at the top of their class, staying there | Hypertrophy phase at maintenance calories; aim to swap fat for muscle slowly; prioritise muscles that limit their weakest lift |
| Lifter carrying extra fat, moving down a class | Keep training volume moderate, use a modest deficit, and keep heavy work to hold strength; save the big hypertrophy push for after the cut |
| Less than about 8–10 weeks to a meet | Not a time for a hypertrophy phase; keep bodyweight stable and train specifically |

**Classes and moving up.** A lifter who moves up a class usually needs time for the new bodyweight to become muscle and for their technique to adjust to it. Plan class changes months ahead, not weeks. Making weight for a meet is covered in Part 4, Chapter 4 (Making weight).

:::tip[In plain words]
More muscle means more strength, but it also means more bodyweight. Decide which weight class you're aiming for first. If you have room to grow, eat a little more than you burn while you build muscle. If you need to stay in your class, build muscle slowly at your current weight. Don't try to build muscle while dieting hard.
:::

## Sample programs

Intermediate lifter: squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg, about 83 kg bodyweight. Loads for the competition lifts use the RPE chart in [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg. Accessory loads are chosen in the session to hit the target RPE.

### Program 1: A 5-week hypertrophy block for a powerlifter (4 days)

**Why it is built this way.** Each competition lift has one heavy "skill set" per week, then moderate-rep volume. Accessories cover the muscles the lifts train only partly (hamstrings, lats, upper back, triceps). Volume rises from week 1 to week 4; week 5 is a deload. Approximate weekly hard sets in week 1: quads 14, hamstrings and glutes 13, chest 15, lats and upper back 9 (plus deadlifts), triceps 3 of direct work on top of all the pressing.

**Week 1**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat (skill set) | 1 × 3 | RPE 7 (~150 kg) |
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 8 | RPE 7 (~127.5 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 8 | RPE 7 (~85 kg) |
| 1 | Seated leg curl | 3 × 10–12 | RPE 9 |
| 1 | Chest-supported row | 3 × 10–12 | RPE 8–9 |
| 2 | Deadlift (skill set) | 1 × 3 | RPE 7 (~175 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 (~160 kg) |
| 2 | Incline dumbbell press | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 2 | Bulgarian split squat | 3 × 10 each leg | RPE 8 |
| 2 | Lat pulldown | 3 × 12 | RPE 8–9 |
| 3 | Bench press (skill set) | 1 × 3 | RPE 7 (~100 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) |
| 3 | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7–8 |
| 3 | Overhead cable triceps extension | 3 × 12–15 | RPE 9 |
| 4 | Bench press | 3 × 10 | RPE 7 (~77.5 kg) |
| 4 | Leg press | 3 × 12 | RPE 8–9 |
| 4 | Back extension | 3 × 12–15 | RPE 8 |
| 4 | One-arm dumbbell row | 3 × 10 each arm | RPE 8–9 |

**Weeks 2–5**

| Week | Competition lifts | Accessories |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | As written (RPE 7) | As written |
| 2 | Same sets and reps, +2.5 kg (bench) or +5 kg (squat, deadlift) if week 1 was on target | +1 set on two accessories per day |
| 3 | Same loads as week 2, +1 set on the volume work (e.g. squat 5 × 8); target RPE 7.5–8 | Keep sets; add reps or load (double progression) |
| 4 | +2.5–5 kg; target RPE 8; skill sets at RPE 7.5–8 | Keep sets; add reps or load |
| 5 (deload) | Skill sets only at RPE 6–7, plus 2 volume sets at ~70% | 1–2 sets per accessory, RPE 7 |

**After this block:** move to a strength block of sets of 3–5 (see Chapter 6 and [Chapter 14: Building a mesocycle](/lets-git-it/building-programs/14-building-a-mesocycle/)).

### Program 2: Lower-body focus week (4 days)

For the same lifter whose squat and deadlift lag behind the bench press, or whose legs are clearly the smallest part of the lifter. Upper-body work drops to roughly maintenance (about 7–9 bench sets per week), while lower-body volume rises (about 16–18 hard sets for the quads, 12–15 for the hamstrings and glutes).

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~137.5 kg) |
| 1 | Leg press | 3 × 10–12 | RPE 8–9 |
| 1 | Seated leg curl | 3 × 10–12 | RPE 9 |
| 1 | Bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift (skill set) | 1 × 3 | RPE 7 (~175 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~165 kg) |
| 2 | Bulgarian split squat | 3 × 8–10 each leg | RPE 8 |
| 2 | Back extension | 3 × 12 | RPE 8 |
| 2 | Pull-up or lat pulldown | 3 × 8–10 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Bench press (skill set) | 1 × 3 | RPE 7 (~100 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| 3 | Chest-supported row | 3 × 10 | RPE 8–9 |
| 3 | Leg extension | 3 × 12–15 | RPE 9 |
| 4 | Squat (skill set) | 1 × 3 | RPE 7 (~150 kg) |
| 4 | High-bar or safety-bar squat | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7–8 |
| 4 | Adductor machine or Copenhagen plank | 3 × 12 / 3 × 20–30 s | RPE 8 |

**Why:** lower-body volume is high but spread over four days so no single session exceeds about 8 hard sets for the quads. The bench press keeps a skill set and enough volume to hold strength while the legs are prioritised (Bickel et al., 2011, on maintaining with less volume). Run this emphasis for one 4–6 week block, then return to balanced training.

### Program 3: Minimal-time hypertrophy (3 days, about 60 minutes each)

For a lifter with limited time (work, family, a long commute). Every session has one heavy skill set, moderate-rep work on the competition lifts, and two accessories paired as a superset to save time.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat (skill set) | 1 × 3 | RPE 7 (~150 kg) |
| Mon | Squat | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 (~127.5 kg) |
| Mon | Bench press | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 (~85 kg) |
| Mon | Superset: chest-supported row + seated leg curl | 3 × 10 each | RPE 9 |
| Wed | Bench press (skill set) | 1 × 3 | RPE 7 (~100 kg) |
| Wed | Bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) |
| Wed | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7–8 |
| Wed | Superset: lat pulldown + overhead triceps extension | 3 × 12 each | RPE 9 |
| Fri | Deadlift (skill set) | 1 × 3 | RPE 7 (~175 kg) |
| Fri | Deadlift | 2 × 6 | RPE 7 (~160 kg) |
| Fri | Bench press | 3 × 10 | RPE 7 (~77.5 kg) |
| Fri | Superset: Bulgarian split squat + one-arm dumbbell row | 2 × 10 each | RPE 8–9 |

**Why:** about 6–10 hard sets per major muscle per week, which is below the volume of Program 1 but still enough to grow, especially for a lifter coming from strength-focused training. Accessories are pushed close to failure because they are cheap to recover from, which gets more growth per set (Robinson et al., 2024). Supersets of non-competing muscles keep the session within an hour. Progress by adding reps, then load, each week; add a fourth set to the competition-lift volume work if time allows.

## Common mistakes

- **Dropping heavy work entirely.** Weeks of only sets of 10–12 leave the lifter unfamiliar with heavy loads. Keep one skill set per lift per week.
- **Turning powerlifting into bodybuilding.** The competition lifts still make up the core; accessories support them.
- **Going to failure on squats and deadlifts.** It adds fatigue and risk without adding meaningful growth. Push the small exercises hard instead.
- **Counting only competition-lift sets.** For muscle, count sets per muscle group, including accessories.
- **Bulking without a plan for the weight class.** Decide the target class before adding bodyweight.
- **Trying to build muscle in a large calorie deficit.** Lean-mass gains largely stall.
- **Judging the phase by a max test in its last week.** Fatigue hides strength; judge it after the following strength block.

## Key takeaways

- More muscle raises a lifter's long-term strength ceiling; short-term strength gains are mostly skill and neural.
- Muscle grows across a wide range of loads when sets are hard; heavy loads are still needed for 1RM strength.
- Volume is the main dial for growth: start around 10–15 hard sets per muscle per week and build from there.
- Growth improves as sets get closer to failure; push accessories hard and keep competition lifts at RPE 7–8.5.
- Keep all three competition lifts and a weekly heavy skill set during hypertrophy phases, and keep phases to 4–8 weeks.
- Plan muscle gain around the lifter's weight class, and avoid building muscle in a large deficit.
