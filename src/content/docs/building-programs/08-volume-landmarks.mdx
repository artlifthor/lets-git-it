## In one minute

Volume landmarks are a coaching framework from Israetel, Hoffmann and Smith that names four points on the volume scale: **maintenance volume (MV)**, the least work that keeps strength; **minimum effective volume (MEV)**, the least that produces progress; **maximum adaptive volume (MAV)**, the range that produces the best progress; and **maximum recoverable volume (MRV)**, the most a lifter can recover from. The research behind it is solid in outline: more weekly sets produce more strength, but with diminishing returns that set in early for strength, and very little volume is needed to maintain or even build some strength. The exact landmark numbers are not research findings; they differ between lifters, lifts and training phases, so the coach estimates them from each lifter's log. In practice, a mesocycle starts near MEV, adds sets each week toward MRV, and then deloads to around MV.

## What the landmarks are

<Evidence level="practice" /> The landmarks come from Israetel, Hoffmann and Smith (*Scientific Principles of Strength Training*). They were developed mainly with muscle groups and hypertrophy in mind. Powerlifting coaches apply them **per lift**: weekly hard sets of the squat, bench press or deadlift and their close variations. This is a practical framework for planning, not a set of measured physiological thresholds.

| Landmark | Name | What it means | What happens if you sit there |
|---|---|---|---|
| **MV** | Maintenance volume | The least weekly work that keeps current strength | Strength holds; little or no progress |
| **MEV** | Minimum effective volume | The least weekly work that produces measurable progress | Slow but real progress, low fatigue |
| **MAV** | Maximum adaptive volume | The range of weekly work that produces the most progress | Best progress for the recovery cost |
| **MRV** | Maximum recoverable volume | The most weekly work the lifter can recover from | Fatigue builds faster than fitness; performance soon drops |

The order is always the same: MV is lowest, then MEV, then MAV, with MRV highest. The gaps between them, and where they sit, differ between lifters and change over time.

:::tip[In plain words]
Think of volume as a dial. Turn it too low and you stand still. Turn it a little higher and you start improving. There is a sweet spot where you improve the most, and past that point you just get more tired. The four landmarks are names for those points on the dial.
:::

## How to count volume for the landmarks

The landmarks only work if sets are counted the same way every week.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Count hard sets:** work sets at roughly RPE 6 or higher. Warm-ups and very easy technique sets don't count.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Count per lift:** the competition lift plus its close variations (pause squat, tempo squat, close-grip bench, Spoto press, deficit or block deadlift). These train the same movement and the same skill.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Indirect work counts, but less.** A large meta-regression of 67 studies found that adaptations were best predicted when sets that trained a muscle only indirectly were counted as **half a set** rather than a full set or zero (Pelland et al., 2025). For powerlifters, this means a leg press or a Romanian deadlift adds some training to the squat or deadlift muscles, but not as much as the lift itself.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Track squat and deadlift together as well.** They share many muscles (hips, back, thighs). A rise in deadlift volume raises the load on the squat muscles too, so watch total lower-body sets as well as each lift.

In this chapter's programs, the weekly totals count the competition lift and its close variations as full sets. Accessories are listed but tracked separately.

:::tip[In plain words]
Count the hard sets you do for each lift every week. Squat variations count toward squat, bench variations toward bench, and so on. Helper exercises such as leg press count a bit, but not as much as the real thing.
:::

## What the research says about volume

### More volume, more strength, with diminishing returns

- <Evidence level="strong" /> Across 67 studies, both strength and muscle size kept rising as weekly sets rose, but **each extra set added less than the one before**. The flattening was **much stronger for strength than for muscle size** (Pelland et al., 2025).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> An earlier meta-analysis found larger strength gains with more weekly sets than with fewer, though the difference was small (Ralston et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="strong" /> For muscle size, a meta-analysis found a graded dose-response: each extra weekly set was linked to a slightly larger gain (Schoenfeld et al., 2017).

### Strength often responds to modest volume in trained lifters

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 34 trained men training three times a week for 8 weeks, 1, 3 or 5 sets per exercise produced **similar squat and bench press 1RM gains**, while muscle growth increased with volume (Schoenfeld et al., 2019).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 49 trained men doing 9, 18 or 27 weekly sets for the biceps over 6 weeks, no group differed significantly, though effect sizes favoured the two higher volumes over the lowest (the higher-volume groups also trained twice a week instead of once) (Heaselgrave et al., 2019).

### More is not always better

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 51 experienced junior weightlifters over 10 weeks, a **moderate**-volume group showed larger strength gains by effect size than low- and high-volume groups (significantly so only vs low volume, in the snatch). The authors suggested lifters may do best at around **85% or less of the maximum volume they can tolerate** (González-Badillo et al., 2005).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 35 trained lifters who squatted about twice bodyweight, 18 weekly lower-body sets showed a strong trend toward larger squat 1RM gains than 24 sets (about +25.5 kg vs +9.5 kg over 8 weeks; 12 sets gave about +18.3 kg), with no difference in muscle growth (Aube et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In contrast, in 27 trained men, 32 weekly sets per muscle group produced larger gains by effect size in lower-body 1RM and muscle thickness than 16 sets over 8 weeks (Brigatto et al., 2022).

These two small studies point in different directions. The likely lesson is not that one number is right, but that **the best volume depends on the lifter and the program**, which is exactly what the landmarks framework assumes.

:::tip[In plain words]
More sets usually means more strength, but each extra set helps less than the last, and for strength the benefit levels off fairly early. Some lifters do best on moderate volume and improve less when they do too much. Others handle and benefit from a lot. You have to find out which kind of lifter you have.
:::

## Maintenance volume (MV)

**The least weekly work that keeps strength.** Used during deloads, busy periods, injuries to other areas, or for a lift that is not the current priority.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> A narrative review, drawing mainly on one long study, concluded that strength and muscle size can be maintained for up to 32 weeks with as little as **one session per week and one set per exercise**, as long as the **relative load stays high**. Older adults may need about two sessions and 2–3 sets per exercise to keep muscle size. The authors noted there was not enough data to give specific numbers for athletes (Spiering et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 70 adults who trained for 16 weeks, cutting the weekly dose to one-third or even one-ninth preserved muscle size in younger adults (but not in adults aged 60–75) over the next 32 weeks. Strength gained was largely kept, even in the group that stopped completely, with only a slight fall by the end (Bickel et al., 2011).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> For a trained powerlifter, a reasonable maintenance dose is **2–4 heavy sets per lift per week (1–3 for deadlift), with at least one session at RPE 7–8**. Keep the load heavy; cut sets first.

:::tip[In plain words]
Keeping strength takes much less work than building it. A couple of heavy sets per lift each week is usually enough for a few weeks, as long as the weights stay heavy. Cut the number of sets, not the weight.
:::

## Minimum effective volume (MEV)

**The least weekly work that still produces progress.** A mesocycle usually starts here, so there is room to add volume later.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of the minimum dose for trained men found that a **single set of 6–12 reps at about 70–85% 1RM, taken to failure, 2–3 times per week** for 8–12 weeks produced significant squat and bench press 1RM gains (meta-analysed averages about +17.5 kg squat and +8.3 kg bench). The authors called these gains "suboptimal yet significant", and noted it is unclear whether this applies to the deadlift, to women, or to highly trained strength athletes (Androulakis-Korakakis et al., 2020).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> For intermediate powerlifters, MEV is usually higher than those single-set doses, because they are stronger, closer to their ceiling, and stop sets short of failure. A practical starting estimate is **about half to two-thirds of the volume the lifter has recently been progressing on**.

:::tip[In plain words]
You don't need much work to keep getting a bit stronger, especially early on. The minimum is the least you can do and still see progress. Start a training block close to that point so there is room to do more later.
:::

## Maximum adaptive volume (MAV)

**The range that gives the best progress.** It is not one number: it shifts during a mesocycle as the lifter adapts and as fatigue accumulates.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> The moderate-volume results above (González-Badillo et al., 2005; Aube et al., 2022) suggest MAV often sits **below** the most a lifter can tolerate.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 16 trained lifters, a leg trained with an **individualized volume** (20% more weekly sets than the lifter's own recent training log) grew more over 8 weeks than the other leg trained with a standard 22 sets per week (Scarpelli et al., 2022). This was a muscle-size study, but it supports setting volume from the lifter's own history instead of a textbook number.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> You find MAV by watching performance: it is the volume range where estimated 1RM and bar speed at fixed loads improve week to week while recovery keeps up.

:::tip[In plain words]
The sweet spot is the amount of work where you improve the most without falling apart. It is usually somewhere in the middle, not at the most you can survive. The best guide is what has worked for you before, plus a little more.
:::

## Maximum recoverable volume (MRV)

**The most work the lifter can recover from.** Above it, fatigue grows faster than fitness and performance falls.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> MRV is not a target. Training at or just over MRV for a week or so can be useful at the end of a mesocycle (a short "overreach") because the deload that follows lets fatigue clear (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith). Staying above it for several weeks leads to stalled or falling performance.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> MRV falls when recovery is worse: poor sleep, high life stress, a calorie deficit or weight cut, heavy phases (more sets at 85%+ cost more), illness, or travel. It usually rises slowly with training age.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Signs you are above MRV are covered in the table below and in [Chapter 11: Deloads and fatigue management](/lets-git-it/building-programs/11-deloads-fatigue-management/).

:::tip[In plain words]
This is the most training you can recover from. Going a little past it for a week right before an easy week is fine. Staying past it for weeks makes you weaker, not stronger. Bad sleep, stress or dieting all lower this ceiling.
:::

## Starting estimates for intermediate lifters

<Evidence level="practice" /> These are this manual's **starting guesses** for an intermediate lifter, counting the competition lift plus close variations as hard sets per week. They bracket the 8–15 sets per lift in [Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/foundations/04-training-variables/) for squat and bench; the deadlift usually sits lower. They are **not research findings**; the research gives the shape of the curve, not powerlifting-specific numbers. Replace them with the lifter's own numbers as soon as you have a log.

| Lift | MV | MEV | MAV (typical range) | MRV (typical) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Squat | 2–4 | 6–8 | 8–14 | 14–20 |
| Bench press | 2–4 | 6–10 | 10–18 | 18–24 |
| Deadlift | 1–3 | 3–5 | 5–8 | 8–12 |

Why the lifts differ: <Evidence level="practice" /> the bench press involves smaller muscles and usually recovers faster, so most lifters tolerate more bench sets. Heavy deadlifts are often reported to take the longest to recover from (see [Chapter 9: Training frequency](/lets-git-it/building-programs/09-training-frequency/)), and they also tire the squat muscles, so deadlift landmarks are lowest.

## How to estimate a lifter's landmarks

<Evidence level="practice" /> A step-by-step method, built on the framework from Israetel, Hoffmann and Smith:

1. **Start from the log.** Count the lifter's actual weekly hard sets per lift over the last 4–8 weeks, and whether they progressed. That number is the first anchor: if they progressed, it sits at or above MEV.
2. **Set the first week near MEV.** Use the log number, or about two-thirds of it if the lifter is coming off a hard block.
3. **Add sets weekly.** Add 1–2 sets per lift per week (bench can take 2; deadlift usually 1).
4. **Watch fixed markers each week:** estimated 1RM from a top set, RPE at a fixed benchmark load (for example the squat at 150 kg for 3), joint aches, sleep, and motivation.
5. **Read the result.** The weeks where markers improve are around MAV. The week where performance drops or RPE at the benchmark load climbs by about 1 point for two sessions in a row marks MRV.
6. **Plan the next mesocycle** to start around MEV and finish just below that MRV, then re-estimate.

| What you see over 1–2 weeks | Likely position | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Estimated 1RM flat for 3+ weeks, RPE at benchmark load unchanged, recovers easily | Below or at MEV | Add 2–3 sets per lift next block |
| Estimated 1RM rising, RPE at benchmark load steady or falling, recovered by next session of that lift | Within MAV | Keep adding 1–2 sets per week as planned |
| Performance flat, RPE at benchmark load up ~0.5, mild aches | Approaching MRV | Hold volume; deload within 1–2 weeks |
| Performance down for 2+ sessions, RPE at benchmark load up 1+, aches, poor sleep or motivation | At or above MRV | Deload now; set next block's peak volume lower |

:::tip[In plain words]
You find your numbers by trying. Start with roughly what you've been doing, add a set or two each week, and watch how a standard weight feels. When a weight you know starts feeling heavier and your lifts go down, you've found your ceiling. Next time, stop just below it.
:::

## How volume progresses across a mesocycle

<Evidence level="practice" /> The classic pattern from Israetel, Hoffmann and Smith:

- **Week 1:** start near MEV, at moderate RPE (about 7).
- **Middle weeks:** add 1–2 sets per lift per week, through MAV. RPE targets rise gradually (7 → 8).
- **Last loading week:** at or near MRV, a deliberate short overreach.
- **Deload:** drop to around MV, keep loads moderately heavy, for about a week.
- **Next mesocycle:** start again near MEV, often a little higher than last time if the lifter adapted.

**Volume and intensity trade off.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Sets at 85%+ and singles cost more recovery per set than sets of 6–8 at 70–75%. In heavier blocks (transmutation, realization in [Chapter 6](/lets-git-it/building-programs/06-block-periodization/)), every landmark sits lower, so a lifter whose squat MRV is 18 sets in a hypertrophy block might reach it at 10–12 sets in a heavy block. Don't carry accumulation-block set numbers into a heavy block.

**Not every program ramps volume.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Many powerlifting programs hold volume steady and raise intensity across a block instead. That is fine. The landmarks still apply: the steady volume should sit within MAV, and the coach should notice when it starts to exceed MRV as loads get heavier.

:::tip[In plain words]
A training block starts with fewer sets and adds a few each week. By the last hard week you're doing about as much as you can handle. Then you take an easy week and start the next block. Heavier weights cost more, so heavy blocks use fewer sets.
:::

## Sample programs

Default lifter: intermediate man, ~83 kg, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. Loads come from the RPE chart in [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg. These are illustrations; adjust them using the RPE targets.

### Program 1: A 5-week volume ramp (weekly set totals)

Starting landmarks estimated from the lifter's log: squat MEV ~8 / MRV ~15, bench MEV ~10 / MRV ~18, deadlift MEV ~5 / MRV ~9. The block starts at MEV and stops just short of the estimated MRV.

| Week | Squat sets (day 1 + day 3) | Bench sets (day 1 + day 2 + day 3) | Deadlift sets (day 2 + day 4) | RPE cap | Position |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8 (4 + 4) | 10 (4 + 3 + 3) | 5 (3 + 2) | 7 | MEV |
| 2 | 10 (5 + 5) | 12 (5 + 4 + 3) | 6 (4 + 2) | 7.5 | Low MAV |
| 3 | 12 (6 + 6) | 14 (5 + 5 + 4) | 7 (4 + 3) | 8 | MAV |
| 4 | 14 (7 + 7) | 16 (6 + 5 + 5) | 8 (5 + 3) | 8 | Near MRV |
| 5 (deload) | 4 (2 + 2) | 5 (2 + 2 + 1) | 2 (2 + 0) | 6 | ~MV |

**Why it is built this way:** sets rise by 2 per week for squat and bench and by 1 for deadlift, which recovers more slowly and shares muscles with the squat. RPE caps rise slowly so that the extra sets, not extra effort, drive the overload. The deload keeps all lifts in but cuts sets by about two-thirds.

### Program 2: Week 1 vs. week 4 of the same block (4 days)

The sessions behind Program 1. Same exercises every week; only the number of sets and the RPE cap change.

**Week 1 (around MEV)**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 (~170 kg) |
| 2 | Bench press | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 (~85 kg) |
| 2 | Barbell row | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Pause squat (2 s) | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Deficit deadlift (5 cm) | 2 × 5 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Leg press, back extension | 3 × 10 each | RPE 8 |

**Week 4 (near MRV)**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 7 × 5 | RPE 7.5–8 (~145 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 6 × 6 | RPE 7.5–8 (~92.5–95 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 5 × 4 | RPE 7.5–8 (~175 kg) |
| 2 | Bench press | 5 × 8 | RPE 7.5–8 (~87.5 kg) |
| 2 | Barbell row | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Pause squat (2 s) | 7 × 4 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Close-grip bench press | 5 × 6 | RPE 8 |
| 4 | Deficit deadlift (5 cm) | 3 × 5 | RPE 8 |
| 4 | Leg press, back extension | 3 × 10 each | RPE 8 |

**Rules for week 4:** the RPE cap applies to every set. If a set goes above RPE 8.5, take 5% off for the remaining sets, or stop the exercise. Accessories stay the same so they don't add hidden lower-body fatigue. If the lifter cannot finish week 4 within the caps, their MRV is lower than estimated: note it and plan the next block lower.

### Program 3: A 3-day ramp for a lifter with lower landmarks, and how to read it

A female lifter in the 63 kg class, squat 120 kg, bench press 65 kg, deadlift 150 kg, training 3 days per week. Her log shows steady progress on about 6 squat, 8 bench and 3 deadlift sets per week (counting Romanian deadlifts as half sets), so those are her starting MEV estimates.

| Week | Day 1 | Day 2 | Day 3 | Weekly sets (S / B / D) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat 3 × 5 RPE 7 (~95 kg); bench 4 × 6 RPE 7 (~50 kg) | Deadlift 2 × 4 RPE 7 (~122.5 kg); bench 2 × 8 RPE 7 | Pause squat 3 × 4 RPE 7; close-grip bench 2 × 6; RDL 2 × 6 | 6 / 8 / 2 + 2 RDL |
| 2 | Squat 4 × 5; bench 4 × 6 | Deadlift 3 × 4; bench 3 × 8 | Pause squat 3 × 4; close-grip bench 3 × 6; RDL 2 × 6 | 7 / 10 / 3 + 2 RDL |
| 3 | Squat 4 × 5; bench 5 × 6 | Deadlift 3 × 4; bench 3 × 8 | Pause squat 4 × 4; close-grip bench 4 × 6; RDL 2 × 6 | 8 / 12 / 3 + 2 RDL |
| 4 | Squat 5 × 5; bench 5 × 6 | Deadlift 4 × 4; bench 4 × 8 | Pause squat 4 × 4; close-grip bench 4 × 6; RDL 2 × 6 | 9 / 13 / 4 + 2 RDL |
| 5 | Deload: squat 2 × 5, bench 2 × 6 | Deadlift 2 × 3; bench 2 × 6 | Pause squat 2 × 3; close-grip bench 1 × 6 | 4 / 5 / 2 |

RPE caps: 7 in week 1, 7.5 in week 2, 8 in weeks 3–4, about 6 in the deload. The Romanian deadlift (RDL) counts as half a deadlift set under the half-set rule (Pelland et al., 2025), so her deadlift volume rises from about 3 to 5 sets. Squat and bench rise faster because her log shows she recovers well on them.

**Reading the log at the end of the block (example outcome):**

| Lift | What happened | Conclusion for next block |
|---|---|---|
| Squat | Top-set estimated 1RM up 2.5 kg each week; week 4 still at RPE 8 | MRV not reached; start next block at 7, finish at 10–11 |
| Bench press | Benchmark 50 kg × 6 felt RPE 7 in week 3 but RPE 8 in week 4; shoulder ache | Week 4 was at or over MRV; next block peaks at ~12 sets |
| Deadlift | Flat estimated 1RM all block | Possibly below MEV; add one more heavy session or 1–2 sets |

This is how the landmarks become personal: each block tests the estimates, and the next block uses the improved numbers.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the landmarks as fixed numbers from a table.** They are estimates for one lifter, one lift and one phase. Re-check them every block.
- **Starting every block at the most the lifter can handle.** That leaves no room to progress. Start near MEV.
- **Counting sets inconsistently.** If warm-ups count one week and not the next, or variations are sometimes counted, the landmarks mean nothing.
- **Using accumulation-block volumes in heavy blocks.** Heavier sets cost more recovery, so landmarks drop when intensity rises.
- **Ignoring squat–deadlift overlap.** Adding deadlift sets also tires the squat muscles; track lower-body work as a whole.
- **Chasing volume for strength.** Strength gains flatten early as sets rise. Extra sets beyond MAV mostly add fatigue.
- **Not adjusting MRV for life.** A stressful month, poor sleep or a diet lowers what the lifter can recover from.

## Key takeaways

- MV, MEV, MAV and MRV are a coaching framework (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith), useful for planning but not measured thresholds.
- Research supports the shape: more weekly sets give more strength with diminishing returns, which are stronger for strength than for muscle size.
- Strength can be maintained with very little volume if loads stay heavy, and a little volume still builds some strength.
- Moderate volume often beats the highest tolerable volume for strength, but some lifters benefit from more; individualize from the log.
- Count hard sets per lift consistently; count indirect work as about half a set.
- Ramp sets across a mesocycle from MEV toward MRV, then deload to about MV, and re-estimate every block.
