## In one minute

**Undulating periodization** changes the training emphasis often: from session to session in **daily undulating periodization (DUP)**, or from week to week in **weekly undulating periodization (WUP)**. A typical DUP week gives each lift a heavy day, a medium or volume day and a light day, so the lifter practises several rep ranges and handles heavy loads every week. A volume-equated meta-analysis found a small strength advantage for undulating over linear models in trained people, but an earlier meta-analysis found no difference, and the studies are short. Undulating models fit lifters who train each lift two to three times a week, as long as heavy, medium and light days are genuinely different.

## What undulating periodization is

In linear periodization the emphasis changes every few weeks. In undulating periodization it changes **much more often**, so several qualities are trained in the same week or the same few weeks.

| Model | What changes | How often | Example for squat |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Daily undulating (DUP)** | Reps, load and sets | Every session | Mon 4 × 8, Wed 6 × 2 fast, Fri heavy triples |
| **Weekly undulating (WUP)** | The whole week's emphasis | Every week | Week 1 sets of 8, week 2 sets of 5, week 3 sets of 3, repeat heavier |
| **Flexible DUP** | Same planned sessions as DUP | Lifter chooses the order on the day | Picks the heavy day when feeling fresh |

Undulating plans are sometimes called **nonlinear periodization**. Over a longer cycle they can still move toward a peak, for example by making each round of heavy, medium and light days a little heavier, and then switching to a peaking block before a meet.

:::tip[In plain words]
Undulating means the workout changes often. In the daily version, each session is different: one heavy, one lighter with more reps, one quick and easy. In the weekly version, each week has its own theme. Either way, you practise heavy and lighter work all the time instead of one at a time.
:::

## Why it might work

- **Frequent heavy practice.** <Evidence level="practice" /> A heavy day every week keeps the skill of handling near-maximal loads sharp, which matters for a strength sport ([Part 1 · Chapter 3](/lets-git-it/foundations/03-core-training-principles/)).
- **Several qualities kept at once.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Because volume work and heavy work happen in the same week, neither has weeks to fade. This addresses one of the main weaknesses of long linear phases.
- **Variety.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> The meta-analysis that found no linear-versus-undulating difference still suggested that novelty or variety in training may be important for further strength gains (Harries et al., 2015).
- **Spreading fatigue.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Only one session per lift each week is truly hard, so the other sessions add practice and volume without stacking fatigue.
- **The true mechanism is unknown.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> Because periodized training increased strength but not muscle growth more than non-periodized training in volume-equated studies, Moesgaard et al. (2022) speculated that the strength benefit may come from neural adaptations rather than extra muscle.

:::tip[In plain words]
Changing the workout often lets you lift heavy every week while also doing enough lighter work to build muscle. Only one day per lift is really hard, so you don't get too tired. Nobody knows yet exactly why it might work a bit better.
:::

## What the evidence says

### Meta-analyses

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **No difference vs. linear:** 17 studies, 510 participants, no difference in upper- or lower-body strength between linear and undulating periodization (Harries et al., 2015).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **A small advantage, mainly in trained people:** in volume-equated studies, undulating periodization produced larger 1RM gains than linear (effect size 0.31). The advantage appeared in trained participants (effect size 0.61; the confidence interval ran from 0.00 to 1.22, so the true effect could be anywhere from zero to large) and not in untrained participants (effect size 0.06) (Moesgaard et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Undulating programs were also linked to larger strength gains in a meta-analysis of periodized vs. non-periodized training (Williams et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Muscle growth:** no difference between linear and daily undulating periodization across 13 studies (Grgic et al., 2017).

### Daily undulating studies

| Study | Who | Length | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rhea et al. (2002) | 20 men | 12 weeks | DUP (8, 6, 4 RM across the week) beat linear for strength |
| Prestes et al. (2009) | 40 men, ≥1 year of training | 12 weeks | DUP had larger percentage gains; group difference not statistically significant |
| Miranda et al. (2011) | 20 trained men | 12 weeks | No significant difference; effect sizes favoured DUP |
| Simão et al. (2012) | 30 untrained men (including a control group) | 12 weeks | Undulating (every two weeks, then daily) gave higher bench press and curl 1RM |
| Kok et al. (2009) | 20 untrained women | 9 weeks | Linear and undulating equally effective |

### Studies in powerlifters and powerlifting-trained men

These are the most relevant studies for this manual. All are small and short, so each is **emerging** evidence.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Order of the days.** In 18 male college powerlifters training squat, bench press and deadlift 3 days a week for 6 weeks, a week ordered **hypertrophy → power → strength** (heavy day last) beat **hypertrophy → strength → power** for bench press 1RM. Effect sizes also favoured it for the squat; deadlift 1RM did not differ. On the strength day lifters did as many reps as possible at a set percentage, and the hypertrophy–power–strength group completed more total volume in the squat and bench press (Zourdos et al., 2016). Putting the heavy day after a light day may let lifters do more good work on it.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Flexible order works as well.** In 25 resistance-trained men who met minimum strength standards (squat 125%, bench press 100% and deadlift 150% of body mass), letting lifters choose the order of their DUP sessions gave similar gains to a fixed order over 9 weeks. Powerlifting total rose by about 37 kg (flexible) and 40 kg (fixed) (Colquhoun et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **With volume matched, rep range made little difference.** In 16 trained men doing only squat and bench press 3 days a week for 8 weeks, a high-rep DUP (12, 10, 8 reps) and a low-rep DUP (6, 4, 2 reps) with matched volume gave similar strength and muscle-size gains. The authors concluded that training volume was a major driver of the adaptations (Klemp et al., 2016).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **RPE or percentages both work inside DUP.** In 21 trained men squatting and bench pressing 3 days a week in a daily undulating format for 8 weeks, RPE-based and percentage-based loading both increased strength, with a possible small advantage for RPE (Helms et al., 2018).

### Weekly undulating studies

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **No difference:** in 28 recreationally trained men and women over 9 weeks, linear, daily undulating and weekly undulating gave similar strength gains (Buford et al., 2007).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Worse than linear:** in 42 recreationally active men (14 linear, 14 weekly undulating and 14 controls) over 12 weeks, the linear group was stronger than the weekly undulating group by the end (10RM tests). The authors linked this to more muscle soreness and fatigue in the weekly undulating group (Apel et al., 2011).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Better than block:** in 17 recreationally trained women over 10 weeks, weekly undulating increased lower-body strength more than block periodization (about 28% vs. 15%) and increased thigh muscle size (Bartolomei et al., 2015).

### How to read this honestly

<Evidence level="moderate" /> Undulating periodization works at least as well as linear periodization, and there may be a small advantage for trained lifters. The data on weekly undulating models are thin and contradictory. <Evidence level="practice" /> For powerlifters, the more useful lessons from the small powerlifting studies are practical: keep enough total volume, put the heavy day where the lifter is freshest, and allow some flexibility when life gets in the way.

:::tip[In plain words]
Changing the workout every session works at least as well as a step-by-step plan, and may help experienced lifters a little more. Studies in powerlifters suggest doing the heavy day after an easy day, and show you can swap the order of days when you need to. The weekly version has much less research behind it.
:::

## Setting up heavy, medium and light days

### Define the days

<Evidence level="practice" /> A useful template, based on common coaching practice:

| Day type | Reps per set | Intensity | Effort | Sets | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Heavy** | 1–4 | ~82–92% 1RM | RPE 8–9 | 3–5 | Heavy-load skill, maximal strength |
| **Medium** | 4–6 | ~75–83% 1RM | RPE 7–8 | 3–5 | Strength with moderate fatigue |
| **Volume** | 6–10 | ~65–75% 1RM | RPE 6–8 | 3–5 | Muscle, work capacity, technique practice |
| **Light** | 2–3 | ~60–72% 1RM | RPE 5–6, bar moving fast | 4–8 | Practice and speed with little fatigue |

"Light" can mean two different things. A **volume** day is light in load but still demanding. A **light** day is easy overall. Know which one you are programming.

### Rules for arranging them

1. **Make the days genuinely different.** <Evidence level="practice" /> If every session drifts to RPE 8–9, the result is not undulating; it is just hard. Keep at least a clear gap in load or effort between days.
2. **Put the heavy day after the easiest day.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> This is what the powerlifting study above found works better (Zourdos et al., 2016).
3. **Space hard days for the same lift.** <Evidence level="practice" /> One review suggested allowing about 72 hours between a high-volume session and a heavy strength–power session for the same muscles (Hartmann et al., 2015). In a Monday–Wednesday–Friday week, a Monday volume day and a Friday heavy day fit this. If Wednesday is also a hard deadlift day, as in Program 1 below, expect some leg fatigue on Friday.
4. **Offset the lifts** in 4-day plans, so not every lift is heavy on the same day.
5. **Treat the deadlift differently.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Most lifters do best with one hard deadlift session and one light session or variation per week, because heavy deadlifts are costly and squats already train the same muscles.
6. **Progress each day type separately.** Add a little load each week to each day type while its RPE stays on target. If a day's RPE runs a full point high, hold that load.
7. **Deload every 4–6 weeks.** Cut sets roughly in half for a week, keeping the movements.
8. **Allow flexibility.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> If the lifter arrives tired, swapping the heavy and light days within the week does not appear to cost results (Colquhoun et al., 2017; McNamara & Stearne, 2010).

:::tip[In plain words]
Have one heavy day, one day with more reps, and one easy day for each lift. Make sure they really feel different. Put the heavy day after the easy day, give each lift a couple of days between hard sessions, and swap days around if you show up tired.
:::

## Sample programs

Loads use the RPE chart from [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg. Warm-ups are not shown. If an RPE target is reached at a different weight than listed, follow the RPE.

### Program 1: DUP, 3 days per week (volume → light → heavy)

For the intermediate lifter in this manual: squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. A 4-week wave, then repeat with loads updated from week 3.

**Week 1**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon (volume) | Squat | 4 × 8 | RPE 7 (~127.5 kg) |
| Mon (volume) | Bench press | 4 × 8 | RPE 7 (~85 kg) |
| Mon (volume) | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| Mon (volume) | Barbell row | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| Wed (light) | Squat | 6 × 2 | 70% (125 kg), fast |
| Wed (light) | Bench press | 6 × 3 | 72.5% (87.5 kg), fast |
| Wed (light) | Deadlift | 4 × 3 | RPE 7 (~175 kg) |
| Fri (heavy) | Squat | 1 × 2, then 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~160 kg), then 85% (152.5 kg) |
| Fri (heavy) | Bench press | 1 × 2, then 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~107.5 kg), then 85% (102.5 kg) |
| Fri (heavy) | Back extension | 3 × 10 | RPE 7 |

**The 4-week wave**

| Week | Mon (volume) squat / bench | Wed (light) squat / bench | Wed deadlift | Fri (heavy) squat / bench |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 × 8 @ RPE 7 (~127.5 / 85 kg) | 6 × 2 @ 70% (125 kg) / 6 × 3 @ 72.5% (87.5 kg) | 4 × 3 @ RPE 7 (~175 kg) | Double @ RPE 8 (~160 / 107.5 kg), 3 × 3 @ 85% (152.5 / 102.5 kg) |
| 2 | 4 × 6 @ RPE 7.5 (~140 / 92.5 kg) | 6 × 2 @ 72.5% (130 kg) / 6 × 3 @ 75% (90 kg) | 4 × 3 @ RPE 7.5 (~177.5 kg) | Single @ RPE 8 (~165 / 110 kg), 3 × 3 @ 85% (152.5 / 102.5 kg) |
| 3 | 4 × 5 @ RPE 8 (~145 / 97.5 kg) | 6 × 2 @ 75% (135 kg) / 6 × 3 @ 77.5% (92.5 kg) | 3 × 2 @ RPE 8 (~187.5 kg) | Single @ RPE 8.5 (~170 / 112.5 kg), 3 × 2 @ 87.5% (157.5 / 105 kg) |
| 4 | Deload: 2 × 5 @ 70% (125 / 85 kg) | 4 × 2 @ 65% (117.5 / 77.5 kg) | 2 × 3 @ 70% (147.5 kg) | 2 × 2 @ 80% (145 / 95 kg) |

**How it is built and why:** the order follows the powerlifter study that found **volume → light → heavy** worked better than putting the heavy day in the middle (Zourdos et al., 2016). Monday's volume day and Friday's heavy day are four days apart. Wednesday is light for squat and bench, but it is the hard deadlift day, so expect some leg fatigue on Friday. Romanian deadlifts are done on Monday. Across the wave, volume-day reps fall from 8 to 5 while RPE rises, so the whole week gets a little heavier: undulation inside the week, a gentle linear trend across weeks. When the next wave starts, use week 3's top single to update the training max.

### Program 2: DUP, 4 days per week (lifts offset)

Same lifter. Each lift gets its heavy, medium or volume, and light days on **different** days, so no session is heavy for everything.

**Which day is which**

| Lift | Day 1 (Mon) | Day 2 (Wed) | Day 3 (Thu) | Day 4 (Sat) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Squat | **Heavy** | — | Volume | Light (pause squat) |
| Bench press | Medium | — | **Heavy** | Volume |
| Deadlift | — | **Heavy** | — | Light (variation) |

**Week 1**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 3 | RPE 7.5–8 (~152.5–155 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 5 | RPE 7.5 (~95 kg) |
| 1 | Chin-up | 3 × 8 | RPE 8 |
| 2 | Deadlift | 3 × 3 | RPE 7.5–8 (~177.5–180 kg) |
| 2 | Overhead press | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| 2 | Barbell row | 4 × 8 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Squat | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~137.5 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press | 5 × 3 | RPE 7.5–8 (~102.5 kg) |
| 3 | Leg curl | 3 × 12 | RPE 8 |
| 4 | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × 4 | RPE 6.5 |
| 4 | Pause deadlift (below knee) | 3 × 4 | RPE 6.5 |
| 4 | Bench press | 4 × 8 | RPE 7 (~85 kg) |
| 4 | Triceps pushdown | 3 × 12 | RPE 8 |

A typical schedule is Monday (1), Wednesday (2), Thursday (3), Saturday (4).

**Progression over 6 weeks**

| Week | Heavy days | Medium and volume days | Light days |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | As above | As above | As above |
| 2 | Same sets × reps, +2.5 kg (bench) or +2.5–5 kg (squat, deadlift) if week 1 stayed ≤ RPE 8 | +2.5 kg | Same |
| 3 | Drop 1 rep (e.g. 4 × 2), +2.5 kg | +2.5 kg | Same |
| 4 | Same as week 3, +2.5 kg if RPE allows | Drop 1–2 reps, +2.5 kg | Same |
| 5 | Work up to a single at RPE 8–8.5, then 2 × 2 at about 90% of it | Same as week 4 | Same |
| 6 | Deload: half the sets, about 10% lighter | Half the sets | Half the sets |

For this lifter, the heavy-day loads for weeks 1–4 run about: squat 152.5–155 → 155–160 → 157.5–162.5 → 160–165 kg; bench press 102.5 → 105 → 107.5 → 110 kg; deadlift 177.5–180 → 180–185 → 182.5–187.5 → 185–190 kg. On the chart, the week-4 doubles at these loads are at most about RPE 9. If a heavy set goes above RPE 9, hold that load.

**How it is built and why:** offsetting the lifts lets each one be trained 2–3 times a week while each session has only **one heavy lift**. The squat's heavy day (Monday) and volume day (Thursday) are three days apart, and the bench's heavy day (Thursday) comes three days after its medium day (Monday). The heavy squat (Monday) and heavy deadlift (Wednesday) days are two days apart, so they are never back to back. The deadlift gets one heavy day and one light variation, which keeps total lower-back stress manageable alongside two squat sessions. This is a common shape for intermediate powerlifters who can train four days a week.

### Program 3: Weekly undulating, 3 days per week (masters lifter)

For a 52-year-old male masters lifter: squat 150 kg, bench press 95 kg, deadlift 180 kg. Each week has **one theme**, and the three themes rotate. After each 3-week wave, the RPE targets rise by half a point. Nine weeks of training, then a test or deload week.

**Main lifts by week**

| Week | Theme | Squat (Mon) | Bench press (Mon, Fri) | Deadlift (Wed) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Volume | 4 × 8 @ RPE 7 (~105 kg) | 4 × 8 @ RPE 7 (~67.5 kg) | 3 × 6 @ RPE 7 (~137.5 kg) |
| 2 | Strength | 4 × 5 @ RPE 7 (~117.5 kg) | 4 × 5 @ RPE 7 (~75 kg) | 3 × 4 @ RPE 7 (~145 kg) |
| 3 | Heavy | 4 × 3 @ RPE 7.5 (~127.5 kg) | 4 × 3 @ RPE 7.5 (~80 kg) | 2 × 3 @ RPE 7.5 (~152.5 kg) |
| 4 | Volume | 4 × 8 @ RPE 7.5 (~107.5 kg) | 4 × 8 @ RPE 7.5 (~67.5 kg) | 3 × 6 @ RPE 7.5 (~140 kg) |
| 5 | Strength | 4 × 5 @ RPE 7.5 (~120 kg) | 4 × 5 @ RPE 7.5 (~75 kg) | 3 × 4 @ RPE 7.5 (~147.5 kg) |
| 6 | Heavy | 4 × 3 @ RPE 8 (~130 kg) | 4 × 3 @ RPE 8 (~82.5 kg) | 2 × 3 @ RPE 8 (~155 kg) |
| 7 | Volume | 4 × 8 @ RPE 8 (~110 kg) | 4 × 8 @ RPE 8 (~70 kg) | 3 × 6 @ RPE 8 (~142.5 kg) |
| 8 | Strength | 4 × 5 @ RPE 8 (~122.5 kg) | 4 × 5 @ RPE 8 (~77.5 kg) | 3 × 4 @ RPE 8 (~150 kg) |
| 9 | Heavy | 4 × 3 @ RPE 8.5 (~132.5 kg) | 4 × 3 @ RPE 8.5 (~82.5 kg) | 2 × 3 @ RPE 8.5 (~157.5 kg) |
| 10 | Test or deload | Single at RPE 8–9, or half the sets | Same | Same |

**The rest of each week:**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat, bench press | As in the table | — |
| Mon | Dumbbell row | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| Wed | Deadlift | As in the table | — |
| Wed | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| Wed | Leg press | 3 × 10 | RPE 7 |
| Fri | Squat | 2 sets of the week's reps | About 90% of Monday's load |
| Fri | Bench press | As in the table | Same as Monday |
| Fri | Lat pulldown | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |

**How it is built and why:** a weekly theme keeps every session in a week similar, which some lifters find easier to follow and recover from than three different sessions. It also means the heavy weeks come only one week in three, which can suit a lifter whose joints prefer fewer heavy exposures; this is coaching judgement, not a tested masters-specific finding. The evidence for weekly undulating models is limited and mixed (Buford et al., 2007; Apel et al., 2011; Bartolomei et al., 2015). **If soreness or fatigue builds up** (as in Apel et al., 2011), drop one set from the volume weeks. Bench press listed loads repeat in some weeks because 2.5 kg is a large step for a 95 kg bench; with smaller plates, add 1–1.25 kg instead.

## Common mistakes

- **Every day becomes a heavy day.** Undulating only works if light and volume days are truly easier. Hold the RPE targets.
- **Too many day types.** Three per lift (heavy, medium or volume, light) is plenty. Five different rep schemes per week just complicates tracking.
- **Heavy squat and heavy deadlift on back-to-back days.** Space hard lower-body sessions.
- **Not progressing each day type.** Track heavy, medium and light days separately; each needs its own small increases.
- **Assuming DUP is proven superior.** The edge over linear models is small and uncertain. Choose it because it fits the lifter's schedule and preferences.
- **Never peaking.** DUP builds strength well, but before a meet switch to a peaking block (Part 4, Chapter 2: Building a peaking block) or a short realization phase ([Chapter 6](/lets-git-it/building-programs/06-block-periodization/)).

## Key takeaways

- Daily undulating periodization changes reps and load each session; weekly undulating changes them each week.
- A volume-equated meta-analysis found a small strength advantage for undulating over linear models, mainly in trained participants; another meta-analysis found no difference. Both models work.
- Small studies in powerlifters suggest placing the heavy day after a light day, that a flexible session order works as well as a fixed one, and that total volume matters more than the exact rep range.
- Weekly undulating evidence is limited and mixed.
- Build each lift's week from a heavy, a medium or volume, and a light day that are genuinely different, offset the lifts, and progress each day type separately.
