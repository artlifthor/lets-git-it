## In one minute

A **mesocycle** is a training block of about 4–6 weeks with one main goal, usually ending in a lighter deload week. Building one is a sequence of decisions: assess the lifter, set the goal, choose frequency, set weekly volume, pick exercises, set intensity and progression, add accessories, plan the deload, and write rules for adjusting on the fly. Each decision draws on an earlier chapter of this manual, and each has a short checklist below. Periodized, planned training reliably beats unplanned training, but no single model is clearly best, so the aim is a **sound, consistent plan you can adjust**, not a perfect one. The chapter ends with a complete 5-week program for a 4-day lifter and a shorter 3-day version.

## The terms

| Term | Length | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Microcycle** | Usually 1 week | Week 3 of the block |
| **Mesocycle** (block) | Usually 4–6 weeks | A 5-week strength block: 4 loading weeks + 1 deload |
| **Macrocycle** | Several months to a year | Off-season to meet: hypertrophy block → strength block → peaking block |

<Evidence level="practice" /> The mesocycle is the most useful planning unit for a coach. It is long enough for a lifter to adapt and show progress, and short enough to adjust after (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith). The 4–6 week length is a coaching convention, not a research-derived number.

:::tip[In plain words]
A mesocycle is a block of training, usually four to six weeks, built around one goal, with an easier week at the end. You plan one block at a time, run it, look at the results, and plan the next.
:::

## Why plan a block at all?

- <Evidence level="strong" /> Two meta-analyses found larger 1RM gains with periodized (planned, varied) training than with non-periodized training, including when total volume was equal (Williams et al., 2017; Moesgaard et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> In volume-matched studies, undulating periodization gave slightly larger 1RM gains than linear, mainly in trained lifters. There were too few studies to compare block periodization (Moesgaard et al., 2022). See [Chapter 3: Periodization overview](/lets-git-it/building-programs/03-periodization-overview/), [Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/building-programs/04-linear-periodization/), [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/building-programs/05-undulating-periodization/) and [Chapter 6](/lets-git-it/building-programs/06-block-periodization/).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Programs that adjust load to the lifter's day, such as RPE targets, reliably improve 1RM strength (Larsen et al., 2021), and RPE-based loading did at least as well as percentage loading in one trial of trained lifters (Helms et al., 2018).

**The practical message:** have a plan, make it progressive, and build in ways to adjust it. Which model you use matters less than running a sensible one consistently.

## Step 1: Assess the lifter

Start from where the lifter actually is, not where you wish they were. Full detail is in [Chapter 1: Assessing the lifter](/lets-git-it/building-programs/01-assessing-the-lifter/).

<Evidence level="practice" /> Gather:

- **Current strength:** estimated 1RMs from the last 2–4 weeks of training, using the RPE chart in [Part 1, Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/). A recent heavy set is better than an old meet result.
- **What worked last block:** weekly sets per lift, frequency, and how each lift responded. Training logs make this possible (see [Part 1, Chapter 12: Tracking training](/lets-git-it/foundations/12-tracking-training/)).
- **Weak points:** where each lift slows down or breaks position (on video).
- **Constraints:** days per week, session length, equipment, injuries or pain, sleep, work and life stress.
- **Calendar:** the next meet date, or none.

**Decision checklist**

- [ ] I have a current estimated 1RM for each competition lift.
- [ ] I know the weekly hard sets per lift from the last block and whether progress came from it.
- [ ] I have named one main weak point per lift (or decided there isn't a clear one).
- [ ] I know how many days and minutes the lifter can really train.
- [ ] I know of any pain or injury that limits exercise choice (and referred on if needed).

:::tip[In plain words]
Before you plan, find out where the lifter is now: how strong they are this month, what training worked last time, where their lifts get stuck, and how much time and energy they actually have.
:::

## Step 2: Set the block's goal and length

<Evidence level="practice" /> Pick **one main goal** for the block, based on where the lifter is in the year (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith). Concentrating each block on a few qualities and sequencing the blocks is the core idea of block periodization (Issurin, 2010):

| Time to meet (or phase) | Block goal | Typical reps on competition lifts | Typical RPE |
|---|---|---|---|
| No meet in sight, or 16+ weeks | Hypertrophy / work capacity ([Chapter 10](/lets-git-it/building-programs/10-hypertrophy-for-powerlifters/)) | 5–10 | 6–8 |
| About 8–16 weeks | Strength (heavier, more specific) | 2–6 | 7–9 |
| Last 3–6 weeks | Peaking (Part 4, Chapter 2) | 1–3 | 8–9.5 |

**Length.** 4–6 weeks is typical: 3–5 loading weeks plus a deload. Shorter blocks suit lifters who fatigue quickly or have a meet soon; longer blocks suit lifters who progress steadily and recover well.

**Decision checklist**

- [ ] The block has one main goal written in a sentence ("Build squat and bench strength in the 3–5 rep range").
- [ ] The goal fits the time to the next meet.
- [ ] The length (4–6 weeks) and the deload week are on the calendar.
- [ ] I know what the next block will be, so this one sets it up.

:::tip[In plain words]
Give each block one job, such as "build muscle" or "get stronger in sets of three to five". The job depends on how far away the next competition is. Four to six weeks per block, with an easy week at the end, is a good default.
:::

## Step 3: Choose frequency and the weekly split

See [Chapter 9: Training frequency](/lets-git-it/building-programs/09-training-frequency/).

- <Evidence level="strong" /> Strength gains rise with training frequency, with diminishing returns (Pelland et al., 2025).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> In practice, frequency is mostly a way to spread the weekly volume, so each session has fewer, fresher sets and more practice of the lifts.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Typical frequencies for intermediate lifters: **bench 3–4×, squat 2–3×, deadlift 1–2× per week**, counting close variations.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Split rules:

1. Put the **heaviest** session for each lift on a day when the lifter is usually fresh.
2. Leave **48 hours or more** between heavy sessions for the same lift.
3. Pair a **lower-body lift with a bench variation** each day so sessions stay balanced.
4. Put heavy deadlifts **at least two days** away from heavy squats when possible.

**Decision checklist**

- [ ] Each lift is trained at least twice per week (deadlift may be once plus a variation).
- [ ] Heavy sessions for the same lift are 48+ hours apart.
- [ ] Sessions fit the lifter's time (usually 60–100 minutes).

:::tip[In plain words]
Decide how many days you'll train and spread each lift across them: bench most often, squat two or three times, deadlift once or twice. Keep hard days for the same lift at least two days apart.
:::

## Step 4: Set weekly volume per lift

See [Chapter 8: Volume landmarks](/lets-git-it/building-programs/08-volume-landmarks/).

- <Evidence level="strong" /> More weekly sets produce more strength and muscle, with diminishing returns that flatten more for strength than for size (Pelland et al., 2025).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Start the block at a volume the lifter **is known to recover from** (often similar to the productive weeks of the last block) and let it rise slightly across the loading weeks, staying below their maximum recoverable volume (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith).

<Evidence level="practice" /> Starting points for an intermediate lifter, counting competition lifts and close variations (hard sets per week):

| Lift | Hypertrophy block | Strength block | Peaking block |
|---|---|---|---|
| Squat | 10–15 | 8–12 | 5–8 |
| Bench press | 12–18 | 12–16 | 6–10 |
| Deadlift | 6–10 | 5–8 | 3–5 |

**Decision checklist**

- [ ] Weekly hard sets per lift are written down for week 1.
- [ ] Week 1 volume is one the lifter has recovered from before.
- [ ] I've planned whether volume rises, stays flat, or falls across the block (and why).

:::tip[In plain words]
Count the hard sets for each lift per week. Start at an amount you know you can recover from, and only add a little across the block. More isn't always better; recoverable is better.
:::

## Step 5: Choose the exercises

See [Part 1, Chapter 8: Exercise selection](/lets-git-it/foundations/08-exercise-selection/).

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Planned variation can help; excessive random variation may hold progress back (Kassiano et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> For each lift: the **competition lift** plus **one close variation** chosen for the weak point. Keep the same exercises for the whole block so they can progress.

**Decision checklist**

- [ ] Each competition lift appears at least once per week, with competition technique and commands.
- [ ] Each variation has a written reason (weak point, technique, or injury).
- [ ] Most of each lift's weekly sets come from the competition lift and close variations.
- [ ] Exercises stay the same for the whole block.

:::tip[In plain words]
Do each competition lift every week and add one close version of it that targets where you get stuck. Keep the same exercises for the whole block so you can see them improve.
:::

## Step 6: Set intensity, effort and progression

See [Part 1, Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/) and [Part 1, Chapter 6](/lets-git-it/foundations/06-progressive-overload/).

- <Evidence level="strong" /> Heavier loads build 1RM strength better than light loads, with a non-significant trend favouring heavy (≤8RM) over moderate loads (Lopez et al., 2021). Stopping short of failure gives similar strength gains to training to failure (Grgic et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> A simple, robust progression for a strength block: **reps per set go down and RPE goes up by about 0.5 per week**, so load rises steadily. Prescribe RPE and give an estimated load from the RPE chart, so the plan bends on good and bad days (Tuchscherer; Helms et al., 2018).

Example progression for a main competition-lift slot:

| Week | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sets × reps | 4 × 5 | 4 × 4 | 5 × 3 | 1 × 2 + 3 × 3 | 2 × 3 |
| Target | RPE 7 | RPE 7.5 | RPE 8 | RPE 8.5 / RPE 8 | ~70% (deload) |

**Decision checklist**

- [ ] Every competition-lift slot has sets × reps, a target RPE and an estimated load.
- [ ] Week 1 starts easy enough (RPE 6–7) to leave room to progress.
- [ ] The hardest week is the last loading week, not the first.
- [ ] Nothing on the competition lifts is planned above RPE 9 in a non-peaking block.

:::tip[In plain words]
Start the block feeling fairly easy and make it a little harder each week: fewer reps, more weight, a bit closer to your limit. Write both a target effort and a rough weight, so the lifter can adjust on the day.
:::

## Step 7: Add accessories

See [Chapter 13: Programming accessories](/lets-git-it/building-programs/13-programming-accessories/).

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Strength improves most in the exercises done first in a session (Nunes et al., 2021), so accessories go after the main work.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> In a strength block, 2–3 accessories per session, 2–4 sets each, at RPE 8–9, progressed by double progression. Choose each by its job: weak point, muscle, balance, or joint health.

**Decision checklist**

- [ ] Every accessory has a written job.
- [ ] No hard leg or lower-back accessory is the day before a heavy squat or deadlift session.
- [ ] Accessories fit inside the session time without crowding the main work.

:::tip[In plain words]
Add two or three helper exercises per session after the main lifts. Each one needs a reason to be there. Keep them away from the day before a heavy squat or deadlift.
:::

## Step 8: Plan the deload

See [Chapter 11: Deloads and fatigue management](/lets-git-it/building-programs/11-deloads-fatigue-management/).

- <Evidence level="practice" /> An international expert consensus defined a deload as **a period of reduced training stress designed to reduce physical and mental fatigue, promote recovery and prepare for the next block of training** (Bell et al., 2023). It is an expert-opinion study, not an experiment.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> In a survey of 246 competitive strength and physique athletes, deloads lasted about a week and came about every 5–6 weeks. Athletes usually cut volume, load and effort but kept the same frequency and exercises (Rogerson et al., 2024).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 39 trained young adults on a 9-week program, a group that **stopped training completely** for one week in the middle gained less lower-body strength than a group that kept training, with no difference in muscle growth (Coleman et al., 2024). This supports a *reduced* week rather than a full week off, at least in a short program.

<Evidence level="practice" /> A default deload (a volume deload, see Chapter 11): **same days and exercises, about half the sets, loads the same or up to ~10% lighter, RPE 6–7 at most.** Cut the load further (a combined deload) after a very high-volume block or when joints ache.

**Decision checklist**

- [ ] The deload week is planned in advance (week 4, 5 or 6).
- [ ] Volume drops by about half; loads stay the same or drop up to ~10% (more for a combined deload); nothing above RPE 7.
- [ ] Frequency and exercises stay the same.
- [ ] I've noted the warning signs that would bring the deload forward (see Step 9).

:::tip[In plain words]
Plan an easier week at the end of the block: same exercises and days, about half the sets, similar or slightly lighter weights, nothing hard. It lets tiredness fade so you start the next block fresh. Easier training works better than stopping completely.
:::

## Step 9: Write the adjustment rules

Plans meet real life. Decide in advance what the lifter does when a session doesn't go to plan, so changes are calm and consistent.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Default rules (Tuchscherer; Helms et al., 2016):

| What happens | What to do |
|---|---|
| A set comes in **1 RPE or more above target** | Drop the load 5% for the remaining sets |
| A set comes in **1 RPE or more below target** | Add 2.5–5 kg for the remaining sets |
| The **top set** is too heavy for the target RPE | Back-off sets come from the load actually used, not the plan |
| Two sessions in a row miss targets for the **same lift** | Repeat that week's loads next week instead of progressing |
| Several lifts miss targets for a **week**, with poor sleep, aches, or low motivation | Bring the deload forward |
| Sharp or worsening pain | Stop that exercise; change it or seek a qualified health professional |

**Decision checklist**

- [ ] The lifter knows the rules before week 1.
- [ ] The log has columns for planned load, actual load and actual RPE.

:::tip[In plain words]
Agree in advance what happens if a session feels too hard or too easy: take a little weight off, add a little, repeat a week, or take the easy week early. Then nobody has to guess in the gym.
:::

## Step 10: Run, review, and plan the next block

<Evidence level="practice" /> At the end of the block, compare the lifter's **estimated 1RMs** from the last loading week with those from Step 1. For example, if the lifter squats 162.5 kg for 2 at RPE 8 (89.2% on the chart), their estimated 1RM is 162.5 ÷ 0.892 ≈ **182 kg**, up from 180 kg.

| Result | Next block |
|---|---|
| Estimated 1RMs up, recovery fine | Keep the structure; add a little volume or move to the next phase |
| Estimated 1RMs flat, recovery fine | Add volume or frequency for the stalled lift, or change its variation |
| Estimated 1RMs flat or down, lifter tired | Reduce volume; check sleep, stress and nutrition |
| One lift up, another flat | Shift sets from the improving lift to the stalled one |

**Decision checklist**

- [ ] I've compared start and end estimated 1RMs for each lift.
- [ ] I've written one thing to keep and one thing to change.
- [ ] The next block's goal follows from this block's result.

:::tip[In plain words]
At the end, check whether the lifter got stronger, using the heavy sets from the last hard week. Keep what worked, change one or two things that didn't, and plan the next block from there.
:::

## Sample programs

### Program 1: A complete 5-week strength block (4 days)

**The lifter (Step 1).** Intermediate man, about 83 kg, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg (recent estimates). Trains Monday, Wednesday, Friday, Saturday for about 75–90 minutes. Coming off a hypertrophy block (see Chapter 10, Program 1). Weak points: slow out of the hole in the squat, slows near lockout in the bench. No pain. Meet about 14 weeks away.

**The plan (Steps 2–8).**

- **Goal:** build strength in the 2–6 rep range; set up a later peaking block.
- **Length:** 4 loading weeks + 1 deload.
- **Frequency:** squat 2×, bench 4×, deadlift 1× plus Romanian deadlift.
- **Variations:** pause squat (out of the hole), close-grip bench (lockout), 2-second paused bench (more competition-style practice).
- **Progression:** reps down, RPE up by about 0.5 per week; deload in week 5.
- **Deload:** a combined deload (about half the sets and lighter loads, about 70–75% 1RM), because it follows the block's highest-volume weeks (see Step 8).
- **Loads:** estimated from the RPE chart in Part 1, Chapter 5 and rounded to 2.5 kg. If the RPE on the day doesn't match, follow Step 9. Variation and accessory loads are chosen in week 1 to hit the target RPE, then progressed.

**Weekly hard sets (competition lifts and close variations)**

| Lift | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4 | Week 5 (deload) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Squat | 8 | 8 | 9 | 7 | 4 |
| Bench press | 14 | 14 | 15 | 14 | 7 |
| Deadlift + RDL | 3 + 3 (≈4.5) | 3 + 3 (≈4.5) | 3 + 3 (≈4.5) | 3 + 3 (≈4.5) | 2 + 1 (≈2.5) |

The Romanian deadlift trains the deadlift muscles less directly, so it counts as half a deadlift set (the half-set rule, see [Chapter 8](/lets-git-it/building-programs/08-volume-landmarks/); Pelland et al., 2025).

**Day 1 (Monday): squat and bench volume**

| Exercise | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4 | Week 5 (deload) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Squat | 4 × 5 @ RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) | 4 × 4 @ RPE 7.5 (~147.5 kg) | 5 × 3 @ RPE 8 (~155 kg) | 1 × 2 @ RPE 8.5 (~162.5 kg), then 3 × 3 @ RPE 8 (~155 kg) | 2 × 3 @ ~70% (125 kg) |
| Bench press | 4 × 6 @ RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) | 4 × 5 @ RPE 7.5 (~95 kg) | 4 × 5 @ RPE 8 (~97.5 kg) | 4 × 4 @ RPE 8 (~100 kg) | 2 × 5 @ ~70% (85 kg) |
| Seated leg curl | 3 × 10–12 @ RPE 8–9 | 3 × 10–12 @ RPE 8–9 | 3 × 10–12 @ RPE 9 | 3 × 10–12 @ RPE 9 | 2 × 10 @ RPE 6–7 |
| Chest-supported row | 3 × 8–10 @ RPE 8 | 4 × 8–10 @ RPE 8 | 4 × 8–10 @ RPE 8–9 | 3 × 8–10 @ RPE 9 | 2 × 10 @ RPE 6–7 |

**Day 2 (Wednesday): deadlift and bench lockout**

| Exercise | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4 | Week 5 (deload) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deadlift | 3 × 4 @ RPE 7 (~170 kg) | 3 × 4 @ RPE 7.5 (~172.5 kg) | 3 × 3 @ RPE 8 (~180 kg) | 1 × 2 @ RPE 8.5 (~190 kg), then 2 × 3 @ RPE 8 (~180 kg) | 2 × 3 @ ~70% (147.5 kg) |
| Close-grip bench press | 3 × 8 @ RPE 7 | 3 × 7 @ RPE 7.5 | 4 × 6 @ RPE 8 | 3 × 6 @ RPE 8 | 1 × 6 @ RPE 6 |
| Bulgarian split squat | 3 × 8 each leg @ RPE 7 | 3 × 8 @ RPE 7.5 | 3 × 8 @ RPE 8 | 2 × 8 @ RPE 8 | 2 × 8 @ RPE 6 |
| Lat pulldown | 3 × 10–12 @ RPE 8 | 3 × 10–12 @ RPE 8–9 | 3 × 10–12 @ RPE 9 | 3 × 10–12 @ RPE 9 | 2 × 10 @ RPE 6–7 |

**Day 3 (Friday): pause squat and heavy bench**

| Exercise | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4 | Week 5 (deload) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pause squat (2 s) | 4 × 5 @ RPE 7 | 4 × 4 @ RPE 7.5 | 4 × 4 @ RPE 8 | 3 × 3 @ RPE 8 | 2 × 3 @ RPE 6 |
| Bench press (top set) | 1 × 3 @ RPE 7.5 (~102.5 kg) | 1 × 2 @ RPE 8 (~107.5 kg) | 1 × 1 @ RPE 8 (~110 kg) | 1 × 1 @ RPE 8.5 (~112.5 kg) | — |
| Bench press (back-off sets) | 3 × 4 @ RPE 7 (~97.5 kg) | 3 × 4 @ RPE 7.5 (~100 kg) | 3 × 3 @ RPE 8 (~102.5 kg) | 3 × 2 @ RPE 8 (~107.5 kg) | 2 × 2 @ ~75% (90 kg) |
| Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 @ RPE 7 | 3 × 8 @ RPE 7.5 | 3 × 6 @ RPE 8 | 3 × 6 @ RPE 8 | 1 × 6 @ RPE 6 |
| Overhead cable triceps extension | 3 × 12–15 @ RPE 9 | 3 × 12–15 @ RPE 9 | 4 × 12–15 @ RPE 9 | 3 × 12–15 @ RPE 9 | 2 × 12 @ RPE 7 |

**Day 4 (Saturday): bench practice and lower-body accessories**

| Exercise | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4 | Week 5 (deload) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Paused bench press (2 s) | 3 × 8 @ RPE 7 | 3 × 8 @ RPE 7.5 | 3 × 6 @ RPE 8 | 3 × 6 @ RPE 8 | 2 × 6 @ RPE 6 |
| Leg press | 3 × 10–12 @ RPE 8 | 3 × 10–12 @ RPE 8–9 | 3 × 10–12 @ RPE 9 | 2 × 10–12 @ RPE 9 | 2 × 10 @ RPE 6–7 |
| Back extension | 3 × 12–15 @ RPE 7–8 | 3 × 12–15 @ RPE 8 | 3 × 12–15 @ RPE 8 | 2 × 12–15 @ RPE 8 | 2 × 12 @ RPE 6 |
| Face pull | 2 × 15–20 @ RPE 8 | 2 × 15–20 @ RPE 8 | 3 × 15–20 @ RPE 8 | 2 × 15–20 @ RPE 8 | — |

**Why it is built this way.**

- **Specificity rises across the block:** reps fall from 5–6 to 1–3 on the competition lifts, and week 4 introduces heavy singles and doubles to set up the peak.
- **Fatigue is controlled:** most sets sit at RPE 7–8; only week 4 top sets reach RPE 8.5. Leg press and back extensions are on Saturday, two days before Monday's squat; Romanian deadlifts are on Friday, five days before the next deadlift on Wednesday.
- **Weak points have both a variation and an accessory:** pause squat + leg press and split squats for the squat; close-grip bench + triceps extensions for the bench.
- **Week 3 has the most volume and week 4 the most intensity**, then the deload drops both.
- **Week 4's top sets give the end-of-block estimated 1RMs** for Step 10.

### Program 2: The same block for a 3-day lifter

Same lifter, but only Monday, Wednesday and Friday, about 90 minutes each. To fit three days: drop Day 4, move the paused bench to Day 2 in place of the close-grip bench, and trim accessories to two per session. Weekly hard sets fall to about squat 8, bench 11, deadlift 3 + RDL 3, which is still enough to progress for most intermediates, though more slowly for the bench.

**Day 2 (Wednesday)**

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Exercise | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4 | Week 5 (deload) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pause squat (2 s) | 4 × 5 @ RPE 7 | 4 × 4 @ RPE 7.5 | 4 × 4 @ RPE 8 | 3 × 3 @ RPE 8 | 2 × 3 @ RPE 6 |
| Bench press (top set) | 1 × 3 @ RPE 7.5 (~102.5 kg) | 1 × 2 @ RPE 8 (~107.5 kg) | 1 × 1 @ RPE 8 (~110 kg) | 1 × 1 @ RPE 8.5 (~112.5 kg) | — |
| Bench press (back-off sets) | 3 × 4 @ RPE 7 (~97.5 kg) | 3 × 4 @ RPE 7.5 (~100 kg) | 3 × 3 @ RPE 8 (~102.5 kg) | 3 × 2 @ RPE 8 (~107.5 kg) | 2 × 2 @ ~75% (90 kg) |
| Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 @ RPE 7 | 3 × 8 @ RPE 7.5 | 3 × 6 @ RPE 8 | 3 × 6 @ RPE 8 | 1 × 6 @ RPE 6 |

**Why it is built this way.** The competition lifts keep exactly the same progression as the 4-day plan, so the 3-day lifter still gets the heavy, specific work. What is lost is mainly bench volume and accessory work. Romanian deadlifts on Friday sit five days before the next deadlift on Wednesday. If sessions run long, cut the last accessory first, never the competition lifts.

## Common mistakes

- **Planning from an old max.** Start from current estimated 1RMs, not last year's meet.
- **Starting too hard.** If week 1 is RPE 8–9, there is nowhere to go. Start at RPE 6–7.
- **Changing everything at once between blocks.** Change one or two things so you can tell what worked.
- **No deload, or a deload of complete rest by default.** Plan a reduced week; rest fully only for a specific reason.
- **No rules for bad days.** Decide the adjustments in advance.
- **Treating the plan as fixed.** The plan is a starting point; RPE and the log tell you how to adjust it.
- **Accessories crowding out the main work.** Cut accessories first when time or energy is short.

## Key takeaways

- A mesocycle is a 4–6 week block with one goal, ending in a deload.
- Build it in order: assess, set the goal, frequency, volume, exercises, intensity and progression, accessories, deload, adjustment rules, review.
- Planned, progressive training beats unplanned training; the specific model matters less than consistency.
- Start week 1 at RPE 6–7 and progress by about 0.5 RPE per week, with fewer reps and more load.
- Use a reduced deload week (about half the sets, similar or slightly lighter loads, RPE 7 at most), not complete rest, as the default; cut the load further after a very high-volume block.
- Judge the block by comparing estimated 1RMs at the start and end, then plan the next block from the result.
