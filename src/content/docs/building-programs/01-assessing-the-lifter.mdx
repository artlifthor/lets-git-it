## In one minute

Before writing a program, a coach has to know who it is for: a **needs analysis** collects the lifter's training age, current strength, weak points, injury history, goals, meet calendar and recovery capacity. **Training age** is judged by how fast a lifter adapts (session to session, week to week, or block to block), not by years in a gym. Maxes can be tested directly, which is reliable for most people, or estimated more safely from rep sets and RPE. Weak points come from four sources used together: lift ratios, sticking points, video, and training history. Anything that looks medical, or pain that does not settle, is referred to a qualified health professional before heavy training continues.

## Why assess before programming

**A program is an answer; the assessment is the question.** The same 12-week plan can be right for one lifter and wrong for another with a different training age, schedule, injury or meet date.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> An expert review of resistance-training prescription describes program design as starting with the lifter's individual needs and goals, then choosing exercises, order, rest, sets, reps and load to match, and re-testing as training continues (Kraemer & Ratamess, 2004).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> Lifters respond very differently to the same program (see individual difference in [Part 1 · Chapter 3](/lets-git-it/foundations/03-core-training-principles/)). Assessment is how the coach starts closer to the right answer instead of finding it by trial and error.

The needs analysis has seven parts. Each feeds a specific program decision:

| Assessment area | Main program decision it drives |
|---|---|
| Training age | Progression model (session, weekly, or block) |
| Current maxes | Starting loads |
| Strengths and weak points | Exercise selection, volume per lift, frequency per lift |
| Injury and health screening | What to avoid, modify, or refer out |
| Goals and meet calendar | Plan length, phases, when to peak |
| Lifestyle and recovery capacity | Days per week, session length, total volume |
| Preferences and equipment | What the lifter will actually do consistently |

:::tip[In plain words]
Before you write anyone a program, find out who they are: how long they've trained, how strong they are, what's weak, what hurts, what they want, and how much time and energy they have. The program should fit the person, not the other way round.
:::

## Training age

**Training age is about how fast a lifter adapts, not how long they have had a gym membership.** Rippetoe & Baker classify lifters by how long it takes for one training stress to produce a measurable gain.

| Stage | How long a stress takes to produce a gain | Typical progression | Typical history |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Novice** | One session (48–72 h) | Add load every session ([linear progression](/lets-git-it/foundations/07-novice-linear-progression/)) | Months, not years, of structured barbell training |
| **Intermediate** | About a week | New best each week; heavy and light days | LP has ended; usually 1–3+ years of consistent training |
| **Advanced** | Several weeks to months | Blocks and periodized cycles; peaks for meets | Many years; close to their own ceiling |

- <Evidence level="practice" /> These stages come from Rippetoe & Baker and are defined by **response**, not calendar time. A lifter who has trained casually for five years but never followed a progressive program may still progress like a novice. A lifter returning after a long break may also progress quickly at first.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Research supports the idea that trained lifters need a different dose. A meta-analysis found the largest gains in untrained people at about 60% 1RM and 3 days per week per muscle group, while trained people did best at about 80% 1RM and 2 days per week (Rhea et al., 2003).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In competition records of 1,897 powerlifters over 15 years, the strongest men improved about half as fast as the least strong men (0.102 vs 0.211 kg of total per day), which the authors suggested may be a ceiling effect (Latella et al., 2020).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Lifts can be at different stages. Bench press often stops progressing session to session before the squat and deadlift do. Classify **each lift**, not just the lifter.

**Quick test:** look at the last 8–12 weeks of logs. If loads went up nearly every session, the lifter is a novice on that lift. If bests come roughly weekly, intermediate. If a new best needs a planned build-up of several weeks, advanced.

:::tip[In plain words]
Your training age is how fast your body responds, not how many years you've lifted. If you can add weight every workout, you're a novice. If you set new bests about once a week, you're an intermediate. If it takes a whole training block to set a best, you're advanced.
:::

## Testing or estimating maxes safely

Starting loads need a current estimate of each lift's one-rep max (1RM). There are four ways to get one.

| Method | How | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Recent meet result** | Use the best competition lifts from the last 3–4 months | Competitive lifters | Out of date after a long break or injury |
| **True 1RM test** | Work up in singles to a max with good technique | Experienced lifters with solid technique | Fatigue, risk of grinding, missed lifts; takes a whole session |
| **Rep-max estimate** | A hard set of 3–5, converted with a formula or chart | Most intermediates; novices | Less accurate with more reps |
| **RPE estimate** | A single, double or triple at RPE 7–9, converted with the RPE chart | Lifters who rate RPE well | Only as accurate as the RPE rating |

**What the evidence says**

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **1RM testing is reliable.** A systematic review of 32 studies (1,595 people) found very high test–retest reliability (median ICC 0.97) and a median variation of about 4% between tests. Reliability was high with and without training experience, with and without familiarization sessions, for upper and lower body, and for men, women and older adults (Grgic et al., 2020).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Low-rep sets predict best.** In 70 men and women aged 18–69, a 5RM predicted bench press and leg press 1RM more accurately than a 10RM or 20RM, and the authors advised using no more than 10 reps in linear prediction equations (Reynolds et al., 2006).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **RPE tracks percentage of 1RM in powerlifters.** In 15 powerlifters working up to a max on squat, bench press and deadlift, RPE and percentage of 1RM were very strongly related (r = 0.88–0.91), and a true max was rated about 9.6–9.7 on average (Helms et al., 2017). Lifters do not always rate a real max as a "10".

**Worked estimate.** A lifter squats 150 kg for 3 at RPE 8. The chart in [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/) puts 3 reps at RPE 8 at 86.3% of 1RM: 150 ÷ 0.863 ≈ **174 kg**, so use about 172.5–175 kg as the working estimate.

**Safe testing protocol** <Evidence level="practice" />

1. General warm-up, then the lift's usual warm-up sets.
2. Work up in progressively smaller jumps: for example sets of 3, 2, then singles at about 80%, 87%, 92% and 96% of the expected max.
3. Stop when bar speed drops sharply or technique changes. A clean single at RPE 9 is a better result than a grinding miss.
4. Use spotters or safety arms on squat and bench press, and test only lifts the lifter has practised under heavy load.
5. Test one or two lifts per session, with the deadlift last or on its own day.

**When not to test a true max:** novices (their maxes rise every week anyway), lifters with current pain, lifters returning from a long break, and the early weeks of a training block. Use rep-max or RPE estimates instead, and save true maxes for meets, mock meets, or the end of a block.

:::tip[In plain words]
You need a good guess of your max to pick training weights. Testing a true max works well if your technique is solid, but a hard set of 3 to 5, or a single at a known RPE, gives a safe and close enough estimate. Don't grind out ugly reps just to get a number.
:::

## Identifying strengths and weak points

No single tool finds weak points reliably. Use four sources and look for agreement.

### 1. Lift ratios and relative strength

**Compare the lifts with each other and with body weight.** A lift that is low compared with the others may be undertrained or limited by a specific weakness.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Normative data from 809,986 entries in drug-tested, unequipped (raw) competitions give realistic benchmarks. For lifters aged 18–35, the 90th percentile was about **2.83 × body weight** squat, **1.95 ×** bench press and **3.25 ×** deadlift for men, and **2.26 ×**, **1.35 ×** and **2.66 ×** for women. Relative strength declined after age 35 (van den Hoek et al., 2024).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Dividing those 90th-percentile values gives rough reference ratios: in men, bench press about 0.69 and deadlift about 1.15 of the squat; in women, bench press about 0.60 and deadlift about 1.18 of the squat. This is arithmetic on group data, not a validated standard. Body proportions, deadlift style and training history shift every lifter's ratios.

| Ratio | Rough reference (men) | Rough reference (women) | If clearly lower, consider… |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bench ÷ squat | ~0.65–0.75 | ~0.55–0.65 | Bench undertrained? Upper-body muscle mass? |
| Deadlift ÷ squat | ~1.10–1.25 | ~1.10–1.25 | Pulling technique, back strength, grip |
| Squat ÷ deadlift | ~0.80–0.90 | ~0.80–0.90 | Squat undertrained, technique, depth, quads |

<Evidence level="practice" /> The ranges are coaching rules of thumb around the norm-derived values. Treat a ratio as a **question to investigate**, not a diagnosis. A long-armed lifter may simply be a good deadlifter.

### 2. Sticking points

**Where in the lift does the bar slow down or stop?** The sticking point is the position where the lift becomes disproportionately harder and where failed reps usually fail.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A review describes the sticking point as arising from the combination of muscle force capacity and the changing mechanics (leverage) through the range of motion, and reviews training methods used to address it (Kompf & Arandjelović, 2016).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Record where heavy sets slow or fail across several sessions, not one. A single bad rep often reflects fatigue or a technical slip.

Detailed diagnosis, lift by lift, is in Part 5, Chapter 7: From sticking point to accessory, and Part 3, Chapter 5: Advanced weak-point analysis.

### 3. Video

<Evidence level="practice" /> Film heavy sets (RPE 8+) from the side at about hip height for squat and deadlift, and from the side or at a 45° angle for bench press. Look for:

- **Squat:** depth, hips rising faster than the chest out of the hole, knees caving, losing balance forward.
- **Bench press:** touch point, elbow path, bar drifting toward the face or feet, hips lifting, uneven lockout.
- **Deadlift:** bar drifting away from the legs, hips shooting up, back rounding increasing during the pull, lockout slowing.

Compare a heavy set with a moderate one. Faults that appear only near max are usually strength or fatigue problems. Faults present at every load are usually technique problems.

### 4. Training history

<Evidence level="practice" /> Logs often explain weak points better than biomechanics. Ask:

- How many hard sets per week, and how many days, has each lift had over the last 3–6 months?
- Which lift has the lifter avoided, skipped or always trained last?
- Which programs produced the best progress, and which caused stalls or pain?
- How does the lifter respond to high volume vs high intensity?

A lift trained once a week for half a year, while the others got three sessions, is a **training gap**, not a mystery weakness.

:::tip[In plain words]
To find your weak spots, use four clues: how your lifts compare with each other, where the bar slows down, what your videos show, and what you've actually been training. When two or three clues point the same way, you've probably found the real weakness.
:::

## Injury, health and limitation screening

**Powerlifters train with pain more often than they stop for injury.** Screening finds what needs modifying, and what needs a professional.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 9 studies found injury rates in powerlifting of about 1.0–4.4 injuries per 1,000 hours of training, similar to other non-contact strength and power sports and low compared with contact sports. The spine, shoulder and knee were most often affected. Most included studies were of low methodological quality (Aasa et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In a questionnaire of 245 competitive powerlifters, 43.3% reported problems during routine training, most often in the shoulder, lower back and knee. Upper-limb injuries (shoulder, elbow, hand and wrist) were more frequent in lifters over 40 (Siewe et al., 2011).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 104 Swedish sub-elite classic powerlifters, 70% reported a current injury defined as pain or impaired function affecting training, and 87% reported one in the past 12 months. Only 16% of those currently injured had stopped training completely; most changed what they did in sessions instead (Strömbäck et al., 2018).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> A position statement on exercise pre-participation screening bases the need for medical clearance on three factors: the person's current physical activity, signs or symptoms or known heart, metabolic or kidney disease, and the intended exercise intensity (Riebe et al., 2015). For a coach, that means asking about all three at intake.

**Refer out before heavy training if the lifter reports** <Evidence level="practice" />:

- Chest pain, unusual breathlessness, dizziness or fainting with exertion.
- Known heart, metabolic (for example diabetes) or kidney disease, especially in someone not currently active.
- Numbness, tingling or weakness in an arm or leg; pain spreading down a limb.
- Pain that is severe, worsening, present at night or at rest, or follows a fall or a sudden "pop".
- Changes in bladder or bowel control with back pain (an emergency).
- Pregnancy, recent surgery, or any condition a doctor has told them limits exercise.

A coach does not diagnose. For lesser complaints (a cranky elbow, a stiff hip), record where, when and how much it hurts, which lifts and grips provoke it, and modify around it. If it does not settle within a couple of weeks, refer to a physiotherapist or sports doctor. Programming around pain is covered in Part 5, Chapter 10: Programming around injury.

:::tip[In plain words]
Most lifters have some aches, and most keep training by adjusting what they do. But some symptoms, like chest pain, fainting, numbness, or pain that gets worse or wakes you at night, need a doctor before you lift heavy. A coach's job is to spot these and send you to the right person, not to diagnose.
:::

## Goals and the meet calendar

**The goal decides the plan's length and shape.** Get both an outcome and a date.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> Write goals as specific lifts or totals with a date: "a 450 kg total at the national championships in 20 weeks", not "get stronger".
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Check the federation calendar (for lifters in the Philippines, the Powerlifting Association of the Philippines' published meet schedule) and any qualifying totals the lifter needs.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Work backwards from the meet: the peak and taper sit in the final 3–6 weeks, so the time before that is for building.

| Weeks to the next meet | Program direction |
|---|---|
| No meet planned | Build: muscle, work capacity, weak points; test maxes every 8–12 weeks |
| 16+ weeks | Build block(s), then strength block, then peak |
| 8–15 weeks | Shorter build, strength block, peak |
| 4–7 weeks | Mostly specific strength and peaking; no big program changes |
| Under 4 weeks | Peak and taper only; do not change technique or test new maxes |

Planning a whole year around several meets is covered in Part 3, Chapter 9: Annual and multi-year planning; peaking in Part 4.

:::tip[In plain words]
Set a clear goal with a date, like "total 300 kg at the March meet." Then count back from that date. The weeks closest to the meet are for getting sharp, and the weeks before that are for building strength.
:::

## Lifestyle and recovery capacity

**Recovery capacity sets the ceiling on how much training a lifter can use.** Two lifters with the same maxes can need very different volumes.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 135 university students on the same 12-week training program, those reporting high life stress gained significantly less bench press and squat strength than those reporting low stress (Bartholomew et al., 2008).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Ask about sleep (hours and regularity), work hours and shifts, physical demands of the job, family duties, travel, diet and body-weight goals, and other sports. Sleep and stress are covered in [Part 1 · Chapter 10](/lets-git-it/foundations/10-recovery-sleep-stress/), nutrition in [Part 1 · Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/foundations/11-nutrition/).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Ask how many days per week, and how many minutes per session, the lifter can **reliably** train, not on their best week. A 3-day program done every week beats a 5-day program done half the time.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Note the environment: equipment, available plates (small change plates matter for bench progress), training partners, and heat. In hot, non-air-conditioned gyms, plan for longer rests and fluid intake.

| Recovery capacity | Signs | Program implication |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | 7–9 h sleep, low stress, flexible schedule, good diet | Normal-to-high volume; 4–5 days possible |
| **Moderate** | Some short nights, a demanding job, occasional travel | Moderate volume; 3–4 days; RPE caps on bad days |
| **Low** | Shift work, new baby, exams, cutting weight, illness | Lower volume, fewer days, autoregulation; aim to maintain or progress slowly |

:::tip[In plain words]
How much training you can handle depends on your whole life, not just the gym. Poor sleep, a stressful job or night shifts all reduce how much you can recover from. Plan for the week you'll usually have, not your best one.
:::

## The intake checklist

Use this at the first meeting and update it every training block.

| Area | Questions to ask | Red flags or key decisions |
|---|---|---|
| **Basics** | Age, sex, body weight, weight class, height | Weight class goals affect nutrition and timing |
| **Training history** | Years training; years on a structured program; past programs; best and worst blocks | Months of structured training → likely novice |
| **Current training** | Days per week; sets per lift per week; exercises; RPE use | Big gaps between lifts; volume tolerance |
| **Current strength** | Meet bests and dates; recent rep sets with RPE | Choose test vs estimate |
| **Progress pattern** | Session-to-session, weekly, or monthly bests? | Sets training age per lift |
| **Technique** | Recent video of heavy sets on all three lifts | Faults at all loads vs only near max |
| **Health** | Medical conditions, medication, surgery, chest pain, dizziness, pregnancy | Refer out before heavy training |
| **Pain and injury** | Current pain (where, when, how much, what provokes it); past injuries | Red flags → refer; otherwise modify |
| **Goals** | Target lifts or total; competition goals; other goals (body composition, health) | Conflicting goals need priorities |
| **Calendar** | Next meet date; qualifying totals; holidays, exams, travel | Plan length and peak timing |
| **Lifestyle** | Sleep, job, shifts, stress, family duties | Recovery capacity → volume and days |
| **Nutrition** | Usual eating pattern; weight trend; making weight | Weight cuts conflict with strength gains |
| **Logistics** | Days and minutes available; gym equipment; change plates; heat | Session design |
| **Preferences** | Favourite and hated exercises; percentage or RPE | Adherence |

**Turn the answers into a one-paragraph needs summary:** training age per lift, estimated maxes, top 1–2 weak points, limitations, goal and date, days available, and recovery capacity. Every program decision should trace back to something in that paragraph.

## Sample programs

Three worked case studies: the lifter's profile, what the coach concludes, the program direction, and a first week. RPE-to-kg conversions use the chart in [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg. Accessories are shortened.

### Program 1: Case study — the bench-heavy lifter with a lagging squat

**Profile.** Marco, 24, male, 74 kg (74 kg class). About 14 months of training: 8 months of novice linear progression, then a mix of online programs. Recent sets: squat 140 kg × 3 at RPE 8, bench press 95 kg × 5 at RPE 8, deadlift 185 kg × 2 at RPE 8. For the last 6 months he squatted once a week (he dislikes it) and benched three times. Office job, sleeps about 7 hours, no pain, no medical issues. Goal: first local meet in 16 weeks. Can train 4 days, 75 minutes.

**Estimated maxes.** Squat 140 ÷ 0.863 ≈ **162.5 kg**. Bench 95 ÷ 0.811 ≈ **117.5 kg**. Deadlift 185 ÷ 0.892 ≈ **207.5 kg**.

**Conclusions.**

| Finding | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Intermediate on all lifts | LP ended months ago; bests now come every 1–2 weeks | Weekly progression; no novice program |
| Squat lags | Bench ÷ squat 0.72 and deadlift ÷ squat 1.28, both above the rough references | Squat is relatively weak |
| Cause is likely a training gap | Squat trained once a week for 6 months; video shows hips rising fast out of the hole near max | More squat frequency and volume; pause squats |
| Good recovery capacity | Sleep, job, schedule | Moderate-to-high volume is fine |
| Meet in 16 weeks | Calendar | About 11 weeks of building, then a 4–5 week peak |

**Direction.** Squat twice a week plus a pause squat day; bench reduced from 3 to 2–3 sessions with slightly fewer sets to free up recovery; deadlift once a week. RPE-based loading, since he rates RPE consistently.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~127.5 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) |
| 1 | Leg press | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 2 | Deadlift | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 (~167.5 kg) |
| 2 | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × 4 | RPE 6.5 |
| 2 | Barbell row | 4 × 8 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Squat | 4 × 3 | RPE 7.5 (~137.5 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press | 5 × 3 | RPE 7.5 (~100 kg) |
| 4 | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Split squat | 3 × 10 each leg | RPE 8 |

**Re-assess** at week 6 with a rep-max or RPE 8 triple on squat. If the squat estimate has risen faster than the other lifts, the diagnosis was right.

### Program 2: Case study — the shift worker with a sore shoulder

**Profile.** Liza, 31, female, 62 kg (63 kg class). Three years of structured training, two meets. Meet bests 3 months ago: squat 120 kg, bench press 62.5 kg, deadlift 150 kg. She is a nurse on rotating 12-hour shifts, including night weeks. Front-of-shoulder pain (3 out of 10) when benching with a wide grip; no numbness, no night pain, settles within a day. A physiotherapist assessed it last month and cleared her to train with modifications. Goal: national championships in 20 weeks. Can train 3 fixed days, plus an optional fourth.

**Relative strength.** Squat 1.94 ×, bench 1.01 ×, deadlift 2.42 × body weight: solid, below the 90th-percentile values for her age group. Bench ÷ squat 0.52 is a little below the rough reference for women; deadlift ÷ squat 1.25 is at the top of the range.

**Conclusions.**

| Finding | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Intermediate, approaching advanced | Bests come over blocks, not weeks | Block-based plan toward nationals |
| Bench is the priority | Ratio; bench stalled the last two cycles | Bench 3 × per week, fewer sets per session |
| Shoulder pain, already assessed | Physio clearance; no red flags | Medium grip; avoid painful positions; monitor pain each session |
| Low-to-moderate recovery capacity | Rotating shifts | Autoregulate with RPE; a short "minimum session" for night weeks |
| Meet in 20 weeks | Calendar | Two building blocks, then a peak |

**Direction.** Three full-body days with bench every day. All loads by RPE so shift weeks adjust automatically. **Shift rule:** in a night-shift week, do only the top set and one back-off set of each main lift, capped at RPE 7. **Pain rule:** if the shoulder exceeds 3 out of 10 during or after a session, or is worse the next day, change the variation and tell the physiotherapist.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 1 × 4 @ RPE 8, then 3 × 4 | ~100 kg, then ~92.5 kg |
| 1 | Bench press (medium grip) | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~50 kg) |
| 1 | Chest-supported row | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 2 | Deadlift | 1 × 3 @ RPE 8, then 2 × 3 | ~130 kg, then ~120 kg |
| 2 | Bench press (medium grip) | 5 × 3 | RPE 7.5 (~52.5 kg) |
| 2 | Split squat | 3 × 8 each leg | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Squat | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| 3 | Pause bench press (2 s) | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Dumbbell overhead press | 3 × 10 | RPE 8, pain-free range |
| 4 (optional) | Close-grip bench press, back extension | 3 × 8 each | RPE 7 |

### Program 3: Case study — the returning masters lifter

**Profile.** Ramon, 52, male, 90 kg. Lifted in university, then nothing for about 10 years. Has type 2 diabetes managed with medication. Mild knee stiffness in the morning, no pain under load so far. Desk job, sleeps well. Goal: compete in a masters category in 9–12 months. Can train 3 days.

**Conclusions.**

| Finding | Evidence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Medical clearance first | Currently inactive, known metabolic disease, plans high-intensity training (the three screening factors in Riebe et al., 2015) | Doctor's clearance before training starts |
| Novice in adaptation terms | Detrained; old maxes are irrelevant | Linear progression with small jumps |
| Do not test maxes | Detrained, older, technique rusty | Start light; estimate from sets of 5 at RPE ≤7 after week 3 |
| Upper-limb care | Upper-limb injuries were more common in lifters over 40 (Siewe et al., 2011) | Bench grip comfort; gradual bench progression; extra upper-back work |
| Long timeline | 9–12 months to a meet | No peaking needed for months; build habits and technique |

**Direction.** After clearance, a 3-day novice-style program with fixed loads, longer warm-ups and smaller jumps than a young novice (squat and deadlift +2.5 kg per session, bench +1–1.25 kg). Teach the RPE scale from day one. Move to weekly progression when the novice phase ends ([Part 2 · Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/building-programs/02-beyond-linear-progression/)). Masters-specific programming is covered in Part 3, Chapter 10: Special populations.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load (week 1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat (to a box at competition depth for weeks 1–2) | 3 × 5 | 60 kg (should feel like RPE 5–6) |
| Mon | Bench press | 3 × 5 | 50 kg |
| Mon | Lat pulldown | 3 × 10 | RPE 7 |
| Wed | Squat | 3 × 5 | 62.5 kg |
| Wed | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | 60 kg |
| Wed | Dumbbell row | 3 × 10 | RPE 7 |
| Fri | Squat | 3 × 5 | 65 kg |
| Fri | Bench press | 3 × 5 | 51.25 kg |
| Fri | Deadlift | 1 × 5 | 90 kg |

## Common mistakes

- **Copying a program before assessing.** A plan written for someone else's training age, schedule or injuries will need fixing later.
- **Judging training age by years in the gym.** Classify by how fast the lifter adapts, and classify each lift separately.
- **Testing a true max when an estimate would do.** Novices, returning lifters and anyone in pain should use rep sets or RPE estimates.
- **Treating a lift ratio as a diagnosis.** Ratios raise questions. Confirm with sticking points, video and training history.
- **Calling a training gap a "weak point".** Check the logs first: the weak lift is often just the one trained least.
- **Diagnosing pain.** Record it, modify around it, and refer red flags or persistent pain to a qualified health professional.
- **Planning for the lifter's best week.** Program for the days and recovery they will reliably have.
- **Assessing once.** Re-assess every block: maxes, weak points, pain and life circumstances all change.

## Key takeaways

- A needs analysis covers training age, current maxes, weak points, injury and health, goals and calendar, recovery capacity, and preferences.
- Training age is defined by how quickly a lifter adapts: session to session (novice), weekly (intermediate), or over blocks (advanced).
- 1RM tests are reliable, but rep sets of about 3–5 or RPE-rated singles give safe, close estimates for most training purposes.
- Find weak points by combining lift ratios, sticking points, video and training history.
- Pain is common in powerlifting and usually managed by modifying training; red flags and persistent pain need a health professional.
- Plan backwards from the meet date, and set volume and training days by the lifter's real recovery capacity.
