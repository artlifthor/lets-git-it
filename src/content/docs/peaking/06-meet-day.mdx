## In one minute

Meet day is a long day of waiting with nine short efforts in it, and most points are lost to poor logistics rather than lack of strength. Know the structure (sessions, flights, rounds), hand in rack heights and openers at weigh-in, and time every warm-up to **when your attempt will actually be called**, not to the clock. Eat and drink familiar food after weigh-in and keep sipping and snacking through the day; under-hydration costs strength. Caffeine helps modestly (clearer for upper-body strength), works best at a dose already tested in training, and has a sleep cost. A calm, rehearsed routine and a handler who manages attempts, timing and cues let the lifter think about one thing only: the next lift.

## How a meet day is organised

Before you can time anything, you need to know how the day runs.

| Term | What it means | Why it matters to you |
|---|---|---|
| **Session** | A group of weight classes that weigh in and lift together (e.g. morning: women; afternoon: men) | Sets your weigh-in time and start time |
| **Flight** | A sub-group of lifters within a session (Flight A, B, C…) | Flights take turns: all of Flight A's squats, then all of Flight B's |
| **Round** | One attempt by every lifter in the flight | You lift once per round, so the gap between your attempts ≈ the size of your flight |
| **Rising bar** | Within a round, the bar only goes up; lighter attempts go first, and lot numbers break ties | Your place in each round depends on your attempt weight, not your name |

<Evidence level="practice" /> A useful rule of thumb: each attempt takes **about one minute** of meet time (the lifter has one minute to start the attempt once the bar is announced as ready, plus loading and changeover time). So:

- A **flight of 10** takes roughly 30–40 minutes per lift (3 rounds).
- Your next attempt comes roughly **one flight-length of lifters later**: about 8–12 minutes in a flight of 10, 14–18 minutes in a flight of 14. (In the IPF rules, flights are formed when a session has 10 or more lifters and must be formed at 15 or more.)
- If you are in Flight B, your warm-ups happen **while Flight A is lifting**, so watch their progress, not the clock.

The exact rules for timing, attempt changes and commands are covered in [Chapter 7: Competition rules and commands](/lets-git-it/peaking/07-competition-rules/).

:::tip[In plain words]
A meet runs in groups called flights. Everyone in your flight takes their first squat, then everyone takes their second, and so on. The more people in your flight, the longer you wait between attempts. Plan your warm-ups around how fast the flight in front of you is moving.
:::

## The timeline, from wake-up to the last deadlift

<Evidence level="practice" /> This is a typical IPF-style day. Under the IPF Technical Rulebook, weigh-in starts no earlier than 2 hours before the session and lasts 1.5 hours; check your meet's schedule, since local meets can differ.

| When | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Night before** | Pack the bag (checklist below); confirm venue, weigh-in time, flight lists; set two alarms |
| **Wake-up** | 2.5–3 h before weigh-in if travel is short; earlier if far. Weigh yourself at home if you are making weight |
| **Arrive** | 20–30 min before weigh-in opens; find the warm-up room, toilets, the scoreboard and the platform |
| **Weigh-in** | Weigh in; hand in **openers for all three lifts**, **squat rack height** and **bench rack/safety heights**; equipment may be checked here |
| **Right after weigh-in** | Drink and eat (see below); rehydration plan if you cut weight |
| **Rules briefing / flight posted** | Confirm your flight, lot number and start time |
| **~50–60 min before your squat opener** | General warm-up, then squat warm-ups timed to the flight |
| **Squat** | Three attempts; next attempt handed in within 1 minute after each |
| **Break** | Usually 10–20 min after the last squat flight; start bench warm-ups during the previous flight |
| **Bench press** | Three attempts |
| **Break and deadlift** | Same pattern; eat a small snack early in the bench–deadlift gap |
| **After** | Awards, drug testing if selected, food, sleep |

:::tip[In plain words]
Get there early, weigh in, hand in your first attempts and rack heights, then eat and drink. After that, the day is a cycle of warm up, lift three times, rest, and repeat. Your handler watches the clock and the scoreboard so you don't have to.
:::

## Weigh-in, rack heights and equipment check

**Weigh-in.** <Evidence level="practice" /> If you are comfortably under your class limit, weigh in early in the window anyway: it gives you more time to eat and settle. If you are cutting weight, follow the plan from [Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/peaking/04-making-weight/) and never try a new method on meet day.

**Rack heights.** <Evidence level="practice" />

- Write down your squat rack setting and bench upright and safety settings **from your mock meet or opener practice** ([Chapter 3](/lets-git-it/peaking/03-mock-meets-openers/)), and the type of rack used if known.
- Competition racks differ from gym racks. If the warm-up room has the same model, test the height there before you hand it in.
- Squat: the bar should sit about at, or slightly below, the middle of the chest, so you can unrack with a small knee straightening, not a calf raise.
- Bench: set the uprights so the handoff (or your own unrack) is short and controlled.

**Equipment.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Bring every item you will lift in to weigh-in, since checks often happen there. All gear must meet the rules and, at IPF-level meets, be on the approved list (details in [Chapter 7](/lets-git-it/peaking/07-competition-rules/)).

| Bag checklist | Notes |
|---|---|
| Singlet, T-shirt, briefs/underwear | Approved items; a spare T-shirt |
| Belt, wrist wraps, knee sleeves | Approved models; check the sleeves are not torn |
| Squat/bench shoes, deadlift shoes | Already used in training |
| Shin-length socks for deadlift | Required by IPF rules for the deadlift |
| Membership/ID card, entry confirmation | PAP registration for local meets |
| Food, water, electrolyte drink | More than you think you need |
| Caffeine source with known dose | Only if tested in training |
| Chalk, towel, slides, warm jacket | Venues can be cold (air-conditioning) or very hot |
| Attempt plan, pen, timer | The handler's tools |

:::tip[In plain words]
At weigh-in you tell the officials your first attempts and how high you want the racks. Know those numbers in advance from your practice sessions. Pack all your gear the night before and bring it to weigh-in in case they check it.
:::

## Warming up to your attempts

<Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis found that warming up improved performance in most outcomes studied (Fradkin et al., 2010). <Evidence level="practice" /> The ramp itself follows [Part 1, Chapter 9](/lets-git-it/foundations/09-session-structure-warm-ups/): fewer reps as the load rises, smaller jumps near the top, last warm-up about 90–95% of the opener. What changes on meet day is **timing**.

**Rules for timing warm-ups** <Evidence level="practice" />

1. Count **attempts until your opener**, not minutes. Check the flight sheet: how many lifters are in front of you, and how many attempts are left in the flight before yours?
2. Aim to finish your **last warm-up 6–10 minutes (about 6–10 attempts) before your opener**. Long enough to wrap, chalk and walk out; short enough to stay warm.
3. Warm-up racks are shared. Agree an order with the lifters on your rack, and allow for loading delays.
4. If the meet runs late, **stretch the gaps between light sets**, not between the heavy ones. If it runs fast, skip a light set, never the last heavy single.
5. The second and third lifts need fewer light sets, because you are already warm.

**Between attempts.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Sit, keep warm (jacket, sleeves rolled down), sip water, and listen to the handler's one cue. In small flights (under about 6 lifters) the gap can be only a few minutes, so stay near the platform. In very long gaps (20+ minutes), some coaches add one easy set (e.g. 1–2 reps at about 60–70% of the next attempt) 6–10 minutes before the attempt; test this in a mock meet first.

**Pause your bench warm-ups.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Pause the heavy bench singles and use the start, press and rack commands from your handler, so the opener is not the first paused rep of the day.

:::tip[In plain words]
Warm up the same way you do in training, but time it to your turn on the platform. Watch how many people are ahead of you and finish your last warm-up about 6–10 attempts before you are called. If the meet slows down, wait longer between your light sets; if it speeds up, skip a light set, never the last heavy one.
:::

## Eating and drinking on meet day

A meet can last 4–10 hours after weigh-in. The goal is to arrive at each lift fed, hydrated and without a heavy stomach.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review found that carbohydrate intake mostly did not change strength-training performance when lifters had already eaten; benefits appeared mainly against fasted conditions and in higher-volume sessions (Henselmans et al., 2022). A meet is low volume but very long, so the practical priority is simple: **don't lift fasted, and keep topping up**.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 28 studies found that hypohydration reduced muscle strength by about 5.5% on average (Savoie et al., 2015).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> The ACSM position stand advises starting exercise well hydrated and drinking enough to avoid losing more than 2% of bodyweight as water, with individual plans because sweat rates vary (Sawka et al., 2007). The ISSN position stand on nutrient timing supports protein- and carbohydrate-containing meals before training (Kerksick et al., 2017).

**Practical rules** <Evidence level="practice" />

1. **Familiar food only.** Eat what you eat before heavy training; nothing new on meet day.
2. **After weigh-in:** a meal like your usual pre-training meal (about 0.3–0.5 g/kg protein and 1–2 g/kg carbohydrate, from [Part 1, Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/foundations/11-nutrition/)), lower in fat and fibre so it digests quickly.
3. **During the meet:** small carbohydrate snacks every 60–90 minutes, especially in the breaks between lifts.
4. **Fluids:** sip steadily; in a hot venue, use an electrolyte drink. Pale-yellow urine is a simple check.
5. **If you cut weight,** follow the rehydration and refuelling plan from [Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/peaking/04-making-weight/) first; it takes priority over everything else in this section.

**Meet-day food ideas (easy to find in the Philippines)**

| Timing | Options | Why |
|---|---|---|
| After weigh-in (main meal) | Rice with chicken (adobo with less oil, or inasal without skin), egg; pandesal with egg or peanut butter; banana | Familiar carbohydrate and protein; quick to digest |
| Between lifts (small snacks) | Bananas, pandesal, suman or puto, crackers (e.g. Skyflakes), a rice ball | Mostly carbohydrate, low fat, easy to carry |
| Fluids all day | Water, oral rehydration solution or sports drink, buko (coconut) water if you know it suits you | Fluid plus salt; important in hot venues |
| Avoid right before lifting | Large fatty meals (lechon, fried food), lots of soft drinks, very spicy food, high-fibre meals | Slow to digest; can cause bloating or stomach upset |

:::tip[In plain words]
Eat a normal, familiar meal after weigh-in, then keep snacking on small, easy carbs like bananas and pandesal all day. Sip water or an electrolyte drink the whole time, especially if the venue is hot. Don't try any new food on meet day.
:::

## Caffeine

**What it does**

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis (10 studies on strength) found caffeine improved maximal strength by a small amount; the effect was significant for **upper-body** strength but not for lower-body strength (Grgic et al., 2018). An umbrella review rated the evidence that caffeine improves muscle strength as moderate quality (Grgic et al., 2020).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> Results differ between studies. In 17 resistance-trained men, 6 mg/kg improved **squat** 1RM by 2.8% but did not improve bench press 1RM (Grgic & Mikulic, 2017). Expect a small effect, and some lifters will not notice one.

**How to use it**

- <Evidence level="practice" /> The ISSN position stand reports benefits at **3–6 mg/kg**, usually taken about **60 minutes** before exercise, with a possible minimum near 2 mg/kg; very high doses (about 9 mg/kg) bring frequent side effects without extra benefit. Responses vary with genetics and habitual intake (Guest et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> A review written specifically for powerlifting meets suggested doses of about 2–6 mg/kg, chosen individually; about 60 minutes before for capsules (5–10 minutes for caffeine gum); one dose may be enough for a short meet, and smaller repeated doses may help in long meets. It also stated that stopping caffeine for a week before the meet does not seem necessary (Grgic et al., 2019).
- <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 24 studies found caffeine reduced total sleep time by about 45 minutes; to avoid this, coffee (about 107 mg) should be taken at least 8.8 hours before bed and a typical pre-workout dose (about 217.5 mg) at least 13.2 hours before (Gardiner et al., 2023).

**Decision rules** <Evidence level="practice" />

1. **Use only a dose you have used before heavy singles in training** (or at a mock meet). Nerves plus a new high dose can mean shaking hands, a racing heart and a sick stomach.
2. **Main dose** about 60 minutes before the squat opener (capsule or measured coffee).
3. **Long meets:** a smaller top-up (about 1–2 mg/kg) before bench or deadlift if you have tested it, keeping the day's total within the tested range.
4. **Afternoon and evening sessions:** a top-up late in the day will cost sleep that night. The night after the meet matters less than the nights before it, but a lifter with a training session or travel the next day should skip late doses.
5. **Check the product.** Caffeine is allowed, but "energy" and pre-workout products can contain prohibited stimulants (see [Part 1, Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/foundations/11-nutrition/)).

| Bodyweight | ~2 mg/kg (top-up / sensitive) | 3 mg/kg (main dose) | 6 mg/kg (upper limit of useful range) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 52 kg | ~105 mg | ~155 mg | ~310 mg |
| 63 kg | ~125 mg | ~190 mg | ~380 mg |
| 83 kg | ~165 mg | ~250 mg | ~500 mg |
| 105 kg | ~210 mg | ~315 mg | ~630 mg |

:::tip[In plain words]
Caffeine can give a small boost, more reliably for the bench than for the squat or deadlift. Take a dose you have already tried in training, about an hour before your first squat. Late doses will hurt your sleep, and you are not obliged to use caffeine at all.
:::

## Sleep the night before

- <Evidence level="practice" /> In a survey of 283 elite Australian athletes, 64% reported worse sleep before an important competition at least once in the past year, mostly trouble falling asleep because of thoughts about the competition and nerves (Juliff et al., 2015).
- <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis found that sleep loss before exercise reduced performance by about 7.6% on average; a full night without sleep and waking early consistently lowered performance, while going to bed late did not show a consistent effect (Craven et al., 2022). <Evidence level="moderate" /> A review focused on lifting found that one night of total sleep loss had little effect on strength, whereas several nights of short sleep could reduce force on multi-joint lifts (Knowles et al., 2018). The reviews disagree on how much one bad night costs.

**Decision rules** <Evidence level="practice" />

- Protect sleep **all week**, not just the night before; the nights before are the ones you can control.
- Keep the normal bedtime the night before. If you can't sleep, read or rest quietly; don't panic, since one restless night is common and its effect on strength is uncertain.
- For early weigh-ins, move bedtime and wake-up earlier over the last 3–4 days rather than suddenly on the last night.
- No caffeine after early afternoon in the last days before the meet (Gardiner et al., 2023).

:::tip[In plain words]
Most athletes sleep badly the night before a big competition, so don't worry if you do too. Sleep well all week, keep your usual bedtime, and rest quietly if you can't fall asleep. One rough night is not the end of your meet.
:::

## Handling and coaching duties

<Evidence level="practice" /> A good handler takes every job off the lifter except lifting.

| Job | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Attempts** | Hand in the next attempt **within 1 minute** after each lift (the rule; see [Chapter 7](/lets-git-it/peaking/07-competition-rules/)). Have the plan A/B/C written for every attempt ([Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/peaking/05-attempt-selection/)) |
| **Timing** | Track the flight sheet and scoreboard; call each warm-up ("next set: 145, in 2 minutes") |
| **Warm-up room** | Book rack time, load plates, check collars and loads |
| **Cues** | One short cue per attempt, the same one used in training (e.g. "brace, wait for 'Squat'", "chest up", "push the floor") |
| **Commands** | In bench warm-ups, give "Start", "Press", "Rack"; remind the lifter to wait for every signal |
| **Equipment** | Wraps on/off, belt notches, sleeves, chalk, deadlift socks, spare everything |
| **Food and drink** | Hand out snacks and drinks on schedule; the lifter will forget |
| **Video and notes** | Film each attempt from the side where the meet allows it (at IPF international events the platform coach may not film, so ask a teammate in the audience); note bar speed and RPE for the next decision and for the debrief ([Chapter 8](/lets-git-it/peaking/08-after-the-meet/)) |
| **Calm** | Calm voice, neutral facts; no news about rivals unless it changes the plan |

:::tip[In plain words]
The handler is the lifter's manager for the day: hand in attempts on time, track when to warm up, load the bar, give one simple reminder before each lift, and keep food and drink coming. The lifter's only job is to lift.
:::

## Mental preparation

Claims about "mindset" are everywhere; the evidence is narrower.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review found that cognitive strategies (goal setting, imagery, self-talk, preparatory arousal) were reliably associated with better strength performance, in roughly 61–65% of results. Results for specific strategies on specific types of strength were mixed, and no strategy had an overall negative effect (Tod et al., 2015).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Psyching up.** An earlier review concluded, tentatively, that psyching up may improve strength and muscular endurance, with preparatory arousal looking most effective (Tod et al., 2003). In 20 strength-trained men and women, a self-chosen psych-up raised peak force in a bench press on a dynamometer by about 8–12% compared with distraction or a placebo task (Tod et al., 2005).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Self-talk.** A meta-analysis of 32 studies found a moderate positive effect of self-talk on sport performance, larger for fine-skill and new tasks than for gross, well-learned ones (Hatzigeorgiadis et al., 2011). A competition lift is a gross, well-learned task, so expect a smaller effect.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Pre-performance routines.** A meta-analysis found that routines (a set sequence of thoughts and actions before performing) improved sport performance, including under pressure (Rupprecht et al., 2024). The abstract does not report maximal-strength tasks, so the size of the effect for a heavy single is unknown.

**What to do** <Evidence level="practice" />

1. **Build one routine** in the peaking block and use it on every heavy single: e.g. chalk → belt → one cue word → walk-in → breath and brace. Same order every time.
2. **One cue word** per lift, practised in training ("tight", "drive", "push").
3. **Arousal on a dial.** Some lifters perform best calm, others hyped. Learn which in training. Turn it up only in the final 30–60 seconds, not in the warm-up room.
4. **Between attempts, switch off.** Sit, breathe slowly, avoid analysing the last lift; that's the handler's job.

:::tip[In plain words]
There is reasonable evidence that psyching yourself up, using a set routine, and talking to yourself in short, helpful words can help strength a little. Practise one simple routine and one cue word in training so meet day feels familiar. Between attempts, relax and let your handler do the thinking.
:::

## Sample programs

Three meet-day plans. All loads and timings are examples; adjust them to the meet's schedule and the flight sheet.

Intermediate male, 83 kg class, walking around at ~82 kg. Openers 165 / 110 / 192.5 kg. Weigh-in 7:00–8:30; session starts 9:00; two flights of 10. Bedtime 10:00 pm.

| Clock | Action | Food, drink, caffeine |
|---|---|---|
| 5:00 | Wake; scale check at home (81.9 kg) | Small breakfast: pandesal with egg, water |
| 6:30 | Arrive; find warm-up room, check flight list | Sip water |
| 7:00 | Weigh in (82.2 kg); hand in openers and rack heights; equipment check | — |
| 7:15 | Eat | Rice, chicken, egg, banana; 500 mL water |
| 8:00 | Rules briefing; tape, set up warm-up rack | ~250 mg caffeine (3 mg/kg), tested in training |
| 8:15 | General warm-up, then squat warm-ups (table above) | Sip water |
| 9:00–9:35 | **Squat** (Flight A) | Small sips between attempts |
| 9:35 | Flight B squats; rest, then bench warm-ups from ~10:00 | Banana, electrolyte drink |
| 10:25–10:55 | **Bench press** | — |
| 11:00 | Rest; deadlift warm-ups from ~11:10 | Pandesal or suman; water; optional ~100 mg caffeine if tested |
| 11:40–12:15 | **Deadlift** | Sips of water |
| 12:30 | Done; full meal | Rice meal with protein |

**Why it's built this way.** No weight cut, so a small breakfast is safe before weigh-in. The main caffeine dose falls about an hour before the squat opener; the optional top-up at 11:00 is about 11 hours before bed, inside the coffee guideline. Flight A lifts first, so the squat warm-up must start before the session does.

Weigh-in 12:00–13:30; session 14:00, Flight A of two; caffeine-sensitive (one coffee a day). Bedtime 11:00 pm.

|---|---|---|

| 11:30 | Arrive | — |
| 12:00 | Weigh in (62.9 kg); openers, rack heights | — |

Warm-ups (last warm-up 6–10 attempts before the opener):

| Lift | Warm-up sets (kg × reps) | Opener |
|---|---|---|
| Squat | 20 × 8, 40 × 5, 65 × 3, 80 × 2, 95 × 1, 102.5 × 1 | 110 kg |

**Why it's built this way.** An afternoon session lets her sleep normally. Rehydration comes first after weigh-in because even modest under-hydration costs strength (Savoie et al., 2015). She uses the low end of the caffeine range because she is sensitive, and skips the late top-up because it would cut into sleep (Gardiner et al., 2023).

### Program 3: Large flights, long day (default lifter at a national meet)

Same default lifter, but three flights of about 14 lifters, and he is in **Flight C**. Each flight takes about 45–50 minutes per lift. Weigh-in 7:00; session 9:00; last deadlift around 4:45 pm. Bedtime 10:30 pm.

| Clock | Action | Food, drink, caffeine |
|---|---|---|
| 7:00 | Weigh in; openers and rack heights | — |
| 7:15 | Main meal | Rice, chicken, egg, banana; water |
| 9:00–10:40 | Flights A and B squat; **rest**, don't warm up yet | Water; light snack at 9:30 |
| ~9:45 | Main caffeine dose | ~200 mg (~2.5 mg/kg) |
| ~9:55 | General warm-up while Flight B is in round 1; squat warm-ups from ~10:05 (Flight B round 2) | — |
| 10:40–11:30 | **Squat** (gaps of ~14–18 min between attempts) | Sips of electrolyte drink |
| 11:30–1:15 | Break; Flights A and B bench. Bench warm-ups from ~12:40 | Small meal ~11:45: rice ball or pandesal with egg, banana |
| ~12:30 | Top-up caffeine (tested) | ~100 mg |
| 1:15–2:00 | **Bench press** | — |
| 2:00–3:55 | Break; Flights A and B deadlift. Deadlift warm-ups from ~3:25 | Snack ~2:15: suman or crackers, sports drink |
| 3:55–4:50 | **Deadlift** | No caffeine after 12:30 (bed 10:30 pm) |

**Why it's built this way.** In a long day the biggest risks are warming up too early and running out of fuel. Warm-ups are tied to Flight B's rounds, not the clock. Caffeine is split into a main dose and one tested top-up, as suggested for long meets (Grgic et al., 2019), and stopped by early afternoon to protect sleep. With 14–18 minutes between attempts, he stays warm with a jacket and walking, and adds an easy set before the next attempt only if he has tested that at a mock meet.

## Common mistakes

- **Warming up by the clock.** Meets run early and late; count attempts on the flight sheet instead.
- **Finishing warm-ups too early or too late.** Aim for the last warm-up 6–10 attempts before the opener.
- **Trying something new:** a new food, a bigger caffeine dose, new shoes or a new cue.
- **Under-eating and under-drinking** over a long day, especially after a weight cut or in a hot venue.
- **Rack heights guessed at weigh-in** instead of copied from the mock meet.
- **Missing the 1-minute deadline** for the next attempt, or a handler who doesn't know the attempt plan.
- **Late caffeine** in evening sessions when the lifter must train or travel the next day.
- **Hyping up too early.** Save arousal for the last minute before the platform.

## Key takeaways

- Know the structure: sessions, flights, rounds and the rising bar decide when you lift.
- Time warm-ups to attempts, finishing about 6–10 attempts before the opener.
- Hand in openers and rack heights at weigh-in, using numbers rehearsed in the mock meet.
- Eat familiar food after weigh-in and snack and drink all day; under-hydration costs about 5% of strength on average.
- Caffeine at 3–6 mg/kg about an hour before gives a small benefit (clearer for upper body); use only tested doses and watch the sleep cost.
- Sleep often gets worse before a big meet; protect sleep all week and don't panic about one night.
- A practised routine, one cue word and a handler who manages the logistics give the lifter the best chance on every attempt.
