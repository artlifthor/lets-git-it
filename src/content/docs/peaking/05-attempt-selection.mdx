## In one minute

A meet is won with **made lifts**, not attempted ones. Competition data show that elite lifters who go 9 for 9 typically open at about **91% of their final lift**, then jump about **5%**, then about **3%**, and that completing 8 or 9 of 9 attempts sharply raises the odds of winning, especially for men. The opener should be a weight the lifter could lift on a bad day (roughly a weight they could triple), the second a solid, confident lift, and the third chosen from how the second moved and what the lifter needs (a total, a placing, or a record). Third attempts succeed far less often than seconds, so big third-attempt jumps are where meets are lost. Plan every attempt before meet day, then adjust with RPE, the rules, and the scoreboard.

## What the competition data show

Several studies have analysed thousands of real meet results. They are observational: they show what successful lifters did, not that doing the same thing causes success.

- **Elite attempt patterns.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 109 elite classic lifters (66 men, 43 women) who made all nine lifts at IPF Classic World Championships (2012–2019), the typical pattern across lifts was an **opener of about 91% of the third attempt**, a jump of about **5%** to the second, and about **3%** to the third. Women opened relatively lower than men on bench press and deadlift and took relatively larger jumps on those lifts (Travis et al., 2021).
- **Openers and the day's best.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 10,672 raw entries from Australian meets (2008–2019), squat openers averaged about **92% of the best squat achieved that day**, and 93.5% of lifters improved on their opener. About 68% of winners made their third squat, compared with about 64% of non-winners. Winners opened their squat about 7 kg heavier on average (Howells et al., 2022), which mostly reflects that winners are stronger, not that a heavier opener wins meets.
- **Making lifts wins.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> At IPF World Championships (2013–2019; 1,312 lifters), completing **8 or 9 of 9 attempts** increased the odds of winning **5.9 times for men** and **1.5 times for women** (van den Hoek et al., 2023).
- **Jump size and success.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> An analysis of 93,333 lifters in 6,979 IPF competitions found that **third attempts consistently succeeded less often than second attempts**. For men, moderate squat and deadlift jumps (about 5–20 kg) went with higher success, but success fell with jumps over 20 kg, especially on thirds; bench press success dropped sharply beyond about 10 kg. For women, squat success fell with jumps over about 10 kg, bench press third-attempt success fell sharply beyond about 4 kg, and deadlift jumps of about 9–11 kg went with the highest success (Darragh et al., 2026). This is observational: lifters likely choose bigger jumps when the previous attempt moved well, which may partly explain why moderate jumps went with more success.
- **Placings are close.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In IPF classic open competitions (2010–2025), moving up one placing required on average about 3.6–9.5% more total, and many gaps between consecutive placings were within the normal day-to-day variation of a lifter's max (about 2% per lift in 16–19 regional-to-national male lifters). Gaps were hardest to separate from that normal variation around the podium (fourth to third for women, third to second for men), which the authors suggest reflects lifters clustering their attempts there (Amdi et al., 2026).

:::tip[In plain words]
Lifters who win usually make almost all of their lifts. The best lifters open at about 91% of what they finish with, then take a medium jump and then a small one. Third attempts are missed much more often than seconds, so that's where you need to be careful.
:::

## The opener

**Job:** get a good lift on the board, settle nerves, and set up the next two attempts.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Common coaching rules for the opener:

- A weight the lifter could **triple** on a normal day. On the RTS chart, a triple at RPE 9–10 sits at about 89–92% of 1RM, which matches the ~90–92% openers seen in competition data.
- A weight the lifter could lift on their **worst** day: after a bad night, a long wait, or a tough cut.
- A weight they have hit many times in training with competition commands, ideally confirmed in a mock meet ([Part 4 · Chapter 3](/lets-git-it/peaking/03-mock-meets-openers/)).
- In the warm-up room, an opener should feel like a single at about **RPE 7–8**. On the RTS chart, that is about 89–92% of 1RM.

<Evidence level="practice" /> The squat opener deserves the most caution. It is the first lift of the day, the one most affected by nerves and depth judging, and a miss puts pressure on every attempt after it. The bench press opener matters for the same reason within bench; if pause timing is new to the lifter, open lighter.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Opening too light has a smaller cost than opening too heavy. A light opener leaves a bigger jump to the second, which is usually fine; a missed heavy opener can leave the lifter repeating the same weight under pressure, and three misses on any lift means no total.

:::tip[In plain words]
Open with a weight you could do for three reps on a normal day, one you'd make even on your worst day. Getting that first lift is about confidence and staying in the meet, not about showing how strong you are.
:::

## The second attempt

**Job:** a confident, heavy lift that the lifter would be content to finish the meet with.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Typical choices:

- About **95–97%** of the expected best on the day, or about a **4–6% jump** from the opener (elite average about 5%; Travis et al., 2021).
- On the RTS chart, a single at RPE 8.5–9 is about 94–96% of 1RM. A second attempt that feels like RPE 8.5–9 is on target.
- Often a weight equal to or just above the lifter's previous competition best on that lift, so the meet produces a result even if the third is missed.

:::tip[In plain words]
Your second attempt should be heavy but sure: a weight you'd be happy to finish the meet on. It's usually about 5% more than your opener.
:::

## The third attempt and RPE-based jumps

**Job:** depends on the goal. The third is where the lifter chooses between a safe improvement and a maximal gamble.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Use the RPE of the second attempt to estimate the day's max, then pick the third from that estimate:

1. Rate the second attempt (bar speed, grind, how it felt). Video and the coach's view help.
2. Estimate today's 1RM: second-attempt weight ÷ RTS-chart percentage for a single at that RPE.
3. For a high-percentage third, choose about **98% of that estimate** (roughly a single at RPE 9.5). Choose the full estimate only when a placing or record demands it.
4. Round to the nearest 2.5 kg, and **down** when in doubt.

**Worked example: second attempt squat 177.5 kg, bench press 117.5 kg**

| Second attempt felt like | Single at that RPE (RTS) | Estimated max | High-percentage third (squat) | Estimated max | High-percentage third (bench) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| RPE 8 (fast) | 92.2% | ~192.5 kg | **187.5 kg** (+10) | ~127.5 kg | **125 kg** (+7.5) |
| RPE 8.5 | 93.9% | ~189 kg | **185 kg** (+7.5) | ~125 kg | **122.5 kg** (+5) |
| RPE 9 | 95.5% | ~186 kg | **182.5 kg** (+5) | ~123 kg | **120 kg** (+2.5) |
| RPE 9.5 (slow) | 97.8% | ~181.5 kg | **180 kg** (+2.5) only if needed | ~120 kg | **120 kg** (+2.5) only if needed |
| RPE 10 (grinder) | 100% | ~177.5 kg | Minimum jump only if the placing needs it; otherwise consider passing | ~117.5 kg | Same |

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> RPE ratings close to a max are not perfect: in 15 powerlifters, a true 1RM was rated about **9.6–9.7** on average rather than 10 (Helms et al., 2017). A second attempt rated "9" may be closer to a max than it feels, so be conservative when in doubt.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Meet-day adrenaline, the long wait between attempts and competition commands all change how a lift feels. Lifters who have practised rating singles in training, and coaches who have watched them do it, estimate better.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> If the second attempt was missed for a **technical** reason (early rack command, missed pause, a balance error) and the bar was moving well, repeating the weight is often right. If it was missed because it was too **heavy**, repeat the weight only if the total depends on it.

:::tip[In plain words]
How your second attempt felt tells you how much is left. Fast? Add 7.5–10 kg on squat or deadlift. Slow? Add the minimum, or stop there. When unsure, pick the lighter option: a made lift beats a missed one.
:::

## Strategy: total, placing or record?

Decide the meet's goal **before** meet day. Each goal changes the third attempts.

| Goal | Openers | Thirds | Typical lifter |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Experience and a total** (first meets) | Conservative: about 88–90% | High-percentage only; aim for 9/9 | Novices, sub-juniors |
| **Personal-best total** | About 90–92% | High-percentage on squat and bench; deadlift third chosen to hit the target total | Most intermediate lifters |
| **Placing / medal** | About 90–92% | Decided by the scoreboard; risk only where it changes the placing | Lifters in a close class |
| **Record** | Secure the total first | Record attempt on the lift that has the best chance | Lifters close to a national record |

<Evidence level="practice" /> For most lifters, most of the time, the best total comes from making lifts. The data showing that 8–9 of 9 successful attempts goes with winning (van den Hoek et al., 2023) support this as a default. Spend risk where it buys something: a placing, a qualifying total, or a record.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Because the gaps between placings are often small (Amdi et al., 2026), a single extra 2.5 kg on a third attempt can decide a medal. That is a reason to know the scoreboard, not a reason to take big jumps every time.

:::tip[In plain words]
Decide before the meet what you're chasing: a first total, a personal best, a medal, or a record. If it's a personal best, the safest path is to make as many lifts as possible. Only take a risk when it could win you something.
:::

## Rules that shape attempt tactics

<Evidence level="practice" /> These points summarise the IPF Technical Rules as commonly applied. Rules and time limits change; **check the current IPF Technical Rules** and the meet's own information before relying on any of them.

- **Openers** for all three lifts are submitted at **weigh-in**. Openers can usually be changed up to a short time before that lift's first round begins.
- After each attempt, the **next attempt** must be submitted within a short time limit (about 1 minute). Miss the deadline and the bar is loaded 2.5 kg heavier after a good lift, or at the same weight after a miss.
- Attempts go up by at least **2.5 kg** after a good lift. The bar can **never be loaded lighter** than the lifter's previous attempt on that lift. Record attempts can use smaller increments (at least 0.5 kg).
- Lifts are taken in **rounds**: everyone's first attempt, then everyone's second, then everyone's third. Within a round, the lightest bar goes first. A lifter whose third attempt is heavier than a rival's will lift **after** that rival and can see the result.
- The **deadlift third attempt** may usually be changed **twice**, up or down (but never lighter than the previous attempt), until the lifter is called to the bar. This is what makes final-round tactics possible.
- **Fourth attempts** for record purposes may be allowed in some competitions; they do not count toward the total. Check PAP and IPF rules for each meet.

:::tip[In plain words]
You hand in your first attempts at weigh-in, and after each lift you have about a minute to say what's next. The bar can only go up. On the last deadlift you can usually change your weight twice, which lets you react to your rivals. Always read the current rulebook.
:::

## Deadlift strategy for placings

The deadlift is the last lift of the day, so it decides most placings.

<Evidence level="practice" /> A simple process:

1. **Know the numbers.** Before the deadlift, list each rival's subtotal (squat + bench), their deadlift opener and their likely best. Assign someone (a coach or a friend) to track the scoreboard.
2. **Secure the position you have.** Pick a second attempt that keeps the current placing safe against the lifter behind.
3. **Calculate what's needed.** "To beat B, I need a total of X, which means a deadlift of X − my subtotal."
4. **Use the order.** If the lifter's third is heavier than a rival's, the rival lifts first. Submit a realistic third, watch the rival, then use the permitted changes to go up (the rival made it) or down (the rival missed).
5. **Respect the lifter.** Only ask for a weight the lifter has a real chance of making. A heroic third that has a 1-in-10 chance usually costs nothing for placing, but if a qualifying total or a record is also at stake, weigh that.

**Example (illustrative).** Lifter A, 83 kg class: squat 182.5 kg + bench press 120 kg = **302.5 kg** subtotal; expected best deadlift ~217.5 kg. Rival B: subtotal **310 kg**, best deadlift so far 207.5 kg, third submitted at 215 kg.

- If B makes 215 kg, B's total is 525 kg. A needs 222.5 kg (total 525 kg) to tie or 225 kg (527.5 kg) to win outright. Both are above A's expected best; check the current tie-break rule before relying on a tie.
- If B misses, B's total stays at 517.5 kg. A needs 215 kg (517.5 kg) to tie or **217.5 kg** to win: a realistic third.
- **Plan:** A submits 217.5 kg (heavier than B's 215 kg, so A pulls after B), watches B, then keeps 217.5 kg if B misses, or decides with the lifter whether a change to 222.5–225 kg is worth trying if B makes it, as long as A's second place is already safe.

:::tip[In plain words]
On the last deadlift, know exactly what your rivals have and what you need. Make sure you keep the place you already have, then use your last deadlift, and the chance to change it, to chase the place above if it's realistic.
:::

## Sample programs

Attempt plans for three lifters. "Expected best" means the lifter's realistic best on the day after peaking, judged from the last heavy singles and the mock meet. Percentages are of the expected best. All jumps follow the third-attempt rules above. The numbers are illustrative.

### Program 1: Intermediate man, 83 kg class, personal-best total

**Lifter:** squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg in training before the peak. Expected best on the day: squat 185 kg, bench press 122.5 kg, deadlift 217.5 kg. Goal: personal-best total, 9/9 if possible.

| Lift | Opener | Second | Third (if second ≈ RPE 8.5–9) | Third range by second-attempt RPE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Squat | 167.5 kg (~91%) | 177.5 kg (~96%) | 185 kg | 180–187.5 kg |
| Bench press | 112.5 kg (~92%) | 117.5 kg (~96%) | 122.5 kg | 120–125 kg |
| Deadlift | 197.5 kg (~91%) | 207.5 kg (~95%) | 217.5 kg | Chosen to reach the target total |
| **Total** | **477.5 kg** | **502.5 kg** | **525 kg** | |

**Why it's built this way:** the jumps (about 6% then about 4%) are close to the elite pattern. After the second attempts the lifter already has a 502.5 kg total, which is a personal best if his previous best was under that. The deadlift third is chosen from the running total: if squat and bench thirds were missed, the deadlift third can still be set to reach the target total, as long as the second pull moved well.

### Program 2: Female lifter, 63 kg class

**Lifter:** squat 120 kg, bench press 65 kg, deadlift 150 kg in training. Expected best: squat 122.5 kg, bench press 67.5 kg, deadlift 152.5 kg. Third meet; goal: 9/9 and a small personal best.

| Lift | Opener | Second | Third (if second ≈ RPE 8.5–9) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Squat | 110 kg (~90%) | 117.5 kg (~96%) | 122.5 kg | Squat jumps kept to 5–7.5 kg |
| Bench press | 60 kg (~89%) | 65 kg (~96%) | 67.5 kg | Third jump only 2.5 kg |
| Deadlift | 137.5 kg (~90%) | 145 kg (~95%) | 152.5 kg | 7.5 kg jumps; 10 kg if the second was fast |
| **Total** | **307.5 kg** | **327.5 kg** | **342.5 kg** | |

**Why it's built this way:** in competition data, women's third-attempt bench press success fell sharply with jumps above about 4 kg, and squat success fell with jumps above about 10 kg (Darragh et al., 2026). So the bench press third is the minimum 2.5 kg jump, and squat jumps stay modest. Elite women also opened bench press and deadlift relatively lower than men (Travis et al., 2021), which gives room for a confident second.

### Program 3: Lifter chasing a PAP national record

**Lifter:** junior man, 74 kg class. Expected best deadlift: 252.5 kg. Assume, for illustration only, that the national junior deadlift record in his class is **250.0 kg**. Always check the current records at [PAP records](/lets-git-it/pap-records/) and PAP's rules on how records are set and ratified.

| Attempt | Plan A: record on the third | Plan B: second felt heavy (RPE 9+) |
|---|---|---|
| Opener | 230 kg (~91%) | 230 kg |
| Second | 242.5 kg (~96%): secures the total | 242.5 kg |
| Third | **250.5 kg** (record attempt: at least 0.5 kg over the record) | 247.5 kg: a personal best, then the record attempt at a later meet or on a fourth attempt if the meet allows it |

**Rules before the meet:**

- Confirm that the meet is one where national records can be set, with the required referees and doping control, and what increments are allowed for a record attempt.
- Secure squat and bench press first. A record deadlift is worth nothing if the lifter has already bombed out.
- Use the record attempt only if the second pull was about RPE 8.5 or easier. By the RTS chart, 242.5 kg at RPE 8.5 suggests a max of about 258 kg, so 250.5 kg is about 97% and realistic. At RPE 9.5, the estimated max is about 248 kg, and 250.5 kg becomes a gamble.

**Why it's built this way:** a record attempt is a single, specific target. The opener and second are planned to make the total safe; the third becomes the record attempt only if the second moved well enough to make it likely.

## Common mistakes

- **Opening with a "show" weight.** A heavy opener missed under nerves can end the meet.
- **Taking the same jump on every lift.** Bench press jumps should usually be smaller, especially for lighter and female lifters.
- **Going for broke on every third.** Third attempts fail much more often than seconds; risk only where it buys something.
- **Choosing attempts on the spot.** Plan openers, seconds and a third-attempt range before meet day.
- **Ignoring the scoreboard.** Not knowing rivals' numbers before the deadlift wastes the most useful tactical moment of the day.
- **Not knowing the rules.** Missing the attempt deadline, or assuming the bar can go down after a miss.
- **Trusting meet-day RPE blindly.** Near a true max, lifters tend to rate slightly under 10; be conservative when unsure.

## Key takeaways

- Made lifts win meets: 8–9 of 9 successful attempts strongly raised the odds of winning at IPF World Championships, especially for men.
- Typical elite pattern: opener about 91% of the third, then about +5%, then about +3%.
- Opener: a weight the lifter could triple, about RPE 7–8 as a single in the warm-up room, about 90–92% of the expected best.
- Second: about 95–97% of the expected best; a weight the lifter would be happy to finish on.
- Third: estimate today's max from the second attempt's RPE (RTS chart), then choose about 98% of it for a high-percentage third.
- Women's bench press thirds in particular should use small jumps.
- Decide the meet's goal in advance; take risks only for a placing, a qualifying total or a record.
- Know the current IPF Technical Rules on attempt changes; the deadlift third can usually be changed twice.
