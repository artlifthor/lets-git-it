## In one minute

**Peaking** is the last few weeks of preparation that bring a lifter to the meet at their strongest; the **taper** is the final part of it, where training load is cut so fatigue clears while strength is kept. The best taper research comes from endurance and team sports: across those studies, cutting **volume by about 40–60%** while **keeping intensity and frequency** for **about 1–3 weeks** works best. Studies in strength athletes are few and small, but they point the same way: keep loads heavy, cut sets and reps, and do not stop training for long. Surveys of powerlifters show a very consistent real-world pattern: peak volume about 4–8 weeks out, peak intensity about 2 weeks out, a step taper of about 1–2 weeks with volume cut by roughly half, and the last session 2–4 days before the meet. Resting for a week or more before a meet is the most common way to get the taper wrong.

## Peaking, tapering and deloading: three different things

| Term | What it is | Typical length | Goal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Peaking** | The whole final phase before a meet: training becomes heavier, more specific, then lighter in volume | About 3–6 weeks | Be at your strongest on one day |
| **Taper** | The final part of the peak, where the training load is reduced on purpose | About 1–2 weeks in powerlifting (longer in endurance sports) | Let fatigue clear while keeping strength |
| **Deload** | A short easy period between training blocks | About a week | Clear fatigue so the **next block** can be hard |

<Evidence level="practice" /> A classic review defines the taper as a **progressive, nonlinear reduction of training load** over a variable time, to reduce the stress of daily training and optimise performance; its aim is to remove accumulated fatigue **without losing the adaptations** (Mujika & Padilla, 2003). A deload resets you for more training. A taper sets you up for the platform ([Part 2 · Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/building-programs/11-deloads-fatigue-management/)).

<Evidence level="practice" /> Peaking is also the last link in **phase potentiation**: muscle and work capacity from earlier blocks become strength in the specific-strength block, and the peak turns that strength into a heavy single on the day (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith). A peak cannot rescue a training year with no build-up behind it.

:::tip[In plain words]
Peaking is the last month or so before a meet, when training gets heavier and more like the competition. The taper is the last week or two of that, when you do much less so you arrive fresh. A deload is an easy week in the middle of the year; a taper is the easy part right before you compete.
:::

## Why a taper works: fitness and fatigue

<Evidence level="practice" /> The fitness–fatigue model treats performance as roughly **fitness minus fatigue** (Chiu & Barnes, 2003; see [Part 1 · Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/foundations/02-stress-recovery-adaptation/)). Hard training builds both. Fatigue clears faster than fitness, so cutting the training load for a short time lets fatigue drop while most of the fitness stays. Performance rises. Cut the load for too long and fitness starts to fade as well.

That gives the taper two jobs that pull against each other:

1. **Remove fatigue** — needs less training.
2. **Keep strength and skill** — needs enough heavy, specific training.

The answer in every line of evidence below is the same: **cut volume a lot, keep intensity, keep training often, and keep it short.**

**Overload before the taper.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> In a meta-analysis of 14 endurance studies, tapers that followed a planned period of **overload training** improved time-trial performance more than conventional tapers (Wang et al., 2023). <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 male powerlifters, bench press 1RM rose from about 147 kg after an overload week to about 156 kg after the taper that followed; no other differences between test sessions were significant, so the post-taper 1RM was not clearly higher than at baseline (Williams et al., 2020). <Evidence level="practice" /> In powerlifting terms: the taper reveals strength that hard training built; it cannot create strength that was never built.

:::tip[In plain words]
Hard training makes you stronger and more tired at the same time. Tiredness goes away faster than strength. So if you cut back for a short while before a meet, the tiredness disappears and your real strength shows. Cut back too long and the strength starts to fade too.
:::

## The parts of a taper

A taper is described by five variables:

| Variable | What it means | What the evidence and practice suggest |
|---|---|---|
| **Volume** | Total sets × reps | Cut it the most: about 40–60% |
| **Intensity** | Load (%1RM or RPE) | Keep it; heavy singles and doubles stay |
| **Frequency** | Sessions per week, per lift | Keep it, or reduce only a little |
| **Duration** | How long the reduction lasts | About 1–2 weeks for strength athletes |
| **Pattern** | How the reduction is applied | Step, linear, or exponential (below) |

**Taper patterns:**

- **Step taper:** volume drops in one go and stays low (for example, from 100% to 50% overnight).
- **Linear (progressive) taper:** volume falls by a similar amount each week or session.
- **Exponential taper:** volume falls quickly at first and then levels off (**fast decay**), or falls gradually and then sharply (**slow decay**).

<Evidence level="practice" /> Endurance research has generally favoured progressive (nonlinear) tapers over step tapers (Mujika & Padilla, 2003). Powerlifters, however, mostly use **step tapers** (Travis et al., 2021b; Winwood et al., 2026). With only 2–4 heavy sessions per lift in the final fortnight, a "progressive" taper in powerlifting usually looks like a short step anyway.

:::tip[In plain words]
In the last week or two, do far fewer sets and reps, but keep the weights heavy and keep training on most of your usual days. You can drop the work all at once (a step) or over two weeks (a slope). Both work; most powerlifters do the step.
:::

## What the meta-analyses show, and who was in them

The strongest taper evidence comes from **endurance and team sports**, not from powerlifting. Read the numbers with that in mind.

| Study | Who | Main finding |
|---|---|---|
| Bosquet et al., 2007 | Competitive athletes, 27 studies; competition or field-based performance | Best results with a **2-week** taper, volume cut **41–60%** in an exponential pattern, **intensity and frequency kept** |
| Wang et al., 2023 | Endurance athletes, 14 studies | Taper improved time-trial performance and time to exhaustion, not VO₂max; volume cut 41–60% with intensity and frequency kept, lasting up to 21 days, was effective |
| Vachon et al., 2021 | Team-sport athletes, 14 studies | Taper produced moderate improvements in maximal power, change of direction, repeated sprints and VO₂max |

- <Evidence level="strong" /> **In competitive athletes** (Bosquet et al., 2007) and **in endurance athletes** specifically (Wang et al., 2023), a short taper that cuts volume by roughly 40–60% while keeping intensity and frequency improves performance. <Evidence level="practice" /> A review of tapering for powerlifting noted that most taper research has focused on endurance sports such as swimming, cycling and running (Travis et al., 2020), so these numbers describe mainly endurance performance, not a 1RM.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **In team-sport athletes**, tapering improved maximal power with moderate certainty; the authors noted there were too few studies comparing taper strategies to make specific recommendations (Vachon et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> A narrative review estimated that a good taper improves performance by about **3%** on average (usual range 0.5–6%), again mainly from endurance data (Mujika & Padilla, 2003).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **There is no meta-analysis of tapering for maximal strength.** A review of tapering for powerlifting concluded that evidence for strength athletes is lacking and that coaches still rely heavily on trial and error (Travis et al., 2020).

:::tip[In plain words]
The big studies on tapering are mostly about runners, swimmers, cyclists and team-sport players. They agree: cut your training volume roughly in half, keep the intensity, and do it for one to three weeks. Nobody has yet pooled enough powerlifting studies to say exactly what works best for a 1RM, so we borrow these rules and check them against the smaller strength studies.
:::

## What strength and powerlifting studies show

These are single, small studies. Each is **emerging** evidence, but together they tell a consistent story.

**Reduce volume, keep intensity.**

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 8 resistance-trained men (elbow-flexor training), a 10-day taper with **high-intensity, low-volume training every second day** increased isometric strength throughout and kept slow-speed concentric strength higher for at least 8 days. Ten days of **complete rest** did not raise isometric strength, and slow-speed strength, after a brief rise on day 2, was lower by days 8–10 (Gibala et al., 1994).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> After 16 weeks of heavy training, 46 physically active men were split into groups. A 4-week **taper** (volume cut, intensity kept high) produced a further ~2% gain in arm and leg maximal strength, while 4 weeks of **complete detraining** cost 6–9% of maximal strength and more power (Izquierdo et al., 2007).

**Heavy or light during the taper?**

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 11 strength-trained men (crossover design), a taper week with volume cut by about 70% and loads **slightly raised** was compared with the same volume cut and loads **lowered**. There were no significant differences between tapers, but the heavier taper **tended** to produce more favourable changes in strength and power (Pritchard et al., 2019).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 13 track and field throwers aged 16–26, tapers with **heavy (85% 1RM)** or **light (30% 1RM)** resistance training improved throwing similarly, but leg press 1RM rose about 6% after the heavy taper and fell about 3% after the light one (Zaras et al., 2014).

**Step or exponential taper?**

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 16 powerlifters on a 6-week peaking program, a 1-week overreach followed by either a **1-week step taper** or a **3-week exponential taper** led to higher squat, bench press and deadlift 1RMs and totals over time. The abstract reports these as time effects; the only group difference reported was for one muscle fibre measure, which, like the other muscle measures, appeared to favour the step taper (Travis et al., 2021c). A 1-week step taper therefore looked at least as good as a 3-week taper.

**How many days off at the end?**

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 8 resistance-trained men, **3.5 or 5.5 days** of complete rest after 4 weeks of strength training did not reduce performance; isometric bench press force and jump height were higher than before training (Pritchard et al., 2018). Note that 1RM was not tested.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 powerlifters, a 1-week step taper followed by **2 days** off produced small increases in bench press, total and DOTS score. After **4 days** off, deadlift rose but bench press fell slightly; squat was unchanged in both groups. The authors concluded that a 1-week step taper followed by **2–4 days** off can maintain or improve maximal strength (Burke et al., 2023).

:::tip[In plain words]
The few studies on strong people say: keep lifting heavy weights in the taper, just much less of them. A one-week taper worked at least as well as a three-week one. Two to four days completely off at the very end is fine; a week or more of doing nothing is not.
:::

## What powerlifters actually do

Surveys and interviews describe practice, not what works best. They are labelled **coaching practice** for that reason. They are still useful because they are remarkably consistent.

| Source | Who | Peak volume | Peak intensity | Taper | Volume cut | Last session |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pritchard et al., 2016 | 11 elite New Zealand raw lifters (interviews) | ~5 weeks out | ~2 weeks out | Intensity kept or slightly reduced | ~59% | ~3.7 days out |
| Grgic & Mikulic, 2017 | 10 Croatian open-class champions (interviews) | — | ~8 days out | Step or fast-decay exponential | ~50% | ~3 days out |
| Travis et al., 2021b | 364 US and Canadian raw lifters (survey) | 5–8 weeks out | ~2 weeks out | Step taper over 7–10 days | 41–50% | All training stopped ~2.8 days out |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Further details from these surveys:

- **Last heavy lifts.** North American lifters did their final heavy (above 85% 1RM) squat and deadlift **7–10 days** out and their final heavy bench press **less than 7 days** out, at about **90–92.5% 1RM**. In the final session for each lift they dropped to about 75–80% for squat and bench and 70–75% for deadlift. Common taper set schemes were 3 × 2 (squat), 3 × 3 (bench) and 3 × 1 (deadlift) (Travis et al., 2021b).
- **Lift-specific rest.** The gap between the last session of a lift and the meet was longest for the deadlift (about 6 days), then squat and bench (about 4 days) (Travis et al., 2021b). A systematic review of seven survey and interview studies across the weightlifting sports found the same order: deadlifts last done 6–8 days out, squats 5–6.5 days, bench press 3–5 days (Winwood et al., 2026).
- **Specificity.** Accessories were removed about 2 weeks out, and lifters trained with competition equipment and movements (Pritchard et al., 2016; Grgic & Mikulic, 2017).
- **What goes wrong.** New Zealand lifters said tapers went badly when they took **a week or more** off (Pritchard et al., 2016). The systematic review linked poor tapers to errors in load management and to outside stress such as work, travel and nutrition (Winwood et al., 2026).
- **Other strength sports agree.** Strongman athletes (454 surveyed) mostly used a step taper of about 8–9 days, cut volume by about 45%, and stopped all training about 4 days out (Winwood et al., 2018).

:::tip[In plain words]
Powerlifters around the world taper in almost the same way. Their hardest volume is about a month or two out, their heaviest lifts about two weeks out, then they cut their training roughly in half for the last week or so and rest the last two to four days. Deadlifts stop first, bench press last.
:::

## Detraining: the cost of resting too long

The taper's danger is not too little rest; it is too much.

- <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 103 studies found that stopping resistance training reduces strength, and the loss grows with the length of the break; losses were larger in older and inactive people (Bosquet et al., 2013).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 power athletes who stopped training for **14 days**, bench press and squat did not change significantly, though eccentric knee strength fell and type II muscle fibres shrank by about 6% (Hortobágyi et al., 1993). Short breaks cost little 1RM, but they are not free.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> Four weeks of complete detraining cost 6–9% of maximal strength (Izquierdo et al., 2007).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> A narrative review of maintenance research concluded that strength can be maintained with **very low volume as long as relative load stays high** (Spiering et al., 2021). This is the logic of a taper: heavy, brief, frequent.

<Evidence level="practice" /> **Practical limits for a powerlifting taper:**

| Situation | Risk | Rule of thumb |
|---|---|---|
| 2–4 days with no lifting before the meet | Low; often helpful | Normal |
| 5–6 days off | Some lifters feel flat, especially on bench press | Do one short, light session 2–4 days out |
| 7 or more days off | Lifters report poor meets; loss of "feel" for heavy weights | Avoid |
| Taper longer than about 3 weeks for a 1RM meet | Strength and skill fade | Avoid unless injured or very fatigued |
| No heavy single in the last 2–3 weeks | Lifter unsure of the weights; openers guessed | Keep heavy singles until 1–2 weeks out |

:::tip[In plain words]
A few days of rest before a meet helps. A full week off usually hurts. Keep lifting heavy weights a few times a week right up to the last few days, just with very few sets.
:::

## Fitting the taper to the lifter

<Evidence level="practice" /> These adjustments come from coaching practice and the survey patterns above, not from controlled trials.

- **Longer taper (about 2 weeks)** for lifters who are stronger in absolute terms, heavier, older, coming off a very high-volume block, or carrying extra stress (work, a weight cut, travel). The surveys' 7–10 day tapers and Bosquet's 2-week figure frame the normal range.
- **Shorter taper (about 5–7 days)** for novices and lighter lifters who recover quickly, and for B or C meets where you do not want to lose training time ([Part 3 · Chapter 9](/lets-git-it/advanced/09-annual-planning/)).
- **Deadlift first, bench press last.** Lifters report that heavy deadlifts take longest to recover from (Pritchard et al., 2016), and they finish heavy deadlifts earliest (Travis et al., 2021b).
- **Learn from each meet.** Record the taper, how the lifter felt, and the result. After two or three meets you will know whether this lifter does better with a shorter or longer taper. Individual responses differ, and the meet log is your best data (Travis et al., 2020).

:::tip[In plain words]
Bigger, stronger, older or more stressed lifters usually need a slightly longer taper. Beginners and lighter lifters often need less. Write down every taper and how the meet went, so the next one is better.
:::

## Sample programs

Default lifter: intermediate man, ~83 kg, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg, training 4 days per week. RPE loads come from the RTS chart ([Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/)), rounded to 2.5 kg. All three meets are on a **Saturday**. These are taper templates; the full peaking blocks that lead into them are in [Chapter 2: Building a peaking block](/lets-git-it/peaking/02-building-a-peaking-block/).

### Program 1: One-week step taper after a heavy week

Modelled on the "overreach, then 1-week step taper, then 2–4 days off" design used in powerlifting research (Travis et al., 2021c; Burke et al., 2023). Week −2 is the hardest, heaviest week of the peak. Week −1 cuts sets by about half in one step.

**Week −2 (heaviest week)**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 1 × 1, then 3 × 2 | RPE 8.5 (~94%, 170 kg), then RPE 7.5 (~88%, 157.5 kg) |
| Mon | Bench press | 1 × 1, then 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~92%, 110 kg), then RPE 7 (~84%, 100 kg) |
| Tue | Bench press | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 (~81%, 97.5 kg) |
| Thu | Deadlift | 1 × 1, then 2 × 2 | RPE 8.5 (~94%, 197.5 kg), then RPE 7.5 (~88%, 185 kg) |
| Thu | Bench press | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 (~84%, 100 kg) |
| Sat | Squat | 3 × 2 | RPE 7.5 (~88%, 157.5 kg) |
| Sat | Bench press | 1 × 1, then 2 × 2 | RPE 8.5 (~94%, 112.5 kg), then RPE 7.5 (~88%, 105 kg) |

**Weekly sets:** squat 7, bench press 13, deadlift 3.

**Week −1 (taper week, meet Saturday)**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon (5 days out) | Squat | 1 × 1, then 2 × 2 | RPE 7 (~89%, 160 kg), then ~80% (145 kg) |
| Mon | Bench press | 1 × 1, then 2 × 2 | RPE 7 (~89%, 107.5 kg), then ~80% (95 kg) |
| Mon | Deadlift | 2 × 1 | ~75% (157.5 kg), easy |
| Wed (3 days out) | Squat | 2 × 1 | ~80% (145 kg), with commands |
| Wed | Bench press | 3 × 1 | ~85% (102.5 kg), with commands and a pause |
| Thu–Fri | Rest | — | Weigh-in plan, travel, sleep |
| Sat | Meet | — | — |

**Weekly sets:** squat 5, bench press 6, deadlift 2 (about 30–55% fewer than week −2, and the sets that remain are lighter). Accessories are removed. **Why it's built this way:** loads stay heavy enough to keep the feel of the bar, the sets that create fatigue are cut, the last heavy deadlift is 9 days out and the last training session is 3 days out, matching the survey data (Travis et al., 2021b).

### Program 2: Two-week progressive taper

For a lifter who is stronger, older, or coming off a high-volume block, who needs a little more time to shed fatigue. Volume falls in two steps instead of one. Week −3 is the reference (heaviest) week, similar to week −2 of Program 1.

| | Week −3 (reference) | Week −2 (taper, step 1) | Week −1 (taper, step 2, meet week) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Squat** | Single RPE 8.5–9 + 3 × 2; second day 3 × 2 (7 sets) | Single at planned opener + 2 × 2 RPE 7; second day 2 × 2 ~80% (5 sets) | 1 × 1 RPE 7 + 2 × 2 ~80% (Mon); 2 × 1 ~80% (Wed) (5 light sets) |
| **Bench press** | 4 days, ~13 sets | 4 days, ~10 sets; opener single on the last day | 2 days, ~6 sets |
| **Deadlift** | Single RPE 8.5–9 + 2 × 2 (3 sets) | Single at planned opener, no back-offs (Thu, 9 days out) | 2 × 1 ~75% (Mon) or none |
| **Accessories** | Reduced (1–2 exercises, 2 sets) | Removed or 1 upper-back exercise | Removed |
| **Volume vs. reference** | 100% | about 65–70% | about 35–40% |

**Why it's built this way:** the heaviest singles happen 2.5–3 weeks out, the openers are rehearsed 9–12 days out (see [Chapter 3: Mock meets and testing openers](/lets-git-it/peaking/03-mock-meets-openers/)), and fatigue is removed in two smaller steps. Intensity never falls below about 75–80% except on the deadlift in the last week.

### Program 3: Mini-taper for a B meet (5–6 days)

For a regional or club meet that is a stepping stone, not the main goal ([Part 3 · Chapter 9](/lets-git-it/advanced/09-annual-planning/), Program 2). The lifter trains normally until the week before, with one heavy single per lift at about RPE 8–8.5 in that week. There is no separate opener week.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon (5 days out) | Squat | 1 × 1, then 2 × 3 | RPE 7 (~89%, 160 kg), then ~80% (145 kg) |
| Mon | Bench press | 1 × 1, then 2 × 3 | RPE 7 (~89%, 107.5 kg), then ~80% (95 kg) |
| Tue (4 days out) | Deadlift | 1 × 1, then 1 × 2 | ~85% (177.5 kg), then ~75% (157.5 kg) |
| Wed (3 days out) | Bench press | 3 × 2 | ~80% (95 kg), with commands |
| Thu–Fri | Rest | — | — |
| Sat | B meet | — | Openers about RPE 7–8, conservative thirds |

**Why it's built this way:** volume drops about half for one week only, so the lifter loses very little training time. Monday's squat single is at RPE 7, below the heavy-work threshold, so Tuesday's deadlift on the next day is not a clash; the deadlift itself is moderate (~85% for a single is below RPE 7). The lifter goes back to the main training block a few days after the meet.

## Common mistakes

- **Resting a week or more before the meet.** Lifters lose the feel for heavy weights and often perform worse. Keep training, lightly, until 2–4 days out.
- **Dropping intensity with volume.** A taper of light sets at 60–70% keeps fatigue low but also lets strength and confidence fade. Keep heavy singles and doubles.
- **Cutting volume too little.** Doing the normal program "but lighter" leaves fatigue in place. Remove sets, especially back-off sets and accessories.
- **Tapering too long.** Two to three weeks of very low volume after a short block can cost more fitness than it saves fatigue.
- **Testing true maxes in the last 1–2 weeks.** All-out attempts create fatigue and risk injury exactly when you want neither; test openers instead (Chapter 3).
- **Treating endurance taper numbers as powerlifting rules.** The 41–60% volume cut and 2-week duration come mostly from endurance research. Use them as a starting point and adjust from the lifter's own meet history.
- **Ignoring life stress.** Work, travel, and weight cuts are part of the load. A lifter with a hard week at work needs less training, not more.

## Key takeaways

- A taper cuts training load before a meet so fatigue clears while strength remains; it is the last part of a 3–6 week peak.
- Meta-analyses in endurance and team-sport athletes support cutting volume about 40–60% while keeping intensity and frequency, over about 1–3 weeks; there is no meta-analysis for maximal strength.
- Small studies in strength athletes agree: heavy, low-volume training beats both light tapers and complete rest, and a 1-week step taper worked at least as well as a 3-week one.
- Powerlifters typically peak volume 4–8 weeks out and intensity about 2 weeks out, use a 7–10 day step taper with volume cut by about half, finish heavy deadlifts first and bench press last, and stop training 2–4 days out.
- Resting a week or more before the meet is a common cause of poor tapers; a short break of 2–4 days is fine.
- Record every taper and its result; the lifter's own history is the best guide for the next one.
