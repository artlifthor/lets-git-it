## In one minute

The meet is not the end of the cycle; it is the most valuable test the lifter will take all year. Afterwards, take **1–2 weeks of reduced training**, not a long complete break: short breaks cost little strength, longer ones cost more. Within a few days, run a **debrief** while memories and videos are fresh: attempt selection, technique and red lights, how well the peak worked, and the weight cut. Use the meet results to set **new training maxes**, a little below the meet bests, and plan the next block around what the debrief found. Expect a dip in motivation or mood for some days; it is common and usually passes, but persistent low mood deserves professional help.

## What a meet costs

<Evidence level="practice" /> There is almost no research on recovery after a powerlifting meet itself, so most of this section is coaching practice. The physical training load of a meet is small: nine heavy singles plus warm-ups, after a taper. The **total stress of the meet week** is larger:

| Stressor | Typical effect | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Maximal singles (RPE 9–10) | Joint and connective-tissue soreness, especially after the deadlift; "beat up" feeling | 3–7 days of light training |
| Weight cut and rehydration | Low energy, poor sleep, hunger; worse with bigger cuts | Normal eating from the evening after the meet |
| Long day, nerves, caffeine | Poor sleep the night after; fatigue next day | Rest, an early night, no hard training for a few days |
| Travel | Fatigue, disturbed sleep and routine | Rest days before training resumes |
| Emotional load | Relief, disappointment or elation, then a "flat" feeling | Talk it through, plan the next step (below) |

:::tip[In plain words]
A meet is only nine hard lifts, but the whole week (cutting weight, nerves, a long day, travel) takes a lot out of you. Plan a few easy days afterwards.
:::

## Recovery after competition

**Short breaks cost little; long breaks cost more.**

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 power athletes who stopped training for 14 days, bench press (−1.7%) and squat (−0.9%) did not change significantly, while eccentric knee strength fell by 12% and type II muscle fibres shrank by about 6% (Hortobágyi et al., 1993).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 8 trained men, 3.5 or 5.5 days off after a 4-week strength block did not reduce strength; isometric bench press force and jump height were higher than before training (Pritchard et al., 2018).
- <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 103 studies found that stopping resistance training reduces strength, with larger losses the longer the break lasts, and larger losses in older people (Bosquet et al., 2013).

**Recovery methods.**

- <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 99 studies found that active recovery, massage, compression garments, cold-water immersion, contrast water therapy and cryotherapy reduced muscle soreness; **massage was the most effective** for soreness and perceived fatigue (Dupuy et al., 2018).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis (8 studies) found that **regular** cold-water immersion during a training program reduced strength gains (Malta et al., 2021). <Evidence level="practice" /> A single ice bath after a meet is a different situation from using it after every session; there is no need for it, but there is little reason to fear it either.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Sleep and food come first. An expert panel of 15 bodybuilding and powerlifting coaches and dietitians agreed that powerlifters need less restrictive post-competition eating than bodybuilders, with most returning to off-season training within 1–2 weeks (Buechel et al., 2026). In practice, a lifter who cut weight should go back to normal meals straight away rather than keep dieting.

**Decision rules** <Evidence level="practice" />

1. **Days 1–3:** no barbell training. Walk, sleep, eat normally, and enjoy the result.
2. **Days 4–14:** light training (RPE 5–7), shorter sessions, more variety (see the sample programs).
3. **Older lifters** and lifters coming off a large cut or a long trip: lean toward the longer end.
4. **Pain that does not settle within 1–2 weeks,** or any sharp, worsening or night pain: see a qualified health professional. Training around pain is covered in Part 5, Chapter 10 (Programming around injury and returning to heavy training).

:::tip[In plain words]
Take a few days completely off the barbell, then train lightly for a week or two. A short break won't make you weaker, but a long one will start to. Sleep, eat normally, and get a massage if it helps you feel better.
:::

## The meet debrief

<Evidence level="practice" /> Run the debrief within a week, with the videos, the attempt cards and the lifter's notes. Separate **what happened** (facts) from **why** (interpretation) and **what we'll change** (decisions). Keep each decision small and testable.

### 1. Attempt selection

- How many attempts were made (out of 9)? Which misses were **strength** misses and which were **technical or rules** misses?
- How did the opener feel (RPE)? Did the jumps match the plan in [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/peaking/05-attempt-selection/)?
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> As a benchmark: elite classic lifters who made all nine lifts at IPF World Championships (109 lifters) opened, on average, at about 91% of their third attempt, then jumped about 5% and then about 3% (Travis et al., 2021). Large gaps from this pattern are worth a question, not automatically a mistake.

### 2. Technique and red lights

- List every red light with its card colour ([Chapter 7](/lets-git-it/peaking/07-competition-rules/)). Depth, commands and lockout misses are training problems, not meet-day bad luck.
- Compare the heaviest attempts with training videos: where did bar path, speed or position break down?

### 3. The peak

- Were the openers and seconds at the expected RPE? Faster than expected suggests the peak worked or the openers were conservative; slower suggests too much fatigue, too little intensity, or a bad day.
- How did the meet results compare with the heaviest training singles and the e1RMs from the peaking block ([Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/peaking/02-building-a-peaking-block/))?
- What would you change in the taper: length, volume cut, timing of the last heavy singles ([Chapter 1](/lets-git-it/peaking/01-peaking-and-tapering/))?

### 4. The weight cut

- How much was lost, over how long, and how did the lifter feel on the platform?
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 53 elite powerlifters at IPF World Championships, who lost on average 2.5% of body mass in the final week, larger relative losses were associated with worse self-reported stress and recovery scores; female lifters reported worse negative emotional state with larger losses (Kwan et al., 2026). This was an exploratory study; it shows a link, not cause and effect.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> In a survey of 321 competitive powerlifters, about 86% reported rapid weight loss before competition, averaging about 3% of body mass (Nolan et al., 2022). If the cut felt hard, consider a slower, earlier approach next time ([Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/peaking/04-making-weight/)) or a different weight class.

### 5. Meet-day logistics

- Warm-up timing, food, caffeine, sleep, handler communication, equipment problems ([Chapter 6](/lets-git-it/peaking/06-meet-day/)).

**Debrief template** (filled in for the default lifter)

| Area | What happened | Interpretation | Decision for next cycle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Squat | 165 ✓ (RPE 7), 175 ✓ (RPE 8), 182.5 ✓ (RPE 9.5) | Peak worked; jumps as planned | Keep the plan; open the same way |
| Bench press | 110 ✓ (RPE 7.5), 115 ✓ (RPE 8.5), 120 ✗ (failed lockout) | Slower than expected; strength miss mid-range | Next block: more bench volume and a mid-range variation |
| Deadlift | 192.5 ✓ (RPE 7), 205 ✓ (RPE 8.5), 215 ✓ (RPE 9.5) | Peak worked | Keep the plan |
| Weight | 83 kg class; no cut (82.2 kg at weigh-in) | No cost | Stay in the 83 kg class |
| Logistics | Bench warm-ups started late; rushed last warm-up | Handler lost track of Flight B | Handler uses the flight sheet, counting attempts |
| **Result** | **Total 512.5 kg (8/9)** vs 510 kg training maxes | A small personal best | — |

:::tip[In plain words]
A few days after the meet, sit down with your coach, the videos and your notes. Go through your attempts, your red lights, how the last weeks of training worked, and your weight cut. Write down what to keep and one or two things to change next time.
:::

## Setting new training maxes from meet results

<Evidence level="practice" /> A meet best is a true 1RM, but it was made **peaked, rested, and on a big day**. The first block after a meet starts **unpeaked and after a break**, so planning from the full meet number makes early sessions too heavy.

**Rules**

1. **Start from the best evidence of today's max.** Use the best made attempt, or an e1RM from a made attempt at RPE 8.5 or above with the RTS chart ([Part 1, Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/)). If a heavier attempt was **missed**, don't let the e1RM go above the missed weight.
2. **Percentage-based programs:** set the training max at about **90–95% of that number** for the first block.
3. **RPE-based programs:** no training max is needed. Use the meet numbers only to estimate starting loads; the first week's sets at RPE 7–8 give fresh e1RMs.
4. **If the meet went badly** (a bad day, a hard cut, illness), don't lower training maxes below the pre-meet training numbers; use those instead.

| Lift | Best made | RPE | e1RM from chart | Cap from misses | Starting number | Training max (~95%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Squat | 182.5 kg | 9.5 | 182.5 ÷ 0.978 ≈ 186.5 kg | None | ~185 kg | ~175 kg |
| Bench press | 115 kg | 8.5 | 115 ÷ 0.939 ≈ 122.5 kg | Missed 120 kg | ~117.5 kg | ~112.5 kg |
| Deadlift | 215 kg | 9.5 | 215 ÷ 0.978 ≈ 220 kg | None | ~217.5 kg | ~207.5 kg |

**Why the bench starts below the e1RM.** The chart suggests about 122.5 kg, but 120 kg was missed on the day. The miss is the better evidence, so 117.5 kg is used. The squat and deadlift e1RMs are rounded down to keep the first block conservative.

:::tip[In plain words]
Your meet lifts were done at your very best, after weeks of preparation. Your next training block starts from a normal, unpeaked state, so base it on numbers a little below your meet bests. If you missed a weight, don't assume you can lift it yet.
:::

## Planning the next cycle

<Evidence level="practice" /> The debrief decides the next block, and the meet calendar decides its length ([Part 3, Chapter 9](/lets-git-it/advanced/09-annual-planning/)).

| Time to the next meet | Plan after the transition |
|---|---|
| **16+ weeks** | Full cycle: accumulation/hypertrophy block, strength block, peak. Best time to change technique or weight class |
| **10–15 weeks** | Shorter accumulation, then strength and peak |
| **6–9 weeks** | One strength block, then a short peak; no major technique changes |
| **Under 6 weeks** | Mostly maintain; short re-peak; treat the first meet as the "real" peak and the second as a bonus |

**From debrief to program** <Evidence level="practice" />

- **Strength misses** → more volume or a specific variation for that lift (see [Part 3, Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/advanced/05-weak-point-analysis/)).
- **Rules misses** (depth, commands) → commands in every heavy set from week 1 ([Chapter 7](/lets-git-it/peaking/07-competition-rules/)).
- **Peak too flat or too fatigued** → adjust taper length or volume cut next time; change one thing.
- **Hard weight cut** → plan a slower bodyweight change in the off-season, or move class.
- **Everything worked** → repeat the structure with slightly higher training maxes.

:::tip[In plain words]
Use what you learned at the meet to plan your next training block. If you missed a lift because you weren't strong enough, train that weakness. If you missed it because of the rules, practise the rules. If everything worked, do it again with slightly heavier weights.
:::

## The post-meet dip

<Evidence level="practice" /> Many lifters feel flat, unmotivated or low after a meet, whether it went well or badly. After weeks of focus on one date, the goal is suddenly gone. Strength can also feel lower in the first sessions back, because the lifter is no longer peaked and has had time off. Good research on this in powerlifters is lacking, so the following is coaching practice.

**What helps**

- **Expect it.** Tell the lifter in advance that a flat week is normal.
- **Set the next goal early,** within 1–2 weeks: a meet date, a technique goal, or a bodyweight goal.
- **Make training fun and different** in the transition: new exercises, higher reps, some sport or conditioning.
- **Celebrate what went well** before fixing what didn't.
- **Judge strength after 3–4 weeks,** not in the first week back.

**Red flags: refer, don't coach.** If low mood, loss of interest in things the lifter used to enjoy, sleep problems or changes in eating last more than about two weeks, or if the lifter talks about self-harm, encourage them to see a doctor or mental health professional. In an emergency, contact local emergency services straight away.

:::tip[In plain words]
Feeling flat after a meet is common, even after a good one. Have a new goal ready, keep training light and fun for a while, and don't judge your strength in the first week back. If you feel low for more than a couple of weeks, talk to a doctor or counsellor.
:::

## Sample programs

Transition plans for the 1–2 weeks after a meet. All work is at **RPE 7 or lower**, so there is no heavy squat or heavy deadlift day. Loads are based on the starting numbers in the table above (default lifter: squat ~185, bench ~117.5, deadlift ~217.5 kg) and the RTS chart.

### Program 1: Two-week transition after the main meet (default lifter)

**Week 1: active rest** (no sessions on days 1–3)

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thu | Squat | 3 × 5 | ~60% (~110 kg) |
| Thu | Dumbbell bench press | 3 × 10 | Easy, about 3–4 reps in reserve |
| Thu | Rows, leg curls, back extensions | 2 × 12–15 each | Easy |
| Sat | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | Light, about 4 reps in reserve |
| Sat | Bench press | 3 × 8 | ~60% (~70 kg) |
| Sat | Walking, cycling or another sport | 20–40 min | Easy |

**Week 2: re-entry**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~145 kg) |
| Mon | Bench press | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) |
| Mon | Leg press, rows | 3 × 10 each | RPE 7–8 |
| Wed | Bench press variation for the next block (e.g. Spoto press) | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| Wed | Accessories | 2–3 × 10–15 | RPE 7–8 |
| Fri | Deadlift | 2 × 5 | RPE 7 (~170 kg) |
| Fri | Bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 (~90 kg) |

**Why it's built this way.** Week 1 removes heavy loading but keeps the movement patterns, in line with research showing that short breaks cost little strength (Hortobágyi et al., 1993; Pritchard et al., 2018). Week 2 returns to the competition lifts at moderate effort with about half the usual sets, and starts the bench variation chosen in the debrief. The next block begins in week 3.

### Program 2: One-week reset after a stepping-stone meet

For a lifter who did not cut weight and used the meet as a checkpoint on the way to a bigger meet in 8–10 weeks. Sessions are short and the next strength block starts the following Monday.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sun–Mon | Off | — | — |
| Tue | Squat | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 (~150 kg) |
| Tue | Bench press | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| Thu | Deadlift | 2 × 3 | RPE 7 (~182.5 kg) |
| Thu | Bench press (close grip) | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| Sat | Squat (pause) | 2 × 3 | ~70% of squat (~130 kg) |
| Sat | Bench press | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) |
| Sat | Accessories | 2 × 10–12 | RPE 7–8 |

**Why it's built this way.** With the next meet close, a long transition would waste time. Two rest days, then a week at RPE 7 with about half the usual sets lets fatigue from the meet clear while keeping the skills sharp. The squat and deadlift days are two days apart, and none of the work is heavy.

### Program 3: Recovery-first transition after a hard cut (female lifter)

Female lifter, 63 kg class, who cut about 3% of body mass and found it hard. Meet bests: squat 117.5, bench 62.5, deadlift 145 kg. She also has sore knees after the meet.

**Week 1**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Days 1–4 | No barbell training; daily walks, normal meals, sleep | — | — |
| Fri | Leg press, hip thrust, rows, dumbbell press | 3 × 10–12 each | About 3–4 reps in reserve |
| Sun | Squat (to a box at depth, slow) | 3 × 5 | ~60% (~70 kg) |
| Sun | Bench press | 3 × 8 | ~60% (~37.5 kg) |

**Week 2**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tue | Squat | 3 × 5 | ~65% (~75 kg) |
| Tue | Bench press | 3 × 6 | ~65% (~40 kg) |
| Thu | Deadlift | 3 × 4 | ~65% (~95 kg) |
| Thu | Accessories | 2 × 12 | RPE 7–8 |
| Sat | Squat | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) |
| Sat | Bench press | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~50 kg) |

**Why it's built this way.** A larger cut is linked to worse stress and recovery scores around competition (Kwan et al., 2026), and the expert panel above considered that powerlifters need less restrictive post-competition eating than bodybuilders (Buechel et al., 2026). So the first days are about food, sleep and walking. The barbell returns gradually, and squat loads only climb to RPE 7 once her knees feel normal; if they do not settle within 1–2 weeks, she should see a qualified health professional. The debrief question for her next cycle is whether to cut less or move up a class.

## Common mistakes

- **Taking a month off** after every meet. Short breaks cost little; long ones mean weeks of rebuilding.
- **Testing maxes in the first week back** to "see if strength is still there". It isn't a fair test, and it adds fatigue.
- **Skipping the debrief,** or doing it only in the emotional hours after the meet.
- **Changing everything** after one meet. Change one or two things and keep what worked.
- **Setting training maxes from the full meet best** and starting the next block too heavy.
- **Continuing to diet after a cut** instead of returning to normal eating.
- **Ignoring pain or persistent low mood** because "it's just the post-meet dip".

## Key takeaways

- Take 1–2 weeks of reduced training after a meet; short breaks cost little strength, long ones cost more.
- Sleep, normal eating and light activity do most of the recovery work; massage helps soreness.
- Debrief within a week: attempts, red lights, peak, weight cut and logistics; write down decisions.
- Set new training maxes at about 90–95% of the best evidence of today's max, capped by any missed weight.
- Let the debrief and the next meet date shape the next block.
- A post-meet dip is common; set the next goal early, and refer if low mood persists.
