## In one minute

Most of a weight class should be made **slowly, over months**, by dieting at about 0.5–1% of bodyweight per week; only a **small final adjustment** belongs in the last days before a meet. Surveys show that most tested, raw powerlifters do cut weight before a meet, on average about 3% of body mass, mostly by restricting fluid and by water loading. Strength studies show that a modest cut followed by about 2 hours of recovery can leave 1RM strength unchanged, but being dehydrated while lifting costs strength, and with a weigh-in only about 2 hours before lifting there is not enough time to fully rehydrate from a large cut. Keep acute cuts small (about 2–3% at most for experienced adults, less or none for novices and youth), never use saunas, sweat suits, diuretics or laxatives, and take heat seriously. A good weigh-in plan is boring, rehearsed, and leaves the lifter feeling normal on the platform.

## Chronic vs. acute weight loss


| | **Chronic weight loss (dieting)** | **Acute weight loss ("cutting", "making weight")** |
|---|---|---|
| **Time frame** | Weeks to months | The last few days, mostly the last 24 hours |
| **What is lost** | Mainly body fat (some muscle if too fast) | Mainly water and gut contents; some glycogen |
| **Comes back after weigh-in?** | No; it is a real change in body composition | Partly, if there is enough time to eat and drink |
| **Main risk** | Losing muscle and strength if too fast; low energy availability | Dehydration, heat illness, weaker lifting, gut problems |
| **Typical safe size** | About 0.5–1% of bodyweight per week | Small: about 2–3% at most with a 2-hour weigh-in (see below) |

- <Evidence level="practice" /> The ACSM expert consensus on weight-category sports describes athletes combining **chronic strategies (body-fat loss)** with **acute manipulations over hours to days** before weigh-in. It recommends choosing a competition weight based on body composition well before the season and getting help from a sports nutrition professional, and supports banning unhealthy weight-loss methods (Burke et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> Slow dieting protects strength and muscle: in 24 elite athletes training with weights, losing about 0.7% of bodyweight per week allowed lean-mass gains while a faster rate (about 1% per week achieved) did not (Garthe et al., 2011). Rates of loss are covered in [Part 1 · Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/foundations/11-nutrition/).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Dieting hard over long periods can lead to low energy availability. Missing periods, frequent illness, stress fractures or constant fatigue are warning signs that need a doctor or sports dietitian (Mountjoy et al., 2023).

:::tip[In plain words]
There are two kinds of weight loss. Dieting slowly over months takes off fat and is the real way to move into a lower class. Cutting in the last days only takes off water and food in your gut; it comes back, and it can make you weaker if you overdo it. Do almost all of it the slow way.
:::

## Choosing a weight class

The detailed decision table is in [Part 3 · Chapter 10](/lets-git-it/advanced/10-special-populations/). For meet planning, three questions decide it:

1. **Where is the lifter's walking weight?** Use the weekly average of morning weights, not one good day.
2. **How far is that from the class limit?** Express it as a percentage: (walking weight − limit) ÷ limit × 100.
3. **How much time is left?** Each week of dieting at 0.5–1% per week is worth about 0.4–0.8 kg for an 83 kg lifter.

| Weekly average vs. class limit (with 8+ weeks to go) | Plan |
|---|---|
| At or under the limit | No cut. Eat and drink normally. |
| 0–2% over | Small final-week adjustment only (Program 2). |
| 2–5% over | Diet slowly into range first, then a small final adjustment (Program 1). |
| More than 5% over, few weeks left | Compete in the heavier class this time. Move down later, slowly, if it makes sense. |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Muscle drives the total; extra fat and water do not. A lifter who is already lean and sits well above a class limit usually does better staying in the heavier class and growing into it.

:::tip[In plain words]
Pick the class that matches your normal weight, or one you can diet into slowly. If you'd need a big last-minute cut, lift in the heavier class this time.
:::

## What the evidence says about cutting

### How common it is in powerlifting

<Evidence level="practice" /> In an anonymous survey of 321 competitive powerlifters (194 men, 127 women), mostly from drug-tested, raw federations with weigh-ins 2 hours or less before lifting, about **86% reported using rapid weight loss**, losing on average about **3% of body mass**. "Always" using fluid restriction was reported by 54% and water loading by 49%. Coaches and online sources were the main influences; doctors and dietitians were rarely influential. The overall weight-cutting scores were lower than those reported in combat sports, which have longer recovery after weigh-in (Nolan et al., 2022).

So cutting is normal in the sport. That does not mean it is free, or that bigger is better.

### Does a cut make powerlifters weaker?

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Powerlifting trial.** In a randomised controlled trial, 26 trained male powerlifters were split into a control group or a 4-day rapid weight-loss protocol (a small calorie cut, under 50 g carbohydrate per day, low sodium, and fluid manipulation). The cut group lost about **4.8% of body mass**; the measured losses included fat-free mass and body water. After a **2-hour recovery**, squat, bench press, deadlift and total were not significantly different from before, and IPF GL points rose because bodyweight fell while strength held (Matras et al., 2025). This is one small study of men in a lab setting with a controlled protocol, not proof that a 5% cut is safe or wise for everyone.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Weightlifting.** In 11 international weightlifters, a 6-day food restriction that cut about 4.3% of bodyweight (energy intake down about 40%) did **not** reduce weightlifting performance, but it worsened recovery scores and increased emotional stress and physical complaints, and intakes of several vitamins and magnesium fell below recommendations (Durguerian et al., 2016).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Combat sports.** A meta-analysis of weight cutting in combat-sport athletes found **small reductions in maximal strength** immediately after rapid weight loss. Comparing before the cut with 3–36 hours after recovery and regaining weight, overall performance was unchanged; whether every outcome fully recovers after rapid weight gain remained unclear (Brechney et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Judo.** A systematic review of 14 studies (1,103 judo athletes) on cuts of 5% or more within a week found conflicting results for physical performance but consistent mood effects: more tension, anger and fatigue, and less vigour (Lakicevic et al., 2020).

### Why lifting while dehydrated costs strength

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis found that being under-hydrated reduced muscle strength by about **5.5%** on average, with smaller losses in trained people and when water was lost passively rather than by exercise (Savoie et al., 2015).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 7 resistance-trained men dehydrated by about 2.5% and 5% of body mass through exercise in the heat, peak force and power in single efforts did not change significantly, but **performance over a six-set back squat session fell**, earlier and more at the larger deficit (Judelson et al., 2007).

<Evidence level="practice" /> Putting it together: small cuts with recovery time seem to cost little or nothing on the platform; large cuts, cuts made by sweating, and lifting while still dehydrated carry real costs. A powerlifting meet also lasts hours, with nine attempts and many warm-up sets, so the lifter must be able to perform well late in the day, not only on the first squat.

:::tip[In plain words]
Most powerlifters cut a little water before a meet, and a small cut with time to drink and eat afterward doesn't seem to hurt their strength. But lifting while still dried out makes you weaker, especially as the meet goes on, and big or sweat-based cuts are where trouble starts.
:::

## What a 2-hour weigh-in really allows

<Evidence level="practice" /> Under the IPF Technical Rules, weigh-in for a session starts **2 hours before** that session begins and lasts about **1.5 hours**, so a lifter who weighs in late may have well under 2 hours before warming up. Lifters also hand in their opening attempts at weigh-in. Check the current IPF Technical Rules and the meet's schedule, because details can change (IPF Technical Rules).

How much fluid can come back in that window?

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 men dehydrated by about 2% of body mass, rehydration was tracked for 7.5 hours. To restore fluid balance, they had to drink **more than they had lost**, and unless the drink contained enough **sodium**, much of the extra fluid was simply passed as urine (Shirreffs et al., 1996).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In contrast, with 15–20 hours between weigh-in and the first fight, 86 judo athletes regained about 2–3% of body mass on average (Reale et al., 2016). Powerlifters do not get that time.

<Evidence level="practice" /> With about 2 hours, you can refill the stomach and gut, top up some fluid and carbohydrate, and feel better. You cannot reliably undo a large dehydration. That is why acute cuts for a 2-hour weigh-in should stay small:

| Lifter | Suggested maximum acute cut (with a 2-hour weigh-in) |
|---|---|
| Sub-junior (14–18) | **None.** Compete at natural bodyweight. |
| Novice, first meets | None, or about 1% from gut contents only |
| Experienced adult, rehearsed plan | About 2–3%, mostly from gut contents and a short fluid reduction |
| Anyone needing more than ~3% | Diet longer, or move up a class |

These limits are coaching practice, built from the evidence above; no study has set an exact safe ceiling for powerlifters.

:::tip[In plain words]
Two hours is enough to eat a meal and drink some fluid, but not enough to fully recover from drying yourself out. So the cut has to be small enough that you can lift normally with only partial recovery.
:::

## The tools for a small cut


### 1. Gut contents: low-residue (low-fibre) eating

<Evidence level="practice" /> Food and fibre in the gut weigh something. A combat-sport review describes athletes restricting food and fluid to reduce body water and **gut contents** before weigh-in, and notes that the time available after weigh-in decides how much acute loss can be reversed (Reale et al., 2017). No study has measured exactly how much a low-fibre diet removes in powerlifters, so treat any number as an estimate and test it yourself before a meet.

- Start **2–3 days out**. Swap high-fibre foods (beans, monggo, leafy vegetables, whole grains, large salads, high-fibre fruit) for low-fibre ones (white rice, white bread, eggs, chicken, fish, bananas, juice).
- Keep protein and energy reasonable; you are reducing bulk, not starving.
- This is the safest tool, because it costs no water and returns quickly once the lifter eats.

### 2. Glycogen and sodium: modest, short changes

<Evidence level="practice" /> Glycogen (stored carbohydrate) is stored with water, so a short cut in carbohydrate lowers body water a little. Lower salt intake can also reduce water held in the body. The one powerlifting trial used both, together with fluid changes, and strength held after 2 hours of recovery (Matras et al., 2025), but no study has tested these methods on their own in lifters.

- Keep it short (1–3 days) and modest. Avoid very salty processed foods (instant noodles, dried fish, soy sauce, chips) in the last 2 days rather than trying to remove salt entirely.
- After weigh-in, **put sodium and carbohydrate straight back in**.

### 3. Body water: water loading and fluid restriction

**Fluid restriction** means drinking less in roughly the last 12–24 hours. **Water loading** means drinking large volumes for several days, then cutting back sharply, on the theory that the body keeps passing extra urine for a while after intake drops.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 21 male combat-sport athletes on a controlled diet, drinking 100 mL/kg per day for 3 days before restricting fluid produced about **0.6% more body-mass loss** than drinking 40 mL/kg. Blood electrolytes stayed within normal ranges and performance did not differ. The authors judged it safe and effective **under the study's conditions** (Reale et al., 2018).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> 100 mL/kg is a lot: about 8.3 L per day for an 83 kg lifter. Drinking that much plain water, especially with low salt intake, carries a risk of low blood sodium (hyponatraemia), which can be dangerous. The study was supervised and diet-controlled; a lifter doing it alone is not. The extra gain is small (about 0.5 kg for an 83 kg lifter).

**Our position:** <Evidence level="practice" /> For most lifters, a modest fluid reduction in the last day is enough. Water loading is optional, adds little, and should be considered only by experienced adult lifters who have rehearsed it, keep salt intake normal while loading, and stop at any sign of headache, nausea or confusion.

### 4. Sweating: do not use it

Saunas, hot baths, sweat suits, plastic wraps, and training in heavy clothing to sweat are **not** part of a powerlifting weigh-in plan.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> In 1997, three previously healthy US college wrestlers died during rapid weight loss before weigh-in. All three restricted food and fluid and tried to maximise sweating with vapour-impermeable suits while exercising hard in hot environments; their deaths involved hyperthermia and dehydration (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 1998).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> An international consensus on training and competing in the heat identifies heat acclimatisation as the most effective protection, and advises starting hydrated, limiting dehydration, and using cooling (Racinais et al., 2015). Sweat-based cutting does the opposite of every one of these.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> In hot, humid places, such as much of the Philippines, a lifter can lose a lot of sweat just travelling to the venue or warming up in a hot gym. Plan the cut so that heat does **not** add to the loss, and keep the lifter cool.

### Red lines

| Never | Why |
|---|---|
| **Diuretics** ("water pills") | Prohibited **at all times** under the WADA Prohibited List (diuretics and masking agents). A positive test means an anti-doping rule violation, whatever the reason. They can also cause dangerous electrolyte changes. |
| **IV drips to rehydrate** | Intravenous infusions or injections of more than 100 mL per 12-hour period are prohibited by WADA unless legitimately received in hospital treatment, surgery or clinical investigations. Check the current list. |
| **Laxatives, enemas, vomiting, spitting all day** | Unsafe, cause electrolyte losses and gut problems; vomiting is a sign of disordered eating that needs help |
| **Saunas, sweat suits, hot baths, sweating sessions** | Heat illness risk; the cases above |
| **Cuts in sub-juniors** | Growing athletes should not cut aggressively; compete at natural weight |
| **Cuts with fever, diarrhoea, vomiting or illness** | Already dehydrated; move up a class or withdraw |
| **Cutting alone** | Someone should know the plan and check on the lifter |

Anti-doping basics are in [Part 1 · Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/foundations/11-nutrition/). The WADA Prohibited List is updated every year; check the current one.

:::tip[In plain words]
The safest way to drop the last bit of weight is to eat low-fibre food for a couple of days, go easy on salty food, and drink a bit less the day before. Never sweat it off, never take water pills (they are banned), and never use drips or laxatives. If you can only make weight by doing something dangerous, you are in the wrong class.
:::

## Rehydrating and refuelling after weigh-in

The goal is to feel normal by the first warm-up, not to "load up".

<Evidence level="practice" /> A practical 2-hour plan (adjust to the actual time between weigh-in and the first warm-up):

| Time after weigh-in | Drink | Eat |
|---|---|---|
| **0–15 min** | 500–750 mL of an oral rehydration solution or sports drink with sodium | Something quick and easy: banana, white bread or pandesal with jam, a rice cake |
| **15–60 min** | Keep sipping, about 200–250 mL every 15–20 min, guided by stomach comfort | Small, familiar, low-fibre, low-fat meal: white rice with chicken or egg |
| **60 min to first warm-up** | Continue sipping; urine should start to appear | Small carbohydrate snacks |
| **During the meet** | Sip between attempts; include sodium if sweating heavily | Small carbohydrate snacks between lifts |

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> Include **sodium**: fluid without enough sodium is largely lost as urine (Shirreffs et al., 1996).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Do not chug litres at once; a sloshing, bloated stomach hurts bracing. Spread intake out.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Eat only foods the lifter has eaten before and tested in training. Meet day is not the day for new foods or new supplements.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Caffeine timing, warm-ups and the full day plan are in [Part 4 · Chapter 6: Meet day](/lets-git-it/peaking/06-meet-day/).

:::tip[In plain words]
As soon as you've weighed in, start drinking something with salt in it and eat something easy. Keep sipping and nibbling in small amounts until you lift. Eat only foods you know sit well with you.
:::

## Rehearse it

<Evidence level="practice" /> A weigh-in plan should be tested before it matters.

- **Learn the lifter's numbers** in a normal week: how much weight they lose overnight, how much a low-fibre day changes the scale, how quickly it returns.
- **Run a rehearsal** 3–6 weeks out, ideally before a mock meet ([Part 4 · Chapter 3: Mock meets and testing openers](/lets-git-it/peaking/03-mock-meets-openers/)): do the final-day plan, weigh in, follow the refuelling plan, and lift. If performance drops, the plan is too aggressive.
- **Check your scale** against the meet scale when possible, and aim to arrive **0.2–0.3 kg under** the limit to cover scale differences.
- **Know the rules:** whether you can re-weigh if over, how long weigh-in lasts, what you must wear. Check the current IPF Technical Rules and the meet's information.

:::tip[In plain words]
Practise your weigh-in plan weeks before the meet, then lift. If you feel weak afterwards, the plan is too hard and needs to be smaller. Aim to weigh a little under the limit, because scales differ.
:::

## Sample programs

These are plans for **bodyweight**, not training. Training follows the peaking block in [Part 4 · Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/peaking/02-building-a-peaking-block/).

### Program 1: 12-week gradual approach (83 kg class)

**Lifter:** intermediate man, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. Weekly average morning weight 88.0 kg, 12 weeks before the meet. Limit 83.00 kg (6% over).

**Strategy:** diet at about 0.4% of bodyweight per week to a walking weight of about 84.5 kg, hold it through the peak, then make the last ~1.7 kg (about 2%) with gut contents and a small fluid reduction.

| Weeks out | Weekly average target | Nutrition | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12–9 | 88.0 → 86.6 kg (−0.35 kg/week) | About 300–400 kcal below maintenance; protein 2.3–2.6 g/kg (dieting range from [Part 1 · Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/foundations/11-nutrition/)) | Higher-volume block; easiest time to diet |
| 8–5 | 86.6 → 85.2 kg | Adjust calories by ~200 kcal if weight is flat for 2 weeks | Strength should hold or rise |
| 4–3 | 85.2 → 84.5 kg | Smaller deficit | **Checkpoint** at 3 weeks out (below) |
| 2–1 | Hold ~84.5 kg | Maintenance calories; carbohydrate back up for the peak | No more dieting during the heaviest weeks |
| Final 2 days | 84.5 → 82.8 kg (−1.7 kg, ~2%) | Low-fibre eating, avoid very salty foods, small fluid reduction the evening before | As in Program 2 |

**Checkpoint rule (3 weeks out):** if the weekly average is still above about 85.5 kg (3% over), do **not** plan a bigger cut. Enter the 93 kg class if entries allow, or accept a slower plan and a later meet.

**Why it's built this way:** most of the 5 kg comes from slow fat loss at a rate that protects strength, and dieting stops before the heaviest peaking weeks, so energy is back up when it matters most. The acute part stays at about 2%, inside what a 2-hour weigh-in can absorb.

### Program 2: Final week for a ~2.5% cut (63 kg class)

**Lifter:** woman, 63 kg class, squat 120 kg, bench press 65 kg, deadlift 150 kg, experienced competitor who has rehearsed this plan. Weekly average morning weight 64.5 kg (2.4% over). Meet on Saturday; session starts 10:00, weigh-in from 08:00. Target on the meet scale: 62.8 kg (1.7 kg to lose).

| Day | Food | Fluid | Expected morning weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sun–Tue** | Normal diet, normal salt | Normal; urine pale | ~64.5 kg |
| **Wed** | Normal diet, start reducing high-fibre foods | Normal | ~64.3 kg |
| **Thu** | Low-fibre: white rice, eggs, chicken, fish, white bread, bananas; no beans, monggo, leafy vegetables or whole grains. Avoid very salty foods. Smaller portions, protein kept up | Normal | ~64.0 kg |
| **Fri** | Low-fibre, small meals; modest carbohydrate; last real meal early evening | Normal until early afternoon, then sips only (about 0.5 L for the rest of the day) | ~63.6 kg |
| **Sat 06:30** | Nothing yet | Small sips only if needed | ~62.8–63.0 kg (her rehearsed overnight drop) |
| **Sat 08:00** | Weigh in early. If 0.1–0.2 kg over: use the toilet, a short easy walk in normal clothing in a cool place, re-weigh if the rules allow | — | ≤ 63.00 kg |
| **Sat after weigh-in** | Refuelling plan above; first warm-up ~09:15 | Electrolyte drink first, then sipping | — |

**Stop rules:** if she feels dizzy, confused, faint, has a headache that will not ease, or stops passing urine before Friday evening, she stops cutting, drinks, and lifts in the 69 kg class or does not compete. No sauna, no sweat suit, no diuretics, ever.

**Why it's built this way:** the cut starts with the safest bucket (gut contents) and touches fluid only for the last ~18 hours. Her expected weights come from a rehearsal, not a guess. An early weigh-in slot gives the full 2 hours to refuel. If the ~2.5% cut costs strength in the rehearsal, the target becomes a smaller cut next time.

### Program 3: The "don't cut" scenarios

Many lifters should not cut at all.

| Lifter | Situation | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| **Sub-junior**, 17, 74 kg class, walking weight 75.5 kg | Still growing | **Compete in 83 kg** at natural weight. Eat and drink normally. Revisit class choice each year as they grow. |
| **Novice**, second meet, 66 kg class, walking weight 67 kg | Has never rehearsed a cut | Compete in 74 kg. Learn the meet first; strength gains matter far more than class choice now. |
| **Intermediate man**, 83 kg class, 86.5 kg two weeks out | 4.2% over, too late to diet safely | Move to 93 kg if allowed, or diet slowly for the next meet. A 4% acute cut with a 2-hour weigh-in is not worth it. |
| **Any lifter, hot venue**, no air-conditioned warm-up area | Heat adds sweat loss on the day | Keep the cut to gut contents only, or skip it. |
| **Lifter with fever or stomach illness** that week | Already losing fluid | No cut. Move up a class or withdraw. |

**Why it's built this way:** a heavier class costs a little on paper, but a lifter who arrives fed, hydrated and calm usually lifts closer to their best. For young and new lifters, building strength and good habits matters far more than any class decision.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the cut instead of a diet.** Trying to lose 4–6% in the final week because the slow diet never happened.
- **Sweating it off.** Saunas, sweat suits and hot-room sessions add heat-illness risk for no benefit over safer methods.
- **Drinking plain water only after weigh-in.** Without sodium, much of it passes straight through.
- **Untested plans.** Trying water loading or a low-fibre diet for the first time in meet week.
- **Ignoring the scale difference.** Arriving at exactly the limit on a home scale that reads light.
- **Cutting youth lifters.** Growing athletes should compete at their natural weight.
- **Taking any "water pill" or "detox" product.** Diuretics are banned at all times, and supplements can be contaminated.
- **Forgetting the meet lasts hours.** A cut that feels fine for the opener can cost the deadlift.

## Key takeaways

- Make a weight class **slowly** (about 0.5–1% of bodyweight per week); keep the final acute cut **small**.
- Most powerlifters cut, on average about 3% of body mass, mostly by fluid restriction and water loading (survey data).
- In one small powerlifting trial, a ~5% cut over 4 days followed by 2 hours of recovery did not reduce strength in trained men; dehydration itself still costs strength, especially over many sets.
- A ~2-hour weigh-in allows partial refuelling, not full rehydration. Keep acute cuts to about 2–3% for experienced adults, less or none for novices, and none for sub-juniors.
- Use the safest tools first: low-fibre eating, avoiding very salty foods, and a short fluid reduction. Water loading adds little and needs care.
- **Never:** saunas, sweat suits, diuretics (WADA-prohibited), IV drips over 100 mL per 12 h (WADA-prohibited), laxatives, or cutting while ill.
- After weigh-in: sodium-containing fluid, easy carbohydrate, familiar food, small and often.
- Rehearse the plan before the meet, and aim to be 0.2–0.3 kg under the limit.
