## In one minute

An annual plan places the lifter's meets first, ranks them by importance, then counts backward to set a sequence of blocks in which each one prepares the next (**phase potentiation**). Research clearly favours planned, periodized training over unplanned training, but no study has compared whole-year or multi-year plans in powerlifters, so the details of long-term planning rest on coaching practice. Competition records do give useful expectations: powerlifters keep improving for many years, the strongest men improve more slowly, and world-class lifters reach their peak at around 35 years of age on average. Good long-term plans are written in pencil: the year in outline, the next block in detail, and every block adjusted to what the lifter's training and meets reveal.

## What long-term planning can and cannot do

- <Evidence level="strong" /> **Planning beats not planning.** Two meta-analyses found larger 1RM gains with periodized than with non-periodized training, with a smaller but still present advantage when volume was equated (Williams et al., 2017; Moesgaard et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **But the evidence is short-term.** Those studies lasted weeks to months, mostly in non-powerlifters. No trial has compared different **annual** or **multi-year** plans in powerlifters. Year-long planning is therefore built on principles, coaching experience and competition records.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Rigid long plans have critics.** Kiely (2012, 2018) argued that traditional periodization rests partly on outdated assumptions, and that because individuals respond so differently, plans should be responsive systems that are adjusted continually, not fixed scripts written months ahead.

**The working compromise** used in this manual: plan at three levels of detail.

| Horizon | Detail | Changes how often |
|---|---|---|
| Multi-year (2–4 years) | Goals and stages only: which meets, which totals, what the lifter needs to build | Once a year |
| Annual (meet to meet) | Order and length of blocks; meet dates; deload and transition weeks | After each meet, or when the calendar changes |
| Next block (3–6 weeks) | Exercises, sets, reps, RPE targets | Every block; adjusted weekly from monitoring |

:::tip[In plain words]
Planning your training is clearly better than winging it. But nobody has tested year-long plans in powerlifters, so plan the year roughly, plan the next few weeks in detail, and expect to change things as you go.
:::

## Phase potentiation across a year

<Evidence level="practice" /> **Phase potentiation** means ordering blocks so each one makes the next more effective (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith). Over a year, this usually looks like a repeating sequence:

| Block | Main goal | What it hands to the next block |
|---|---|---|
| **Transition** (1–2 weeks after a meet) | Recover physically and mentally; let aches settle | A fresh lifter who can tolerate volume again |
| **Accumulation / hypertrophy** (4–8 weeks) | Muscle, work capacity, weak-point variations | More muscle to train and the capacity to handle heavier work |
| **Transmutation / specific strength** (4–6 weeks) | Heavier sets of 2–5 on the competition lifts | Higher strength that the peak can express |
| **Realization / peak** (2–4 weeks, including the taper) | Heavy singles and doubles, volume cut | A rested lifter, practised at near-maximal loads, on meet day |

**Residual effects decide block length.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Issurin (2010) proposed that trained qualities fade gradually after the emphasis moves on, with maximal strength lasting roughly a month and muscle-related qualities somewhat longer. These are coaching estimates, not powerlifting data. In practice they mean:

- Keep the **competition lifts in every block**, even during accumulation, so specific strength and skill never fade far.
- Keep the gap between the end of a heavy, specific block and the meet to a few weeks, not a few months.
- After a long accumulation block, expect the first 1–2 heavy weeks to feel rusty; that is normal and not lost strength.

**Sequential vs concurrent emphasis.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Block-style plans shift emphasis from block to block (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith). Sheiko-style plans train the competition lifts frequently all year at mostly moderate intensities and vary the load from week to week (Sheiko). Both work. Most coaches blend them: competition-lift practice all year, with the emphasis on volume or intensity rotating across blocks.

:::tip[In plain words]
Arrange the year so each stage sets up the next: recover, build muscle, turn it into strength, then peak. Keep practising the squat, bench and deadlift all year so you never lose the skill, and don't let your heavy training end months before the meet.
:::

## Planning around the meet calendar

### Rank the meets

<Evidence level="practice" /> Not every meet deserves a full peak. Give each one a priority:

| Priority | Purpose | How to train into it |
|---|---|---|
| **A meet** | The main goal of the year; best possible total | Full block sequence and a full peak and taper (Part 4) |
| **B meet** | A useful result, qualification or experience, but not the main goal | Short taper (about 1–2 weeks); attempts that are likely to be made; no long peak |
| **C meet** | Practice, team support, meet experience, novice lifters' first meets | Train through it: a few lighter days before; openers and moderate second attempts |

### The Philippine calendar

<Evidence level="practice" /> In the Philippines, the Powerlifting Association of the Philippines (PAP) runs **Raw Nationals** as its main national classic championship, along with **Equipped Nationals** and **regional meets** (see [Part 1 · Chapter 1](/lets-git-it/foundations/01-what-is-powerlifting/)). Selected lifters may also compete at IPF-affiliated international meets. For most competitive classic lifters, Raw Nationals is the natural **A meet**, with a regional meet as a **B or C meet**. Dates, entry rules and any qualifying requirements change from year to year, so build the plan from **PAP's current announcements**, and re-plan if a date moves.

### Count backward from the A meet

<Evidence level="emerging" /> In interviews with 11 elite New Zealand raw powerlifters, total training volume peaked about **5 weeks** before competition, average intensity peaked about **2 weeks** out, volume was cut by roughly 59% during the taper with intensity kept, and the last training session was about **3–4 days** before the meet. Lifters said tapers went badly when they took a week or more off (Pritchard et al., 2016). <Evidence level="practice" /> A review found that research on peaking maximal strength is still limited, so practitioners rely heavily on trial and error (Travis et al., 2020).

A typical count-back from the A meet (week 0):

| Weeks before the meet | Block | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Meet | — |
| 1–3 | Realization and taper | Heavy singles; volume falling; last heavy session about a week out |
| 4–8 | Specific strength | Highest-intensity block before the peak; volume starts to fall in the final weeks |
| 9–15 | Accumulation | Highest volume of the cycle; weak-point variations |
| 16+ | Transition, then the previous cycle | Depends on the previous meet |

Peaking and tapering are covered in detail in Part 4, Chapters 1 and 2.

### How many meets per year?

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In competition records of 1,897 powerlifters over 15 years, there were **weak** relationships between meet frequency and rate of progress: shorter gaps between meets went with slightly faster strength gain per day, while among men, a larger number of competitions went with slightly slower gain (Latella et al., 2020). These are weak correlations in observational data; they cannot show that competing more or less often **causes** faster progress.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Each full peak costs time: about 3–4 weeks of reduced volume before the meet, and 1–2 weeks of transition afterwards. Two full peaks a year leave roughly 30–40 weeks for building; four leave much less. Most coaches plan **one or two A meets per year**, with B and C meets in between as needed.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Novices benefit from more frequent, low-pressure meets (C meets) to learn the commands, the timeline and attempt selection. Advanced lifters usually need more building time between full peaks.

:::tip[In plain words]
Pick your one or two most important meets each year and plan backward from them. Treat the other meets as practice, without a full peak. Every full peak and recovery costs about five or six weeks of building time, so don't peak too often.
:::

## Transition and off-season: don't stop completely

<Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 103 studies found that **stopping** resistance training reduced maximal force, submaximal strength and power, with larger losses the longer the break lasted, and larger losses in older adults (Bosquet et al., 2013).

<Evidence level="practice" /> Plan the post-meet transition as **reduced** training, not no training: 1–2 weeks of shorter sessions, lighter loads (about RPE 5–7), more variety, and some other activity the lifter enjoys. A longer break is fine when injury, burnout or life demands it, but expect a few weeks of rebuilding afterwards. Conditioning (see [Part 3 · Chapter 7](/lets-git-it/advanced/07-concurrent-training/)) fits best in this phase and the early accumulation block.

:::tip[In plain words]
After a meet, ease off for a week or two, but keep moving and lifting lightly. Long complete breaks cost strength, and the longer the break, the more you lose.
:::

## What competition data say about long-term progress

These studies use real competition results, so they show what powerlifters actually achieve over years.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Progress continues for years but slows as lifters get stronger.** In 1,897 powerlifters followed for up to 15 years, women and men gained strength at similar average rates, but the strongest quarter of men improved about half as fast as the least strong quarter (Latella et al., 2020). A second analysis of competition records from the same period (1,896 lifters) found the same pattern on each lift (Latella et al., 2022). Training-age guidelines say the same: novices progress session to session, intermediates week to week, advanced lifters over months (Rippetoe & Baker).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Squat and deadlift progress faster than bench press.** In that analysis, best improvements ranged from roughly **20–25 kg per year** on the squat, **18–21 kg per year** on the deadlift and **10.5–13 kg per year** on the bench press (Latella et al., 2022). These are averages across all competitors, including newer lifters; expect less in an advanced lifter.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Powerlifting careers peak late.** Among world-class lifters at powerlifting World Championships from 2003 to 2017, the average age of peak performance was **35 years** (standard deviation 7 years), about 9 years later than in weightlifting. In the 5 years before their peak, powerlifters improved by about **12%** on average (Solberg et al., 2019).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Relative strength peaks in young adulthood and declines slowly after.** In 809,986 entries from drug-tested raw competitions, relative strength (kg lifted per kg body weight) was greatest at 18–35 years and declined after that, though lifters over 80 were still lifting substantial loads (van den Hoek et al., 2024).

**What this means for planning:**

- <Evidence level="practice" /> A total that rises a few percent a year is normal for an advanced lifter. Judge a year by the trend across several meets, not by one meet.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> A lifter in their twenties is usually years from their peak. There is time to build muscle, fix technique and stay healthy; short-term heroics that risk injury rarely pay off.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Masters lifters can still improve, especially if they started late, but recovery and goals change (Part 3, Chapter 10: Special populations).

:::tip[In plain words]
Powerlifters keep getting stronger for many years, but the stronger you get, the slower it goes. The best lifters in the world peak around age 35. A few percent a year is good progress for an experienced lifter, so be patient and stay healthy.
:::

## Multi-year development stages

<Evidence level="practice" /> These stages are coaching practice, built from training-age models (Rippetoe & Baker; Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith) and the competition data above. Ages and years are flexible.

| Stage | Typical training age | Main priorities | Meets per year | Planning style |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Novice** | 0–1 year | Learn the lifts; add load session to session; build habits | 1–3 C meets once technique is safe | Simple linear progression ([Part 1 · Chapter 7](/lets-git-it/foundations/07-novice-linear-progression/)) |
| **Early intermediate** | 1–3 years | Muscle; competition technique under heavier loads; learn RPE | 2–3 (one A meet) | Weekly progression; simple blocks or undulating weeks |
| **Late intermediate** | 3–5 years | Specific weak points; volume tolerance; peaking skill | 2 (one or two A meets) | Full block sequences; planned deloads |
| **Advanced** | 5+ years | Small, specific gains; managing fatigue, injuries and life | 1–2 A meets plus selected B meets | Long blocks, individualised volume, careful monitoring |

**Across stages, three things grow:** the length of time between real progress tests, the amount of individualisation, and the importance of staying healthy.

:::tip[In plain words]
Beginners just need simple programs and practice. Each year after that, plans get longer, more personal and more careful about recovery and injuries. Advanced lifters gain slowly, so they plan around one or two big meets a year.
:::

## Building the annual plan: step by step

<Evidence level="practice" />

1. **Review last year.** Meet results, best training numbers, injuries, missed weeks, and what the monitoring data showed ([Part 3 · Chapter 8](/lets-git-it/advanced/08-monitoring-readiness/)).
2. **Set goals.** One main goal (for example a total at Raw Nationals) and one or two supporting goals (weight class, technique fix, muscle gain).
3. **Fix the meets and rank them** A, B or C from the current federation calendar.
4. **Count back from each A meet**: peak, specific strength, accumulation.
5. **Fill the gaps** with longer accumulation or off-season blocks, which is where muscle and weak points are built.
6. **Insert transitions and deloads**: a transition after each meet, and a deload or lighter week every 4–6 weeks ([Part 2 · Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/building-programs/11-deloads-fatigue-management/)).
7. **Mark checkpoints**: a heavy single at about RPE 8–8.5 at the end of each specific-strength block, so progress is measured without a full peak.
8. **Mark known life events**: exams, work deadlines, travel, holidays. Put deloads or easier blocks there.
9. **Review after every block** and re-plan the rest of the year if needed.

:::tip[In plain words]
Start from last year, lock in your meets, plan backward from the big one, and leave room for easy weeks around exams, travel and busy times. Check the plan after every block and change it if needed.
:::

## Sample programs

These are **year-level outlines**. Each block would be written out using the chapters on block, linear and undulating periodization (Part 2) and peaking (Part 4). Meet weeks are examples only; use PAP's current calendar.

### Program 1: One A meet per year (advanced lifter)

Advanced male lifter, 93 kg class, about 7 years of training: squat 230 kg, bench press 150 kg, deadlift 270 kg. Trains 4–5 days per week. One A meet (Raw Nationals) in week 44; no other meets. The table shows a ~46-week meet-to-meet cycle; if the meets are a full year apart, lengthen Accumulation 1. Weekly hard sets are per competition lift **including close variations**.

| Weeks | Block | Weekly hard sets (S / B / D) | Main RPE range | Focus and hand-off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Transition | 6 / 8 / 3 | 5–7 | Recover from last year's meet; review results |
| 3–10 | Accumulation 1 (deload in week 7) | 14 / 18 / 8 | 6–8 | Muscle and work capacity; weak-point variations; most conditioning here |
| 11–16 | Specific strength 1 (deload in week 15) | 12 / 15 / 7 | 7–9 | Sets of 3–5; ends with a checkpoint single at RPE 8–8.5 |
| 17–24 | Accumulation 2 (deload in week 21) | 15 / 18 / 8 | 6–8 | Variations chosen from checkpoint weaknesses |
| 25–30 | Specific strength 2 (deload in week 27) | 12 / 15 / 7 | 7–9 | Heavier triples and doubles; checkpoint single |
| 31 | Deload | 6 / 8 / 3 | 6–7 | Clear fatigue before the meet cycle |
| 32–36 | Accumulation 3 (shorter; deload in week 36 at 7 / 8 / 4) | 14 / 16 / 7 | 6–8 | Last high-volume block; variations move closer to competition form |
| 37–41 | Specific strength 3 | 11 / 14 / 6 | 7.5–9 | Competition lifts only, commands; loads rise as sets fall, so tonnage peaks about week 39 (about 5 weeks out), then falls |
| 42–43 | Realization and taper | 6 / 9 / 3 | 8–9 (singles) | Openers tested about 10–14 days out; volume cut by about half |
| 44 | **A meet: Raw Nationals** | — | — | — |
| 45–46 | Transition | Light | 5–7 | Start of next year's plan |

**Why it's built this way:** with only one meet, most of the year is building. Three accumulation-to-strength waves give three checkpoints, so the lifter sees progress (or a problem) at weeks 16 and 30 without the cost of a peak. Hard sets peak in the accumulation blocks, but tonnage (sets × reps × load) peaks in Specific strength 3, about 5 weeks out, because loads rise while sets fall. That matches the timing elite raw lifters report (Pritchard et al., 2016). The deadlift has the fewest sets because heavy pulls are the slowest to recover from. For a lifter at this level, a total a few percent higher than last year is a good result.

### Program 2: Two meets per year: B meet then A meet (intermediate lifter)

Intermediate lifter: squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg, 83 kg class. A regional meet (B) in week 18; Raw Nationals (A) in week 42. Trains 4 days per week.

| Weeks | Block | Main RPE range | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Transition | 5–7 | After last year's A meet |
| 3–8 | Accumulation 1 (deload in week 6 or 8 as needed) | 6–8 | Sets of 5–8; muscle; weak points |
| 9–15 | Specific strength 1 (deload in week 12) | 7–9 | Sets of 2–5 on the competition lifts |
| 16–17 | Short taper for the **B meet** | 7–8.5 | Heavy singles to about RPE 8.5 in week 16; week 17 reduced volume only |
| 18 | **B meet: regional** | — | Openers at about RPE 7–8, seconds near recent training bests, thirds only if the day is good |
| 19 | Transition (short) | 5–7 | One week only; the B meet cost less fatigue than a full peak |
| 20–27 | Accumulation 2 (deload in week 24) | 6–8 | Variations chosen from what the B meet revealed |
| 28–34 | Specific strength 2 (deload in week 31) | 7–9 | Heavier work; checkpoint single at the end |
| 35 | Deload | 6–7 | Clear fatigue before the meet cycle |
| 36–39 | Specific strength 3 / pre-peak | 7.5–9 | Competition lifts and commands; volume starts to fall around week 37 |
| 40–41 | Realization and taper | 8–9 (singles) | Openers tested about 10–14 days out |
| 42 | **A meet: Raw Nationals** | — | Full attempt strategy (Part 4) |
| 43–44 | Transition | 5–7 | Review the year; plan the next |

**Why it's built this way:** the B meet sits after a full specific-strength block, so the lifter arrives strong without a long peak, and the short taper means the lifter loses only about 4 weeks of building time instead of 5–6. Conservative attempts protect confidence and keep the meet useful: it gives a real competition total, practice with commands, and data for the second half of the year. The second cycle is longer and ends with the full peak. If the B meet is also a qualifying meet, treat it as an A meet and adjust.

### Program 3: Four-year outline (from first meet to national level)

Female lifter starting at about 1 year of training, 63 kg class: squat 100 kg, bench press 52.5 kg, deadlift 120 kg. Trains 3–4 days per week.

| Year | Stage | Meets | Training focus | Progress checks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Early intermediate | 2–3 regional meets: one treated as the A meet, the others as C meets | Weekly progression; learn RPE; competition technique and commands; build muscle | Steady rises in training top sets; all attempts technically legal by the end of the year |
| 2 | Early intermediate | One B meet (regional), one A meet (Raw Nationals) | Two block cycles a year; weak-point variations from video and meet results; 4-day training | New best total at each meet is likely; bench gains smaller than squat and deadlift |
| 3 | Late intermediate | One B meet, one A meet | Longer accumulation blocks; volume tolerance; peaking practice; decide on long-term weight class | Gains slower than years 1–2; judge by the trend across meets |
| 4 | Late intermediate / advanced | One A meet; B meet optional | Individualised volume per lift; careful monitoring; plan built around life events | Smaller yearly gains are expected; the aim is a long, healthy career |

**Expectations, honestly stated:** across competition records, best improvements ranged from roughly 20–25 kg per year on the squat, 18–21 kg on the deadlift and 10.5–13 kg on the bench press (Latella et al., 2022). These are averages across all competitors and all career stages, with women and men gaining at similar rates. An individual lifter may gain more in years 1–2 and less later, and some years will be flat. Use these figures to set realistic goals, not as targets that must be hit.

**Why it's built this way:** early years prioritise skill, muscle and meet experience, which every later stage builds on. The number of full peaks stays low so most of each year is spent building. Each year's plan is written only after reviewing the year before.

## Common mistakes

- **Planning every week of the year in detail.** Outline the year; write the next block in detail; adjust after every block.
- **Peaking for every meet.** Full peaks cost building time. Rank meets and peak only for A meets.
- **Letting heavy training end months before the meet.** Strength residuals fade; the specific strength block must run into the peak.
- **Stopping completely after a meet.** Short, reduced transitions keep more strength than long, complete breaks.
- **Judging a year by one meet.** Progress for advanced lifters is slow and noisy; look at the trend across meets and checkpoints.
- **Ignoring life events.** Exams, deadlines and travel belong in the plan as easier weeks, not as surprises.
- **Copying another lifter's calendar.** Meet ranking, block lengths and volume must fit this lifter's stage and recovery.

## Key takeaways

- Periodized training beats unplanned training, but long-term plans have not been tested directly; plan the year roughly and the next block in detail.
- Sequence blocks so each prepares the next: transition, accumulation, specific strength, peak; keep the competition lifts in every block.
- Rank meets as A, B or C; give only A meets a full peak; count backward from the A meet.
- Use PAP's current calendar for Raw Nationals and regional meets; never plan from assumed dates.
- Competition data show steady progress over years, slower in the strongest men, faster in squat and deadlift than bench press, and a peak age around 35 in world-class lifters.
- Plan transitions as reduced training, not complete breaks, and review the whole plan after every block and meet.
