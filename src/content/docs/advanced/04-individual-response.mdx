## In one minute

Put a group of people on the same program and their results spread widely: some barely change, some gain a lot. Part of that spread is real (genetics, training history, recovery, adherence), but part of it is **noise**: normal day-to-day variation and measurement error make some people look like "non-responders" when they are not. True non-responders to resistance training appear to be rare; a lifter who is not progressing usually needs a **different dose or setup**, not a different body. Coaches handle individual response by tracking trends over several weeks, separating real change from noise, and changing **one variable at a time** in planned test blocks. The aim is a program that fits this lifter, built from evidence about the average lifter.

## How big are individual differences?

Studies that report every participant's result, not just the group average, show a wide spread.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In pooled data from 287 previously untrained men and women aged 19–78 (215 who trained and 72 non-training controls), strength changes ranged from −8% to +60% and muscle size changes from −11% to +30%. After allowing for the variation seen in non-training controls, about 7% were classed as low responders for strength and 29% for muscle size. Age and sex did not change the responses (Ahtiainen et al., 2016).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 66 untrained adults clustered after 16 weeks of training, average muscle-fibre growth ranged from large in the "extreme responders" to roughly zero in the "non-responders" (Bamman et al., 2007).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 20 resistance-trained young men, each leg followed a different program for 8 weeks. Differences **between people** in muscle growth were about 40 times larger than the differences **between one person's two legs** on different programs. Every participant responded, and none did clearly better on one specific protocol (Damas et al., 2019).

That last finding matters for coaches. Within the range of sensible programs, **who the lifter is** often explains more of the result than **which sensible program they run**.

:::tip[In plain words]
Two lifters on the same program can get very different results. Often, who you are (your body, history and life) matters more than small details of the program. That's why copying someone else's program rarely gives you their results.
:::

## Do non-responders exist? The honest answer

The idea of the "non-responder" is popular, but the evidence is more careful than the headlines.

### The case that most people respond

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In older adults (over 65) trained for 12 to 24 weeks (110 people at 12 weeks, 85 at 24 weeks), every participant improved on at least one measure (lean mass, fibre size, strength or chair-rise time), and responses were more positive with longer training (Churchward-Venne et al., 2015).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In the pooled data above, once the normal variation seen in non-training controls was allowed for, only about 7% of people were low responders for strength (Ahtiainen et al., 2016). In 20 resistance-trained young men, every participant was classed as a responder (Damas et al., 2019).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> A review concluded that **global** non-responders to exercise are unlikely to exist. Someone who doesn't respond on one measure, or to one type of training, may respond to another, or to more of the same (Pickering & Kiely, 2019; the first author works for a genetic-testing company).

### The measurement-error critique

Many "responder" findings are distorted by how they are measured.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Random error creates fake responders.** This point comes from a methodological paper. Every measurement varies a little from day to day, even with good equipment. Plots of individual changes can suggest large individual differences even when everyone's true response is identical. True individual differences can only be estimated by comparing the spread of changes in the training group with the spread in a control group (or a repeatability study) over the same period (Atkinson & Batterham, 2015).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Common classification methods find "responders" even without training.** This is also a methodological paper. Splitting people by percentiles, standard deviations or clusters will always produce high and low responders, even in a group that did nothing. Only people whose change exceeds the random error measured in a control group can be confidently labelled (Dankel & Loenneke, 2020). This criticism applies to cluster studies such as Bamman et al. (2007).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Different methods disagree.** In a 1-year endurance training study with repeated testing (36 untrained adults), several analysis methods showed real individual differences in response. But only 11 of 20 participants were classified the same way by every method (Hecksteden et al., 2018).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **One block is not enough.** This point comes from a methodological paper. Measurement error limits how accurately any one person's response can be estimated, and a larger sample can't fix that for a single lifter. The same person's response to the same training may also vary from one period to the next, which only shows up if the training is repeated (Hecksteden et al., 2015).

### What this means for coaches

- Real differences in response **do exist**, but they are smaller than raw before-and-after numbers suggest.
- Calling a lifter a "non-responder" after one block, based on one test, is almost always premature.
- The practical question is not "does this lifter respond?" but "**what dose and type of training does this lifter respond to best?**"

:::tip[In plain words]
Almost everyone gets stronger with good training. Some people look like they didn't improve because a single test on a single day is a bit random. Before deciding a program "doesn't work for you", look at several weeks of results and consider changing how much or how hard you train.
:::

## What drives individual differences

| Factor | What the evidence says | What a coach can change |
|---|---|---|
| **Genetics** | About half the variation in strength between sedentary people is heritable; the environmental share grows with age | Nothing directly; it explains why ceilings differ |
| **Sex** | Similar gains in muscle and lower-body strength; females gained more relative upper-body strength in short studies | Little: program by the lifter, not the label |
| **Age** | Untrained older adults respond well; age did not change response in pooled data | Recovery time, volume, exercise choice |
| **Training history** | The strongest male powerlifters improve more slowly | Expectations, block length, variety of stimulus |
| **Recovery** | Sleep, stress and energy intake affect what a lifter can absorb | Volume, frequency, deloads, lifestyle advice |
| **Adherence** | Missed sessions and unplanned changes make any program look ineffective | Program fit, session length, schedule |

### Genetics

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of twin and family studies estimated that about **half (52%)** of the variation in muscle strength measures between sedentary people is heritable. The rest is environmental, and the environmental share increased with age (Zempo et al., 2017). The studies varied a lot, and these figures describe differences in strength level, not in response to training.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> There is no established way to choose a powerlifting program from a genetic test. The lifter's actual training response, tracked over several blocks, is better information.

### Sex

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis found that males and females gained muscle and **lower-body strength** to a similar degree on the same program. Females gained more **relative upper-body strength**, a moderate effect. The authors noted this may apply mainly to untrained females and may partly reflect how short the studies were (Roberts et al., 2020).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Sex is a weak guide to an individual's program. Female lifters are covered further in Part 3, Chapter 10: Special populations.

### Age

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In the pooled data of Ahtiainen et al. (2016), **age and sex did not affect** the size of the response in untrained adults aged 19–78. Older adults clearly respond to training (Churchward-Venne et al., 2015).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Masters lifters often need more recovery between hard sessions, even if their response to a given dose is similar. Adjust frequency and volume by how the lifter recovers.

### Training history

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 1,897 powerlifters followed over competition results spanning 15 years (2003–2018), strength gain per day was similar between men and women. However, the **strongest men improved about half as fast** as the least strong men (0.102 vs 0.211 kg per day), possibly a ceiling effect. No such difference was found in women (Latella et al., 2020).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Advanced lifters therefore see smaller changes, which are harder to separate from noise. Their test blocks need longer windows and better tracking.

### Volume: the most-studied individual factor

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 34 untrained adults, each leg trained with 1 or 3 sets per exercise for 12 weeks. On average, 3 sets gave more muscle and strength. But only **13 participants showed a clear extra benefit for muscle growth and 16 for strength** from the higher volume (Hammarström et al., 2020). Not everyone gains more from more.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 16 trained people, one leg trained with a standard research volume (22 sets per week) and the other with **1.2 times each person's own logged weekly sets**. The individualised leg grew more over 8 weeks (Scarpelli et al., 2022). This was muscle size, not strength, but it supports **basing volume on the lifter's own history**.

### Recovery and adherence

- <Evidence level="practice" /> Sleep, life stress and energy intake change how much training a lifter can absorb. These are covered in [Part 1 · Chapter 10](/lets-git-it/foundations/10-recovery-sleep-stress/) and [Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/foundations/11-nutrition/).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Before deciding a lifter "doesn't respond", check that the program was actually done. A block with three missed sessions, or with loads changed on the day, is not a test of the program.

:::tip[In plain words]
Your genes, your training history, your sleep and stress, and whether you actually did the sessions all shape your results. You can't change your genes, but you can change how much you train, how often, and how well you recover. Start there.
:::

## Separating real change from noise

Before you can adjust to a lifter's response, you need to know whether there was a response.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 32 studies found that 1RM testing is reliable, but still varies between repeat tests: the **median coefficient of variation was 4.2%** (range 0.5–12.1%) (Grgic et al., 2020).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> A methodological paper advises that practitioners should compare an observed change with both the **measurement error** and the **smallest worthwhile change**, ideally using confidence intervals, before calling it real (Swinton et al., 2018).

<Evidence level="practice" /> A working rule for coaches, based on those ideas:

| Situation | Rough noise band | How to read it |
|---|---|---|
| One test vs another (1RM or e1RM) | About ±5–6% or more | A 120 kg bencher's e1RM moving by 5 kg could be noise |
| Average of the top-set e1RMs over 2–3 sessions | Smaller | Better: trends cut day-to-day variation |
| Trend over 4–6 weeks, with RPE ≥ 7.5 sets only | Smaller still | The best routine signal for an individual |

**How to track it:**

1. Use the same exercise, technique standard and rest each time.
2. Calculate an e1RM from top sets of 1–5 reps at about RPE 7.5 or higher (see [Part 1 · Chapter 12](/lets-git-it/foundations/12-tracking-training/)).
3. Average the week's top-set e1RMs and look at the **trend** across the block, not single sessions.
4. Note anything that would confound the result: illness, poor sleep, missed sessions, a weight cut.

:::tip[In plain words]
Your max goes up and down a little from day to day, by about 4% on average. So a small change after one test means very little. Watch the trend over several weeks of good sets before deciding anything worked or didn't.
:::

## How coaches adjust: the n-of-1 approach

An **n-of-1 test** treats one lifter as their own experiment: run a baseline, change one thing, and compare. It does not give research-grade certainty, but it is far better than guessing.

<Evidence level="practice" /> This approach applies the statistical ideas above (repeated measurement, noise, one change at a time) to coaching. It has not been tested as a coaching method in powerlifters.

### The steps

1. **Baseline block (3–6 weeks).** Run a sound program and track e1RM trends, RPE at fixed loads, and recovery notes.
2. **Choose one variable.** Pick the most likely limiter from the log: volume, frequency, intensity, exercise selection or recovery.
3. **Change only that variable** by a meaningful step (for example ±25–30% of weekly sets, or one extra day). Keep everything else the same.
4. **Test block (same length).** Track the same measures.
5. **Compare trends**, not single numbers, against the noise band.
6. **Repeat** the comparison later if the answer matters. One test can mislead (Hecksteden et al., 2015).

### What to change first

<Evidence level="practice" /> Order of suspicion, from the most common to the least:

| Sign in the log | Likely limiter | First change to test |
|---|---|---|
| Same RPE at the same load for 3+ weeks; lifter feels fresh | Too little stimulus | Add 2–4 hard sets per week to the stuck lift |
| RPE at fixed loads rising week to week; sleep or motivation dropping | Too much fatigue | Cut 25–30% of sets, or add a deload |
| Good sessions early in the week, poor later | Distribution | Spread the same sets over more days |
| Variations improving, competition lift flat | Specificity | Shift sets from variations to the competition lift |
| Lifter progresses for 2 weeks, then stalls every block | Block structure | Shorten blocks, or add a planned lighter week |
| Progress collapses whenever work or school is busy | Recovery | Plan lighter blocks around known busy periods |

### Lifter response profiles

<Evidence level="practice" /> Over several blocks, many coaches sort lifters into rough profiles. The idea that recoverable volume differs between lifters is central to Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith's volume landmarks, and Tuchscherer's approach sets fatigue targets for each lifter individually. The profiles below are working labels, not fixed types; they change with training age and life circumstances.

| Profile | Signs | Program tendency |
|---|---|---|
| **Volume-tolerant** | Recovers fast, progresses on many sets, RPE stays stable | More sets, moderate intensity |
| **Intensity-responsive** | Progresses on heavy, low-rep work; high volume flattens them | Fewer, heavier sets; more singles and doubles |
| **High-frequency** | Technique and strength improve with frequent practice | Lift trained 3–4× per week, fewer sets each day |
| **Low recovery** | RPE climbs quickly, sleep and mood suffer under load | Lower volume, more deloads, longer between heavy sessions |

:::tip[In plain words]
Treat your training like a simple experiment. Run a normal block, then change one thing (more sets, fewer sets, or an extra day) and run it again. Compare how your lifts trended. Change only one thing at a time, or you won't know what helped.
:::

## Sample programs

These are case studies: each shows a baseline, the decision, and the change. Loads use the RPE chart from [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg.

### Program 1: The bench press volume test (intermediate, 4 days)

**Lifter:** male, 83 kg, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. Bench e1RM has been flat at about 120 kg for two blocks. RPE at 100 kg for 3 has stayed at about 7 for six weeks, and he says he feels fresh.

**Hypothesis:** too little bench stimulus. **Change:** weekly bench hard sets from 12 to 16. **Everything else stays the same** (squat, deadlift, accessories, sleep routine).

| Day | Exercise | Baseline block (weeks 1–4) | Test block (weeks 5–8) | Load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 5 | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 5 | **5 × 5** | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 3 × 4 | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 (~170 kg) |
| 2 | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 6 | **4 × 6** | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Squat | 4 × 3 | 4 × 3 | RPE 8 (~155 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press | 1 × 1, then 4 × 3 | 1 × 1, then 4 × 3 | Single RPE 8 (~110 kg); back-offs RPE 7 (~100 kg) |
| 4 | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × 4 | 3 × 4 | RPE 6.5 |
| 4 | Larsen press | — | **2 × 6** | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 6 | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |

Weekly bench hard sets: baseline 12 (4 + 3 + 5, counting the single), test block 16 (5 + 4 + 5 + 2). Day 3 is left unchanged so the tracking set stays the same.

**Tracking:** average the day 3 single's e1RM each week, and record the RPE of the 100 kg back-off triples.

**Decision rules:**

| Result after the test block | Interpretation | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| e1RM up 5 kg or more as a consistent upward trend across the block, not one session, and back-off RPE falling | Volume was limiting | Keep 16 sets; next block try 18 |
| e1RM flat, RPE flat, lifter fresh | Volume was not the limiter | Return to 12 sets; test frequency or specificity next |
| e1RM flat or down, RPE rising, sleep worse | Too much for now | Return to 12 sets; check recovery |

**Why it's built this way.** Only bench sets change, so any difference in the bench trend can reasonably be linked to volume. The step is large enough (about +33%) to matter. One study showed many but not all people gain more from more sets (Hammarström et al., 2020), so this is a test, not an assumption.

### Program 2: Individualising volume from the log (female lifter, 4 days)

**Lifter:** female, 63 kg class, squat 120 kg, bench press 65 kg, deadlift 150 kg, four years of training. She previously ran a template with 16 squat sets per week and stalled with sore knees and poor sleep. Her log from her best block (squat up 7.5 kg) shows about **9 hard squat sets per week**.

**Approach:** instead of a standard number, start from her own logged volume and add about 20%, as in the individualised-volume study (Scarpelli et al., 2022; a muscle-size study, so this is an extrapolation to strength). 9 × 1.2 ≈ **11 squat sets per week**.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~50 kg) |
| 1 | Chest-supported row | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 2 | Deadlift | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 (~122.5 kg) |
| 2 | Bench press | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~52.5 kg) |
| 2 | Leg curl | 3 × 12 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Squat | 1 × 3 @ RPE 8, then 2 × 3 | Top ~102.5 kg; back-offs ~97.5 kg |
| 3 | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Pause squat (2 s) | 4 × 4 | RPE 6.5 |
| 4 | Bench press | 3 × 5 | RPE 7.5 |
| 4 | Back extension | 3 × 12 | RPE 8 |

Weekly squat hard sets: 4 + 3 + 4 = **11**.

**Adjustment rules for the next block:**

- Squat e1RM trend up, knees fine, sleep fine: add 1–2 sets (to 12–13).
- Trend up, but knee soreness or sleep worse: hold at 11.
- Trend flat, everything fine: add 2 sets, then re-test.
- Trend flat and fatigue rising: drop back to 9 and look at frequency or exercise choice instead.

**Why it's built this way.** Her history shows that 16 sets was beyond what she could recover from at that time, while about 9 sets produced progress. Starting slightly above her proven dose is a smaller, safer step than adopting someone else's number.

### Program 3: Two lifters, one template, two adjustments (masters lifter, 3 days)

**Lifters:** two men aged 50–55 on the same 3-day program. Lifter C: squat 160 kg, bench 100 kg, deadlift 190 kg. Lifter D has similar numbers. After 4 weeks:

- **Lifter C:** e1RMs rising, RPE at fixed loads falling, sleeping well. *Responding as planned.*
- **Lifter D:** e1RMs flat, deadlift RPE at 160 kg went from 7 to 8.5, lower back stiff for two days after day 2, sleeping poorly in a busy work month. *Fatigue, not lack of stimulus.*

**Shared baseline week (both lifters, weeks 1–4)**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load (Lifter C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~125 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~77.5 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 4 × 3 | RPE 7 (~160 kg) |
| 2 | Bench press | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~80 kg) |
| 3 | Squat | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 (~135 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press | 1 × 2 @ RPE 8, then 3 × 3 | Top ~90 kg; back-offs ~82.5 kg |
| 3 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |

**Weeks 5–8: Lifter C (responder) — progress the same plan**

| Change | Detail |
|---|---|
| Load | Add 2.5 kg to squat and bench, 5 kg to deadlift, at the same RPE targets |
| Volume | Unchanged |

**Weeks 5–8: Lifter D (fatigued) — change one thing: deadlift stress**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Deadlift | **2 × 3** (was 4 × 3) | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Romanian deadlift | **2 × 6** (was 3 × 6) | RPE 7 |
| All | Everything else | Unchanged | Same RPE targets |

**What to check:** if Lifter D's deadlift RPE at 160 kg returns to about 7, his back stiffness clears and his e1RMs start rising, the extra hinge volume was the problem. If nothing changes, the limiter is more likely to be recovery outside the gym (sleep, work stress), so reduce overall volume for the busy period rather than removing more deadlift work.

**Why it's built this way.** The same program produced two different responses. One lifter keeps progressing. The other has one obvious source of extra fatigue, so only that is changed. Age alone did not decide this (Ahtiainen et al., 2016); the log did. More on masters lifters is in Part 3, Chapter 10: Special populations.

## Common mistakes

- **Labelling a lifter a "non-responder" after one block.** One test is partly noise; most people respond to some dose.
- **Changing several variables at once.** If volume, frequency and exercises all change, you can't tell what worked.
- **Judging from single sessions.** Use weekly averages and multi-week trends of top-set e1RMs.
- **Copying a strong lifter's program exactly.** Their result reflects their history, recovery and genetics, not just the program.
- **Treating response profiles as permanent.** A lifter who needed low volume during a stressful year may handle more later.
- **Ignoring adherence.** A program that wasn't followed hasn't been tested.
- **Using genetic tests to choose training.** The lifter's actual response is better information.

## Key takeaways

- Responses to the same program vary widely, but part of the spread is measurement error and day-to-day variation.
- True global non-responders appear rare; a lifter who isn't progressing usually needs a different dose or setup.
- Genetics, training history, recovery and adherence matter; age and sex matter less than often assumed.
- A single 1RM test varies by about 4%; judge change by multi-week trends.
- Use n-of-1 test blocks: baseline, change one variable, compare trends, repeat if needed.
- Base volume on the lifter's own log, then adjust by response.
