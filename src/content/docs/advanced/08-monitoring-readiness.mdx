## In one minute

Hard training is supposed to make a lifter tired for a while; the question is whether that tiredness turns into progress (**functional overreaching**), lingers without progress (**non-functional overreaching**), or becomes a long-lasting illness-like state (**overtraining syndrome**, which is rare in strength sports). Research has not found a blood test, hormone or device that reliably diagnoses these states; **a sustained drop in performance** is the only established marker in resistance training. Simple self-report questionnaires track training stress at least as well as most objective tests, and bar velocity, jump tests and heart rate variability can add detail if they are measured consistently and compared with the lifter's own normal range. The best system is the smallest one the lifter will fill in honestly, read alongside performance, with decision rules written down in advance.

## The fatigue continuum

<Evidence level="practice" /> The European College of Sport Science and the American College of Sports Medicine published a joint consensus statement that defines the states below (Meeusen et al., 2013). The time frames in the table are the typical descriptions used in this research, not fixed cut-offs, and match those in [Part 2 · Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/building-programs/11-deloads-fatigue-management/).

| State | What happens | Typical recovery | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Functional overreaching (FOR)** | Short-term drop in performance after a hard block, without severe or lasting other symptoms | Days to about two weeks | Performance **improves** after recovery. This is the goal of a hard week or a planned overreach |
| **Non-functional overreaching (NFOR)** | Longer performance drop, often with low mood, poor sleep or other symptoms | Weeks | Performance returns, but **no improvement**; time has been lost |
| **Overtraining syndrome (OTS)** | Prolonged maladaptation; performance and well-being stay down despite rest | Months or longer | Can end a season. Diagnosed by excluding other causes |

Key points from the consensus statement (Meeusen et al., 2013):

- NFOR and OTS can look the same. Telling them apart depends on how long recovery takes and on **excluding other causes**.
- Other causes to rule out include illness or infection, too little energy intake (low energy availability), too little carbohydrate or protein, iron deficiency, and others. That is a job for a doctor, not a coach.
- Many markers have been tried (hormones, performance tests, psychological questionnaires, blood and immune markers), and **none met all the criteria** to be generally accepted for diagnosis.

:::tip[In plain words]
Being tired after a hard week is normal and, if you then rest a bit, you come back stronger. If you stay flat for weeks, you overdid it. If you stay flat for months even with rest, something bigger is wrong, and a doctor should check you.
:::

## What we know about overreaching in strength sports

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Overtraining syndrome is rarely documented in lifters.** A scoping review of 47 studies found that short periods of high-volume or high-intensity resistance training can produce functional overreaching, that chronic high-volume and/or high-intensity training can lead to non-functional overreaching, and that there was **minimal evidence that true OTS has occurred** in strength sports or resistance training (Bell et al., 2020).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Performance is the only reliable marker.** A systematic review of 22 resistance training overreaching studies found that methods and diagnostic criteria were too inconsistent to establish FOR, NFOR or OTS reliably. It suggested overtraining may be linked to frequent, high-intensity, **monotonous** training, and concluded that **no marker other than a sustained decrease in performance** has been established (Grandou et al., 2020).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Daily maximal lifting can overreach a trained lifter quickly.** In 11 weight-trained men who trained on a squat machine daily at 100% of their 1RM for 2 weeks, 1RM fell by about 12 kg on average, while a low-intensity control group did not change (Fry et al., 1994).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> A review proposed that **too much volume** and **too much intensity** produce different hormonal and nervous-system profiles, so markers taken from endurance-sport overtraining may not apply to every lifting scenario (Fry & Kraemer, 1997).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In interviews with 14 high-performance strength-sport coaches (including 4 powerlifting coaches), most had **not** seen long-term performance drops in their athletes, and they considered OTS rare in their sports. Their definitions and symptom lists varied widely (Bell et al., 2021).

**What this means for coaches:** in powerlifting, the realistic risk is not OTS; it is **non-functional overreaching**, months of plateau from blocks that are too hard, too long, too monotonous, or stacked on top of poor sleep, low food intake or life stress. Monitoring is mostly about catching that early.

:::tip[In plain words]
Real overtraining is rare in lifters. The common problem is milder: training too hard for too long, so you stall for weeks or months. The only sure sign is that your lifts keep going down, which is why your training log matters more than any gadget.
:::

## Monitoring tools

<Evidence level="practice" /> A review of load monitoring noted that very few markers have strong scientific support, that there is no single definitive marker, and that monitoring should be **individualised** and kept simple enough to give quick, useful feedback (Halson, 2014). An international consensus statement on monitoring training loads separates **external load** (what the lifter did: sets, reps, kilograms) from **internal load** (how hard it was for them) (Bourdon et al., 2017). The tools below cover both.

### 1. Training performance (the reference standard)

What to track, from the log ([Part 1 · Chapter 12](/lets-git-it/foundations/12-tracking-training/)):

- **Estimated 1RM from top sets.** Use only sets rated about RPE 7.5 or higher, where ratings are most accurate.
- **RPE at a known load.** The same load and reps that felt like RPE 7 three weeks ago now feels like RPE 8.5.
- **Missed reps** at loads that should be manageable.

<Evidence level="moderate" /> This is the only marker with established value for detecting overtraining in resistance training (Grandou et al., 2020). Its limit is that it shows the problem **after** it has started to cost performance.

:::tip[In plain words]
Your lifts are the best fatigue test you have. If weights you know feel heavier and your estimated max keeps slipping for several sessions, you're carrying too much fatigue.
:::

### 2. Session RPE and training load

**Session RPE (sRPE)** is a single 0–10 rating of how hard the **whole session** was, given about 30 minutes after training. Multiplying it by session minutes gives a "session load" in arbitrary units, for example RPE 7 × 90 min = 630 units. This method was proposed as a simple way to monitor training of any kind (Foster et al., 2001).

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 19 men and women performing high-, moderate- and low-intensity resistance sessions twice each, session RPE was reliable (intraclass correlation 0.88), and fewer reps at a heavier load were rated harder than more reps at a lighter load (Day et al., 2004).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Do not confuse it with the RIR-based RPE** used for single sets ([Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/)). Set RPE describes reps left in one set; session RPE describes the whole session.
- **Limits.** Session load does not tell squats from bench presses or heavy singles from volume work. <Evidence level="practice" /> Ratios built from it, such as the **acute:chronic workload ratio**, have serious statistical problems, and a critical review concluded there is no evidence supporting their use to manage injury risk (Impellizzeri et al., 2020).

Use weekly session load to see **how hard the week was overall** and to check that planned easy weeks were actually easy. Do not use it to predict injury.

:::tip[In plain words]
After each session, rate the whole workout from 0 to 10 and multiply by the minutes trained. Add up the week. It's a quick way to see whether a week was harder or easier than planned, not a crystal ball for injuries.
:::

### 3. Wellness questionnaires

A short daily form, usually 4–6 items rated 1–5: sleep quality, fatigue, muscle soreness, stress, mood or motivation.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 56 studies found that **subjective measures** (mood, perceived stress, perceived recovery) responded to both short-term and long-term changes in training load **more sensitively and consistently** than objective measures such as blood markers and heart rate. Subjective and objective measures generally did not agree with each other (Saw et al., 2016).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 14 elite swimmers followed for a 6-month season, daily self-ratings of well-being were related to which swimmers became stale, and during the taper they were related to how much race times improved (Hooper et al., 1995).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 professional rugby league players, a short wellness form (fatigue, well-being, soreness) and jump performance both dropped after matches and recovered within about 4 days (McLean et al., 2010).

**Limits.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Questionnaires only work if the lifter answers honestly and the form is short enough to fill in every day. Lifters may under-report when they fear the coach will cut their training, or stop filling it in when nothing is done with the answers. Look at **trends against the lifter's own normal**, not single scores.

:::tip[In plain words]
A one-minute daily check-in on sleep, soreness, stress and motivation is one of the best monitoring tools there is, often better than blood tests or heart-rate gadgets. It only works if you answer honestly and your coach actually uses the answers.
:::

### 4. Bar velocity

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 18 strength-trained men, the loss of bar velocity across sets was very strongly related to blood lactate and to loss of jump height, supporting velocity loss as an objective measure of fatigue **within a session** (Sánchez-Medina & González-Badillo, 2011).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 15 powerlifters, average bar velocity was strongly related to RPE and to percentage of 1RM on all three lifts, but the authors advised building **individual** load–velocity profiles for accurate prescription. Velocity at 1RM differed between lifts: about 0.23 m/s on the squat, 0.14 m/s on the deadlift and 0.10 m/s on the bench press (Helms et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 17 strength-trained men, velocity at the 1RM squat varied a lot from day to day (coefficient of variation 22.5%), so load–velocity predictions of 1RM were not accurate enough to adjust daily loads, even though the actual 1RM was very stable (Banyard et al., 2017).

<Evidence level="practice" /> **Practical use for readiness:** pick one **fixed submaximal load** that the lifter uses often, for example the last warm-up before the top set, and record its mean velocity every session. Compare it with the lifter's own range over the last 4–6 exposures. A clearly slow warm-up, together with a higher RPE, is a stronger signal than either alone. Use the same device, the same lift, and the same depth and commands each time. Velocity-based training itself is covered in [Part 3 · Chapter 3](/lets-git-it/advanced/03-velocity-based-training/).

:::tip[In plain words]
A bar-speed device can show when a familiar warm-up weight is moving slower than usual. Compare each lifter with their own normal, not with someone else's numbers, and use it alongside RPE rather than instead of it.
:::

### 5. Jump tests

<Evidence level="emerging" /> A meta-analysis of 151 articles using the countermovement jump to monitor fatigue and recovery found that the **average** of several jumps was more sensitive than the single **best** jump for detecting fatigue and supercompensation (Claudino et al., 2017).

<Evidence level="practice" /> **For powerlifters:** jumps need a jump mat, force plate or a validated phone app, and the link between jump height and squat or deadlift readiness is indirect. They suit teams or coaches who already have the equipment. If you use them, take the **average of 3–5 jumps** at the same time of day, after the same warm-up.

:::tip[In plain words]
A few jumps can show whether your legs are fresh or tired, if you have a way to measure them. Use the average of several jumps, not your best one, and remember that jumping is not the same as squatting.
:::

### 6. Heart rate variability (HRV)

HRV is the beat-to-beat variation in heart rate, usually measured for a few minutes on waking with a chest strap, finger sensor or phone app. Higher values at rest generally reflect more parasympathetic ("rest and digest") activity.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 26 studies found that a single resistance-training session shifts HRV towards **less parasympathetic and more sympathetic activity** about 30 minutes afterwards, and that the shift was larger with higher training volume (Marasingha-Arachchige et al., 2022). This shows HRV responds to lifting; it does not show HRV can guide a powerlifting program.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 7 weightlifters, a parasympathetic HRV index fell after a demanding 2-hour session and returned to baseline within 24 hours, in parallel with weightlifting performance (Chen et al., 2011).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Most HRV research comes from **endurance sport**. A review of elite endurance athletes found that both rises and falls in HRV had been linked to poor adaptation, and recommended **rolling averages** (for example 7 days) and long-term individual monitoring rather than reacting to single daily readings (Plews et al., 2013).

**Limits.** HRV is affected by sleep, alcohol, illness, heat, hydration, caffeine, menstrual cycle phase, body position and breathing. It is noisy day to day. For a powerlifter it is an **optional extra**, not a decision-maker.

:::tip[In plain words]
Heart rate variability watches can show your body is under stress, but they are noisy and mostly tested on endurance athletes. If you use one, look at the weekly average, not today's number, and never let it overrule how your lifts are actually moving.
:::

### 7. Blood tests and hormones

<Evidence level="practice" /> Not recommended for routine readiness monitoring: the consensus statement found that no hormonal or blood marker met the criteria for diagnosing overtraining (Meeusen et al., 2013). <Evidence level="moderate" /> Objective markers also tracked training load less consistently than self-report (Saw et al., 2016). Blood tests belong with a doctor when health problems are suspected, for example to check for iron deficiency.

## Choosing your tools

| Tool | Cost | Effort per day | Evidence for readiness | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Training performance (e1RM, RPE at known load) | Free | Already in the log | Strongest | Everyone; the final judge |
| Wellness questionnaire | Free | 1 minute | Good | Everyone |
| Session RPE × minutes | Free | 10 seconds | Fair (tracks load, not readiness) | Checking that the plan's hard and easy weeks really were hard and easy |
| Bar velocity at a fixed load | Device | Built into warm-ups | Fair (needs individual baseline) | Lifters who already use VBT |
| Jump test | Mat, plate or app | 2 minutes | Fair (indirect for powerlifting) | Teams, labs, coaches with equipment |
| HRV | Sensor or app | 2–5 minutes on waking | Limited in strength sports | Optional; weekly averages only |

## Decision rules

<Evidence level="practice" /> Monitoring only helps if the response is decided **in advance**. The rules below are coaching rules of thumb, matching the thresholds in [Part 2 · Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/building-programs/11-deloads-fatigue-management/). Compare each lifter with their **own** recent normal (for example the last 2–4 weeks).

**Daily (before or during the session)**

| What you see | Status | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Wellness normal, warm-ups move normally | Green | Train as planned |
| Wellness clearly below normal, but warm-ups and RPE normal | Amber | Train as planned, cap top sets at the planned RPE (no "bonus" sets) |
| Warm-ups slow or top set RPE 1+ higher than expected | Amber | Keep the top set at the target RPE (the load drops automatically); cut back-off sets by 1–2 |
| Both wellness and performance clearly down | Red | Make it a light technique session (about RPE 6), or swap the day with a rest day |
| Pain that is sharp, worsening or changes technique | Red | Stop that lift; see a qualified health professional if it persists |

**Weekly (coach review)**

| Pattern | Likely state | Action |
|---|---|---|
| RPE up and e1RM down near the end of a hard block, wellness dipping | Expected fatigue (heading for FOR) | Deload as planned; expect a rebound |
| RPE 1+ higher than expected for 2 sessions in a row on the same lift, or e1RM down about 3–5% over 2–3 sessions | Accumulated fatigue | Bring the deload forward |
| Performance still down 1–2 weeks **after** a deload | Possible NFOR | Extend reduced training; review sleep, food intake, illness and life stress |
| Performance and well-being down for several weeks despite reduced training | Possible NFOR or a medical issue | Refer to a doctor to rule out other causes (Meeusen et al., 2013); keep training light and enjoyable |

:::tip[In plain words]
Write your rules before you need them. One bad day means adjust the session. A bad trend over a week or two means take your easy week early. Weeks of feeling bad even after resting mean see a doctor.
:::

## Sample programs

These are **monitoring plans**, not training programs. They sit alongside whatever program the lifter runs. Default lifter: squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg.

### Program 1: Minimal monitoring for any lifter (free, about 2 minutes a day)

**Before each session: wellness check (1 = very poor, 5 = very good)**

| Item | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sleep quality last night | Very poor | Normal | Excellent |
| Muscle soreness | Very sore | Normal | None |
| Energy / fatigue | Exhausted | Normal | Very fresh |
| Stress (work, study, family) | Very high | Normal | Very low |
| Motivation to train | None | Normal | Very high |

Total: 5–25. Also record **hours slept**.

**During the session:** log RPE for every working set; calculate e1RM from top sets at RPE 7.5 or higher.

**About 30 minutes after:** session RPE (0–10) × minutes.

**Weekly review (coach, 10 minutes):**

| Check | Flag |
|---|---|
| Average wellness score | 3+ points below the lifter's usual weekly average |
| Top-set e1RM per lift | Down about 3–5% across 2–3 sessions |
| RPE at a repeated load | 1+ higher than expected for 2 sessions in a row |
| Weekly session load | Planned easy week not clearly lower than the hard weeks |

**Rule:** one flag, watch. Two or more flags in the same week, apply the weekly decision rules above.

**Why it's built this way:** it uses the tools with the best evidence (performance and self-report) and costs nothing. Five items are few enough to fill in every day; the 3-point weekly threshold is a rule of thumb that should be adjusted once you know the lifter's normal range.

### Program 2: Velocity-assisted monitoring (lifter with a bar-speed device)

Everything in Program 1, plus a fixed **velocity checkpoint** on each main lift.

| Lift | Checkpoint set | Example load |
|---|---|---|
| Squat | Last warm-up: 1 rep, maximal intent | 140 kg (~78%) |
| Bench press | Last warm-up: 1 rep, competition pause | 92.5 kg (~77%) |
| Deadlift | Last warm-up: 1 rep | 165 kg (~79%) |

**How to read it:**

1. Build a baseline from the first 4–6 sessions: note the **slowest and fastest** checkpoint velocity for each lift.
2. Each session, compare the checkpoint with that range.

| Checkpoint velocity | Top-set RPE at the planned load | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Within range | As expected | Train as planned |
| Faster than range | Lower than expected | Allow the top set to go up, staying at the target RPE |
| Below range | As expected | Train as planned; watch the next session |
| Below range | 1+ higher than expected | Amber: keep the target RPE, cut back-offs by 1–2 sets |
| Below range on 2+ sessions in a row, with low wellness | — | Apply the weekly rules (bring the deload forward) |

**Why it's built this way:** velocity at a familiar submaximal load is more stable than velocity at a max (Banyard et al., 2017), and individual profiles are needed (Helms et al., 2017). Reading velocity together with RPE avoids acting on one noisy number.

### Program 3: A planned overreach with exit rules (advanced lifter, 5 weeks)

A deliberate functional overreach: two weeks above normal volume, then a reduction. Monitoring tells the coach whether it stayed **functional**. Main lifts only; loads at RPE 7–8 from the RPE chart in [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/).

| Week | Training | Squat example | Monitoring focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Normal week (baseline) | 4 × 4 @ RPE 7 (~145 kg), twice | Record baseline wellness, e1RM, checkpoint velocity |
| 2 | Overreach: about +30% hard sets | 5 × 4 @ RPE 7.5, twice, plus 3 × 3 @ RPE 7 (~150 kg) | Daily wellness; expect a dip |
| 3 | Overreach continued | Same sets, loads held | Expect RPE at the same loads to rise about 0.5–1 |
| 4 | Reduction: about −40% hard sets, intensity kept | 3 × 3 @ RPE 7.5, twice | Wellness should recover within days |
| 5 | Test week | Work up to a single @ RPE 8–8.5 (~167.5–170 kg) | e1RM vs week 1 |

**Exit rules (stop the overreach early and move straight to week 4):**

- e1RM on any main lift down more than about 5% from week 1 by mid-week 3, or
- wellness 5+ points below baseline for 3 or more days, or
- a new joint pain that changes technique, or illness.

**Reading the result:**

| Week 5 outcome | Interpretation | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| e1RM at or above week 1, wellness back to normal | Functional overreaching worked | Use a similar overreach in future blocks |
| e1RM below week 1, wellness normal | Not yet recovered | Add another light week, re-test |
| e1RM below week 1 and wellness still low | Possible NFOR | Two light weeks; next time use a smaller overreach; review sleep and food |

**Why it's built this way:** short planned overreaches followed by a reduction are common in strength training and can produce functional overreaching (Bell et al., 2020). Written exit rules stop a planned overreach from drifting into a non-functional one, and the week 1 baseline makes the result measurable.

## Common mistakes

- **Collecting data nobody uses.** If a lifter fills in a form every day and nothing ever changes, they will stop filling it in honestly.
- **Reacting to one number on one day.** Single readings of HRV, velocity or wellness are noisy; act on patterns, or on two signals agreeing.
- **Comparing lifters with each other.** Every marker here is useful only against the lifter's own normal range.
- **Calling every stall "overtraining".** True overtraining syndrome is rare in strength sports; most stalls are non-functional overreaching or simple program problems.
- **Trusting devices over performance.** When the device and the bar disagree, the bar wins.
- **Using monitoring to diagnose illness.** Long-lasting fatigue, low mood or poor performance despite rest needs a doctor, not a new spreadsheet.

## Key takeaways

- Functional overreaching is planned and leads to improvement; non-functional overreaching costs weeks; overtraining syndrome costs months and is rarely documented in strength sports.
- No blood, hormone or device marker reliably diagnoses overtraining; a sustained drop in performance is the only established marker.
- Self-report wellness questionnaires track training stress more sensitively than most objective measures.
- Session RPE × minutes shows how hard a week was; don't use workload ratios to predict injury.
- Bar velocity, jumps and HRV are useful add-ons only when measured consistently and compared with the lifter's own baseline.
- Decide responses in advance: adjust the session for one bad day, deload early for a bad trend, refer to a doctor for weeks of poor performance despite rest.
