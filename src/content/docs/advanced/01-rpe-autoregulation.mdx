## In one minute

**Autoregulation** means letting the lifter's performance on the day adjust the plan, instead of fixing every load weeks in advance. In the RPE/RIR approach popularised by Mike Tuchscherer's Reactive Training Systems (RTS), the coach prescribes **reps and a target effort** ("3 @ RPE 8"), and the lifter finds the load that meets it. Each heavy set then gives an **estimated 1RM (e1RM)** for that day, which sets the back-off sets and tracks progress across the block. Research shows RPE-based loading builds strength at least as well as percentage-based loading, with some studies and reviews suggesting a small advantage, but the method is only as good as the lifter's ratings. Calibrating those ratings, and knowing when not to trust them, is the skill this chapter teaches.

## What autoregulation is, and what it adjusts

A review of the field defines autoregulation as adjusting training based mainly on measured performance or the athlete's perceived capability to perform, and notes it has existed as a training framework since the 1940s (Greig et al., 2020). Two things can be autoregulated:

| What is adjusted | How | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Load** (intensity) | The lifter picks the weight that meets a target RPE or velocity | "Squat 1 × 3 @ RPE 8" |
| **Volume** (sets) | The number of sets stops when fatigue reaches a target | "Then repeat 3s until RPE 9" (covered in [Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/advanced/02-fatigue-based-autoregulation/)) |

This chapter covers **load** autoregulation with RPE. Volume autoregulation is the subject of [Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/advanced/02-fatigue-based-autoregulation/), and the velocity-based version of both is in [Chapter 3](/lets-git-it/advanced/03-velocity-based-training/).

**Why it matters more for advanced lifters.** <Evidence level="strong" /> Reps at a given percentage of 1RM differ widely between people and between lifts (Nuzzo et al., 2024). <Evidence level="practice" /> On top of that, an advanced lifter's strength moves up and down with sleep, stress, travel and the previous week's training, while their long-term progress is slow. A fixed percentage written a month ago can be too heavy on a bad day and too light on a good one. RPE lets the load follow the lifter (Tuchscherer).

:::tip[In plain words]
Autoregulation means the plan tells you how hard a set should feel, and you pick the weight that makes it feel that way today. On a strong day the weight goes up; on a tired day it comes down. The effort stays right, even when your body changes from day to day.
:::

## The RTS approach in practice

<Evidence level="practice" /> The core ideas of the RTS approach (Tuchscherer), in plain form:

1. **Prescribe reps and RPE, not kilograms.** "Squat 1 × 3 @ 8" means: work up until a set of 3 is RPE 8.
2. **Work up in small steps near the target.** The last warm-ups should be close enough that the lifter can predict the next jump. Jumps of about 2–4% of the expected top-set load are common near the top.
3. **One top set, then back-off work.** The top set tests the day. Back-off sets add volume at a load set from the top set.
4. **Every top set is data.** The load, reps and RPE give that day's e1RM, which tracks progress without max testing.
5. **Fatigue sets the volume.** Back-off sets can be fixed in number, or stopped when fatigue reaches a target (load drops and repeats, [Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/advanced/02-fatigue-based-autoregulation/)).

### Common prescription formats

| Written as | Means | Use |
|---|---|---|
| `x3 @ 8` | One set of 3 at RPE 8 | Top set |
| `x3 @ 8, then 3 × 3 @ 7` | Top set, then 3 back-off triples at the load that feels like RPE 7 | Strength work with volume |
| `x3 @ 8, then 3 × 3 at −5%` | Back-offs at 5% below the top set | Same, with a fixed back-off load |
| `x1 @ 8, then 4 × 4 @ 7` | Heavy single to "check in", then volume at a lower RPE | Keeps heavy practice in volume blocks |
| `4 × 5 @ 7–8` | Straight sets; adjust load so all sets land in the range | Simple, good for variations |
| `4 × 5 at 75%, cap @ 8` | Percentage with an RPE ceiling | Lifters still learning RPE |

:::tip[In plain words]
In this system the coach writes "3 reps at RPE 8" instead of "150 kg". You warm up in small steps until a set of 3 leaves about 2 reps in the tank. That set tells you how strong you are today, and the rest of the session is built from it.
:::

## Top sets and back-off sets

The top set gives the day's strength. The back-off sets give most of the training volume. There are two ways to set the back-off load.

**1. Back-offs by RPE.** The lifter picks a load that feels like the back-off RPE. Simple, but every set needs a judgement.

**2. Back-offs by percentage of the top set.** The coach converts the target RPEs with the chart in [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/):

Back-off load = top-set load × (chart % for back-off reps and RPE ÷ chart % for top-set reps and RPE)

| Top set | Back-off sets | Calculation | Back-off is… |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 @ 8 (86.3%) | 3s @ 7 (83.7%) | 83.7 ÷ 86.3 | **~3% lighter** |
| 5 @ 8 (81.1%) | 5s @ 7 (78.6%) | 78.6 ÷ 81.1 | **~3% lighter** |
| 1 @ 8 (92.2%) | 3s @ 7 (83.7%) | 83.7 ÷ 92.2 | **~9% lighter** |
| 3 @ 8 (86.3%) | 5s @ 7 (78.6%) | 78.6 ÷ 86.3 | **~9% lighter** |
| 1 @ 8 (92.2%) | 5s @ 7 (78.6%) | 78.6 ÷ 92.2 | **~15% lighter** |

**Worked example.** The 180 kg squatter's plan says `x1 @ 8, then 3 × 3 @ 7`.

- **Normal day:** the single at RPE 8 is 165 kg. Back-offs: 165 × 0.908 ≈ 150 kg.
- **Good day:** the single at RPE 8 is 172.5 kg. Back-offs: 172.5 × 0.908 ≈ 157.5 kg.
- **Bad day:** the single at RPE 8 is 157.5 kg. Back-offs: 157.5 × 0.908 ≈ 142.5 kg.

The chart is an average, so after a few weeks check that the back-off sets really feel like the target. If 3 × 3 at "RPE 7" keeps feeling like RPE 8, the lifter's personal numbers differ from the chart (see calibration below).

<Evidence level="emerging" /> This structure has been tested in nationally qualified powerlifters: in 12 lifters (9 men, 3 women) training 3 days a week for 3 weeks, a top set at a target RPE followed by back-off sets at 2%, 4% or 6% below it, repeated until the target RPE was reached again, worked as a way to autoregulate volume; bigger reductions allowed more volume (Helms et al., 2018b). Details are in [Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/advanced/02-fatigue-based-autoregulation/).

:::tip[In plain words]
Do one hard set to see how strong you are today, then do your remaining sets a little lighter. If the hard set was heavier than usual, the lighter sets go up too. If it was lighter, they come down. The whole session moves with you.
:::

## e1RM-driven programming

Every top set at a known RPE gives an **estimated 1RM**: e1RM = load ÷ chart percentage. A squat of 160 kg × 3 at RPE 8 gives 160 ÷ 0.863 ≈ **185 kg**. Advanced RPE programming uses these numbers in three ways.

**1. Plan next week's loads.** Use the e1RM to predict next week's top set, so the lifter knows roughly where to finish the warm-ups. Example: e1RM 185 kg, next week's top set is 2 @ 8.5 (90.7%): 185 × 0.907 ≈ 167.5 kg.

**2. Track progress without testing.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Plot the top-set e1RM for each competition lift every session. A rising trend across a block is the main sign the program is working.

**3. Detect fatigue.** <Evidence level="practice" /> A falling trend at a stable RPE target is an early warning. The deload rule used in this manual is a fall of about 3–5% or more across 2–3 sessions of the same lift ([Part 2 · Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/building-programs/11-deloads-fatigue-management/)).

**Rules for trustworthy e1RMs** <Evidence level="practice" /> (consistent with [Part 1 · Chapter 12](/lets-git-it/foundations/12-tracking-training/)):

- Use sets of **1–5 reps at RPE 7.5–10**. Lower ratings are less accurate.
- Compare **like with like**: the same lift, same variation, same rep count where possible.
- Use a **rolling average** of the last 2–3 top sets when setting loads, so one great or bad day doesn't swing the plan.
- Treat changes of 1–2% as noise.

**Why ratings near failure are the ones to trust.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 12 studies (414 participants) found that people tended to under-predict how many reps they had left by about one rep, with very large differences between studies, and that accuracy improved slightly closer to failure and in sets of 12 reps or fewer (Halperin et al., 2022).

:::tip[In plain words]
Every hard set tells you roughly what your max is today. Write that number down every session. Over weeks it shows whether you're getting stronger, staying the same, or getting tired, without ever testing a real max. Trust the numbers from heavy sets of five or fewer much more than the ones from easy sets.
:::

## How accurate are RPE ratings in trained lifters?

The whole method depends on this, so it is worth knowing the evidence well.

- **Powerlifters can hit target RPEs closely.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 powerlifters (9 men, 3 women), over 3 weeks of training with self-selected loads, reported RPE differed from the target by about 0.3 RPE on average. Accuracy on 8-rep squat sets was poorer than on bench press, and rating squat "power" sets (2 @ 8) took about 3 weeks to reach peak accuracy (Helms et al., 2017b).
- **Trained lifters predict 1 and 3 reps in reserve well on the bench press.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 24 resistance-trained men and women benching at 75% of 1RM, predictions of 1 and 3 RIR were off by about 0.65 reps on average, with a slight tendency to under-predict. Accuracy was not related to sex, years of training or relative strength (Refalo et al., 2024).
- **Even a true max is rarely rated 10.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 15 powerlifters, a true 1RM was rated about 9.6–9.7 on average on the squat, bench press and deadlift (Helms et al., 2017).
- **Does experience help?** The evidence is mixed. <Evidence level="emerging" /> In two small studies, experienced lifters rated near-maximal sets more accurately than novices (Zourdos et al., 2016; Ormsbee et al., 2019). <Evidence level="moderate" /> But the meta-analysis above found that training status did not clearly affect accuracy (Halperin et al., 2022).

**Practical reading.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Expect an experienced lifter's RPE to be within about half to one RPE of reality on heavy, low-rep competition lifts, and less accurate on high-rep sets, new variations and very light sets. That error is about 1.5–3% of load at low reps, which is acceptable for most training but matters when setting attempts.

:::tip[In plain words]
Experienced powerlifters are usually good at hitting the effort they're asked for, within about half a point. They're worse on long sets and on new exercises, and most people think they have fewer reps left than they really do. Ratings are good enough to train with, not perfect.
:::

## Calibrating a lifter's RPE

<Evidence level="practice" /> Calibration means checking the lifter's ratings against something objective, then correcting either the ratings or the chart.

| Method | How | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Rep-out check** | Once per block, after the planned set, the lifter predicts RIR, then keeps going to the last good rep (bench with a spotter; squat in a rack with safeties set correctly). | Whether "RPE 8" really means 2 reps left |
| **Repeat a known load** | Use the same load × reps at the start of each block (for example 150 kg × 3 squat). | Whether the RPE for a known load is drifting |
| **Video and bar speed** | Film top sets from the side; compare grinding reps with the rating. A velocity device makes this objective ([Chapter 3](/lets-git-it/advanced/03-velocity-based-training/)). | Sandbagging or over-rating |
| **Predicted vs actual top set** | Before the session, the lifter predicts the top-set load from last week's e1RM. Compare with the actual. | Whether the lifter knows their own strength |
| **Personal chart** | Log reps, RPE and e1RM for 6–8 weeks. If 5 @ 8 is consistently 83% of e1RM rather than 81%, adjust that lifter's chart. | Individual rep–percentage differences (Nuzzo et al., 2024) |

**Signs a lifter's ratings can't yet be trusted:** RPE that never goes above 8 even when bar speed collapses; the same load rated 7 one week and 9 the next with no obvious reason; e1RMs that jump more than 5% between sessions.

<Evidence level="emerging" /> RPE also tracks bar speed: in 15 powerlifters, RPE and average bar velocity were strongly related on all three lifts, and RPE and %1RM very strongly related (Helms et al., 2017). This is why a velocity device is a useful calibration tool.

:::tip[In plain words]
Check your effort ratings now and then. Once a block, after a planned set, guess how many reps you have left and then do them (safely, with a spotter or safety bars). If you said 2 and did 4, you're rating too hard and need to push a bit more on your RPE sets.
:::

## When RPE fails, and what to do

<Evidence level="practice" /> RPE is a tool, not a law. These are the common failure points and fixes:

| Situation | Problem | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Novices or lifters new to RPE** | Ratings are inconsistent | Use fixed loads or percentages with an RPE cap; teach the scale ([Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/)) |
| **High-rep sets (8+)** | Accuracy worsens with more reps (Halperin et al., 2022) | Use RPE ranges (7–8) and rep targets; don't use them for e1RM |
| **Very low RPE targets (5–6)** | Lifters under-predict reps left | Prescribe percentage or "top set −X%" instead |
| **Ego or competitive lifters** | Top sets creep up; RPE 8 becomes 9.5 | Cap the top set at a percentage of the rolling e1RM (for example no more than +3%); check video |
| **Anxious or cautious lifters** | Sandbagging: RPE 8 is really 6 | Rep-out checks; velocity; set minimum loads |
| **New exercise variations** | No reference point | Run 2–3 weeks of conservative loads before trusting ratings |
| **Pain or a niggle** | Effort ratings mix with discomfort | Stop autoregulating that lift; use a planned, lighter load and see a qualified professional if pain persists |
| **Close to a meet** | Ratings matter most when attempts are chosen | Combine RPE with planned percentages; see Part 4, Chapter 5: Attempt selection |

:::tip[In plain words]
RPE works badly for beginners, long sets, very easy sets, brand-new exercises, and lifters who always push too hard or always hold back. In those cases, set the weights for the lifter, or add limits like "no heavier than X kg", until their ratings can be trusted.
:::

## What the research says about RPE-based training

- **RPE vs percentage in trained lifters.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In an 8-week study of squat and bench press training with matched sets and reps, both percentage-based (11 lifters) and RPE-based loading (10 lifters) increased strength and muscle thickness, with no significant difference between groups. Statistical estimates suggested a likely small advantage for RPE in squat and total strength (Helms et al., 2018).
- **Reps-in-reserve loading vs fixed loading.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> A 12-week study reported greater strength improvements with loads set by reps in reserve than with fixed loading (Graham & Cleather, 2021).
- **Autoregulation in general.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 14 studies found every autoregulation method tested increased 1RM strength (Larsen et al., 2021).
- **Head-to-head meta-analyses disagree on the size of any advantage.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> One meta-analysis (6 studies on load autoregulation) found no significant difference in 1RM gains between autoregulated and standard percentage loading; the average difference was about 2 kg in favour of autoregulation (Hickmott et al., 2022). A later network meta-analysis ranked autoregulated methods (a progressive "reps-to-failure" system, RPE and velocity-based training) above percentage-based training, though differences for the back squat were small (Huang et al., 2025).
- **Daily adjustment from performance.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 23 Division I college football players over 6 weeks of preseason training, a program that adjusted load from each week's performance improved bench press 1RM and estimated squat 1RM more than a fixed linear increase. The squat result was borderline (p = 0.05) (Mann et al., 2010).

**Bottom line.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> RPE-based loading is at least as effective as percentage-based loading for strength in trained lifters, and may be slightly better. None of the studies cited here found it worse when lifters are trained to use it. Most trials are short (6–12 weeks) and small, and few use competitive powerlifters, so the size of any advantage is uncertain.

:::tip[In plain words]
Studies show training by RPE builds strength as well as training by percentages, and maybe a little better. The difference is small. The real benefit is that RPE adjusts to your day automatically, so you don't have to guess or rewrite the program when life gets in the way.
:::

## Sample programs

All three use the RPE-to-percentage chart from [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/). Kilogram values are the expected loads, rounded to 2.5 kg; the lifter adjusts on the day.

### Program 1: An RTS-style week (intermediate–advanced, 4 days)

Lifter: squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. Accessories omitted.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 1 × 3 @ 8, then 3 × 3 @ 7 | RPE 8 (~155 kg), then RPE 7 (~150 kg) |
| Mon | Bench press | 1 × 5 @ 8, then 3 × 5 @ 7 | RPE 8 (~97.5 kg), then RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| Wed | Deadlift | 1 × 2 @ 8, then 2 × 4 @ 7 | RPE 8 (~187.5 kg), then RPE 7 (~170 kg) |
| Wed | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7–8 |
| Fri | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| Fri | Bench press | 1 × 1 @ 8, then 4 × 3 @ 7 | RPE 8 (~110 kg), then RPE 7 (~100 kg) |
| Sat | Squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) |
| Sat | Bench press | 3 × 8 | RPE 8 (~87.5 kg) |
| Sat | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |

Mon squat and Wed deadlift are 48 h apart; Fri and Sat squat work is RPE ≤ 7.

**How it is built and why:** each competition lift has one session with a **top set** (a daily strength check and e1RM) and one or two sessions of **straight sets** at lower RPE for volume. Top sets sit at RPE 8, not 9–10, because the purpose is a reliable reading and practice, not a test. Variations (pause squat, close-grip bench) use RPE ranges because the lifter has no chart data for them yet. After each top set the lifter logs the e1RM (for example 160 kg × 3 @ 8 → 185 kg).

### Program 2: An e1RM-driven 4-week squat block (worked numbers)

Same lifter. Starting rolling e1RM: 180 kg. Each week's planned top-set load = rolling e1RM × chart %. The lifter adjusts the actual load to hit the RPE; the new e1RM updates the plan.

| Week | Prescription | Planned top set | What happened | New e1RM | Rolling e1RM (avg of last 2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 × 4 @ 8, then 3 × 4 at −3% | 180 × 0.837 ≈ 150 kg | 152.5 × 4 @ 8 | 152.5 ÷ 0.837 ≈ 182 kg | (180 + 182) ÷ 2 = 181 kg |
| 2 | 1 × 3 @ 8, then 3 × 3 at −3% | 181 × 0.863 ≈ 155 kg | 157.5 × 3 @ 7.5 | 157.5 ÷ 0.850 ≈ 185 kg | (182 + 185) ÷ 2 ≈ 183.5 kg |
| 3 | 1 × 2 @ 8.5, then 3 × 3 at −8% | 183.5 × 0.907 ≈ 167.5 kg | 167.5 × 2 @ 8.5 | 167.5 ÷ 0.907 ≈ 185 kg | (185 + 185) ÷ 2 = 185 kg |
| 4 | Deload: 2 × 3 at ~75% of 185 kg (~140 kg) | ~140 kg | — | Not used (RPE too low) | 185 kg |

**Back-off loads:** week 1: 152.5 × 0.97 ≈ 147.5 kg; week 2: 157.5 × 0.97 ≈ 152.5 kg; week 3: 167.5 × 0.92 ≈ 155 kg.

**Decision rules for this block** <Evidence level="practice" />:

| If… | Then… |
|---|---|
| Top set comes in 1 RPE **easier** than planned | Add 2.5–5 kg and do one more top set, or keep it and add one back-off set |
| Top set comes in 1 RPE **harder** | Accept it; drop the back-offs by a further 2–3% |
| e1RM falls ≥3–5% across 2–3 sessions | Bring the deload forward ([Part 2 · Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/building-programs/11-deloads-fatigue-management/)) |
| e1RM jumps >5% in one session | Check the rating (video); use the rolling average, not the single value |

**How it is built and why:** the rolling average stops one good day from pushing next week's loads too high. Week 4 is excluded from e1RM tracking because RPE 6–7 sets give poor estimates. The block ends with a reliable e1RM of about 185 kg, which becomes the starting point for the next block.

### Program 3: Learning to autoregulate (a lifter new to RPE, 3 days, 4 weeks)

Lifter: female, 63 kg class, squat 120 kg, bench press 65 kg, deadlift 150 kg, two years of percentage-based training. The aim is to **teach and check the ratings** before relying on them. Loads are set from the chart, and an RPE cap protects against bad days.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 5 | ~78% (92.5 kg), cap RPE 8 |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 5 | ~79% (RPE 7, 50 kg), cap RPE 8 |
| 2 | Deadlift | 3 × 4 | ~81% (122.5 kg), cap RPE 8 |
| 2 | Bench press | 1 × 3 @ 8 (lifter selects load) | ~86% (~55 kg), then 3 × 3 at −3% (~52.5 kg) |
| 3 | Squat | 1 × 3 @ 8 (lifter selects load) | ~86% (~102.5 kg), then 3 × 3 at −3% (~100 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press | 3 × 8 | RPE 7–8 (~47.5 kg) |

**Rules:**

- Weeks 1–3: the lifter **rates every working set** and predicts the RPE before each top set. Days 2 and 3 introduce lifter-selected top sets on one lift each.
- If a capped set is rated above RPE 8, drop 5% for the remaining sets.
- **Week 4 calibration:** after the last bench set of day 1, the lifter predicts reps in reserve, then continues to the last good rep with a spotter. On day 3, film the squat top set and compare bar speed and grind with the rating.
- If predictions are within 1 rep on the bench rep-out, and the squat video matches the rating, move to Program 1-style prescriptions next block. If not, repeat this format for another block.

**How it is built and why:** the lifter keeps the safety of planned loads while practising the skill that autoregulation needs. Top sets are introduced on one lift at a time so mistakes are small and easy to spot. Bench press calibration is done to failure because it is the safest lift to rep out with a spotter.

## Common mistakes

- **Turning every top set into a max.** RPE 8 means about 2 reps in reserve. Chasing "RPE 8" numbers that are really 9.5 adds fatigue and corrupts the e1RM data.
- **Big jumps near the top.** Jumping 10 kg into the top set makes it hard to land on the target RPE. Use small final warm-up jumps.
- **Using e1RMs from easy or long sets.** Only sets of about 1–5 reps at RPE 7.5+ give useful estimates.
- **Reacting to one session.** Use rolling averages and trends across 2–3 sessions.
- **Never checking the ratings.** Calibrate once a block with a rep-out set, video or bar speed.
- **Autoregulating everything for everyone.** Novices, painful lifts and brand-new variations need planned loads.

## Key takeaways

- Autoregulation adjusts load (and volume) to the lifter's day; RPE does this by prescribing reps and effort instead of kilograms.
- A top set at a target RPE gives the day's e1RM; back-off loads can be set by RPE or by a percentage drop from the top set.
- Track top-set e1RMs as a rolling average to plan loads, measure progress and catch fatigue.
- Trained lifters rate RPE within about half to one point on heavy, low-rep sets; accuracy is worse on high-rep sets and new exercises.
- RPE-based loading builds strength at least as well as percentages; any advantage is small and the studies are short.
- Calibrate ratings every block, and switch to planned loads when RPE can't be trusted.
