## In one minute

A **special exercise** copies the important features of a competition lift closely enough to transfer to it, while changing something on purpose. **Dynamic correspondence**, an idea from the Soviet sport scientist Yuri Verkhoshansky made widely known in the West by Mel Siff's *Supertraining*, gives five criteria for judging how closely an exercise matches: the **range and direction** of movement, the **region** where peak force is needed, the **pattern of effort**, the **rate and duration** of force production, and the **type of muscle action**. The more criteria an exercise meets, the more directly it should transfer; the fewer it meets, the more general it is. Research supports the broad idea, since strength gains are specific to range, movement and mode, but the five criteria themselves have not been tested as a system. Coaches use them as a structured way to rank exercises and to shift training from general to specific as a meet approaches.

## General, special and competition exercises

Soviet training theory sorted exercises into three groups by how closely they resemble the competition event. Siff presents this classification in *Supertraining*, and Sheiko's programs use the same logic, with many special variations of the three lifts.

| Group | Definition | Powerlifting examples | Main role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Competition exercise** | The event itself, as performed in competition | Squat, bench press and deadlift with competition technique and commands | Skill, maximal strength, peaking |
| **Special exercises** | Exercises that reproduce key features of the competition lift, with one or two features changed | Pause squat, long-pause bench, deficit deadlift, close-grip bench | Overloading a feature, fixing weak points, adding specific volume |
| **General exercises** | Exercises that build qualities the lift needs, without copying its movement | Leg press, rows, triceps extensions, back extensions, conditioning | Muscle size, work capacity, joint resilience, balance |

<Evidence level="practice" /> The groups are a continuum, not boxes. This chapter's job is to make the line between "special" and "general" more precise.

:::tip[In plain words]
Exercises come in three kinds: the competition lift itself, close copies of it that change one thing, and general exercises that build muscle and fitness. Close copies carry over best, but general exercises still have a job.
:::

## Dynamic correspondence: the idea

<Evidence level="practice" /> Siff, following Verkhoshansky, argued that an exercise transfers to a sport skill when it corresponds to that skill in specific ways, not just when it uses "the same muscles". Two exercises can train the same muscles and still transfer very differently if they differ in range, the region where force peaks, speed or type of muscle action (Siff).

Two general reviews support the principle while noting its limits:

- <Evidence level="practice" /> A review of transfer to sport concluded that **movement pattern and contraction velocity specificity** are important for transfer, and that coordination between muscles is a key part of it. It also noted that general strength training is useful for building muscle, reducing soft-tissue injury risk and other general purposes, but that optimal transfer requires specific training as well (Young, 2006).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> A review of training transfer described transfer as **positive, neutral or negative**, and concluded that whether training transfers depends on how well it fits the specific demands and physiological adaptations of the target performance (Issurin, 2013).

:::tip[In plain words]
An exercise helps your competition lift most when it moves the same way, is hardest in the same spot, and is done at a similar effort and speed. Using the same muscles is not enough.
:::

## The five criteria

### 1. Amplitude and direction of movement

**Does the exercise move through the same range, in the same direction, with similar joint angles?**

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Full-range training produced larger strength gains than partial-range training across 16 studies (Pallarés et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> Range-specific gains: in 17 male students, shallow-squat training improved the shallow squat by about 36% but the deep squat by only about 9% (Bloomquist et al., 2013). In 50 trained men, partial bench press training produced smaller full-range gains the shorter its range (Martínez-Cava et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Direction matters.** In adolescent male athletes over 6 weeks, hip thrust training very likely improved hip thrust strength more than front squat training did, while the front squat had a possible advantage for front squat strength and vertical jump (Contreras et al., 2017). In 22 well-trained women over 12 weeks, back squat training increased back squat 1RM by 35.9% compared with 4.3% for hip thrust training, while both groups improved the hip thrust (Barbalho et al., 2020). A horizontal hip extension did not transfer well to a vertical squat.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Strength gains are specific to equipment: free-weight training improved free-weight tests more, and machine training tended to improve machine tests more (Haugen et al., 2023).

<Evidence level="practice" /> **Powerlifting use:** a special exercise should keep the competition range, or extend it slightly (a small deficit), and keep the bar path and stance close to competition. Partial ranges score low on this criterion.

:::tip[In plain words]
The closer an exercise's range and direction are to your competition lift, the better it carries over. Half-reps and exercises that push in a different direction build strength mostly for themselves.
:::

### 2. Accentuated region of force production

**Is the greatest effort needed in the same part of the range as in the competition lift?**

In raw powerlifting the hardest region is usually low in the range: out of the hole in the squat, off the chest or just above it in the bench, and off the floor or around the knee in the deadlift. The sticking region research is covered in [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/advanced/05-weak-point-analysis/).

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of isometric training found that training at **longer muscle lengths** transferred better to dynamic performance and produced more muscle growth than training at shorter lengths (Oranchuk et al., 2019).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Exercises that load the top of the range (board presses, block pulls, heavy band tension at lockout) accentuate a region that is rarely the limit in raw lifting. They correspond poorly on this criterion unless the lifter's diagnosed weak point really is the top. Bands and chains are discussed in [Part 2 · Chapter 7](/lets-git-it/building-programs/07-conjugate-concurrent/).

:::tip[In plain words]
Pick exercises that are hardest where your competition lift is hardest. For most raw lifters that's near the bottom, so exercises that make the bottom harder usually help more than ones that make the top harder.
:::

### 3. Dynamics of the effort

**Is the size and pattern of the effort similar: heavy, maximal-intent efforts like the competition lift?**

- <Evidence level="strong" /> Heavy loads produce larger 1RM gains than light loads (Lopez et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 38 untrained people over 8 weeks, simply practising the 1RM test produced 1RM gains similar to 4 sets to failure of 8–12 reps, even though the high-volume group gained more muscle (Mattocks et al., 2017). Practising the exact maximal effort is itself a powerful stimulus.

<Evidence level="practice" /> **Powerlifting use:** a special exercise should allow loads high enough to be a real strength stimulus, typically sets of 1–6 at RPE 7–9. A variation that forces very light loads (for example a 5-second tempo) is closer to a technique drill than a strength exercise.

:::tip[In plain words]
To get strong at lifting heavy, some of your special exercises need to be heavy too. If a variation makes you use very light weights, treat it as technique practice, not as your main strength work.
:::

### 4. Rate and time of maximum force production

**Does force need to rise as fast, and be held as long, as in the competition lift?**

A maximal squat, bench or deadlift is slow: the lifter must push near-maximally for a second or more, often through a sticking region. <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 recreational lifters, the sticking period of a 1RM bench press lasted about 0.9 seconds (van den Tillaar & Ettema, 2010).

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Intent matters more than actual speed.** In 16 adults training ankle dorsiflexion for 16 weeks, trying to contract explosively produced the same velocity-specific gains whether the movement was actually fast or blocked (isometric) (Behm & Sale, 1993).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 20 participants over 6 weeks, bench pressing every rep with maximal intended speed improved bench 1RM by 18.2%, compared with 9.7% when reps were deliberately performed at half speed (González-Badillo et al., 2014).

<Evidence level="practice" /> **Powerlifting use:** every rep of a special exercise should be lifted with the intent to move fast, even when heavy loads make it slow. Exercises that are fast and light (jumps, throws) correspond poorly on time of force; they may have other uses, but not as special exercises for a 1RM.

:::tip[In plain words]
Heavy lifts are slow, but you should always try to push the bar as fast as you can. Trying to be fast makes you stronger even when the bar can't actually move quickly.
:::

### 5. Regime of muscular work

**Is the type of muscle action the same: lowering then lifting, a pause, or a lift from a dead start?**

| Competition lift | Muscle action under IPF rules |
|---|---|
| Squat | Lower, then lift without a pause (a stretch–shortening action) |
| Bench press | Lower, **hold motionless on the chest**, then press (a pause removes most of the bounce) |
| Deadlift | Lift from a dead start, with no lowering first |

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review found that eccentric (lowering-focused) training improved strength **largely in a mode-specific way**: mostly in eccentric tests (Douglas et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Isometric training produced substantial gains in maximal force, and training with ballistic intent improved rapid force production more (Oranchuk et al., 2019).

<Evidence level="practice" /> **Powerlifting use:** match the action. A paused bench press matches the competition bench well; a touch-and-go bench changes its regime. A pause squat changes the squat's regime (removes the bounce), which is exactly why it is used to build strength out of the hole, but it is less specific than the competition squat. A Romanian deadlift starts with a lowering phase, so it corresponds poorly to the deadlift's dead start.

:::tip[In plain words]
Match how the muscles work in the real lift: a bench press with a pause on the chest, a squat that goes down and straight back up, a deadlift that starts from a dead stop. Changing this on purpose is fine, but know that it makes the exercise less like the real thing.
:::

## Classifying exercises: a correspondence score

<Evidence level="practice" /> The table below is a simple **coaching tool built on Siff's five criteria**. Each criterion is scored 0 (different), 1 (partly matches) or 2 (matches), out of 10. It is not a validated scale; the scores are judgements for a typical raw, low-bar squatter, conventional deadlifter and IPF bench presser. Adjust them for your lifter.

**Criteria:** A = amplitude and direction, R = accentuated region, E = dynamics of effort, T = rate and time of force, M = regime of muscular work.

### Squat

| Exercise | A | R | E | T | M | Total | Class |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Competition squat | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | **10** | Competition |
| High-bar squat (for a low-bar lifter) | 1 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | **9** | Special |
| Pause squat (2–3 s) | 2 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 1 | **7** | Special |
| Front squat | 1 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 2 | **7** | Special / general |
| Tempo squat (4 s down) | 2 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 1 | **7** | Special (technique) |
| Box squat (below parallel) | 1 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 1 | **6** | Special / general |
| Pin squat (above parallel) | 0 | 0 | 2 | 1 | 1 | **4** | General |
| Leg press | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 2 | **5** | General |
| Split squat | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 2 | **4** | General |

### Bench press

| Exercise | A | R | E | T | M | Total | Class |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Competition bench (paused) | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | **10** | Competition |
| Long-pause bench (3 s) | 2 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 2 | **8** | Special |
| Larsen press | 2 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 2 | **8** | Special |
| Touch-and-go bench | 2 | 1 | 2 | 1 | 1 | **7** | Special |
| Close-grip bench | 1 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 2 | **7** | Special |
| Spoto press | 1 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 1 | **6** | Special |
| Pin press (top third) | 0 | 0 | 2 | 1 | 1 | **4** | General |
| Dumbbell bench press | 1 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 1 | **4** | General |

### Deadlift (conventional)

| Exercise | A | R | E | T | M | Total | Class |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Competition deadlift | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | **10** | Competition |
| Paused deadlift (just off the floor) | 2 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 1 | **7** | Special |
| Deficit deadlift (2.5–5 cm) | 1 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 2 | **7** | Special |
| Block pull (below the knee) | 0 | 0 | 2 | 1 | 2 | **5** | Special / general |
| Romanian deadlift | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0 | **3** | General |
| Hip thrust | 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 2 | **4** | General |

**How to use the scores:**

- **8–10:** use for most heavy work and close to a meet.
- **6–7:** the main special exercises of a preparatory block; good for weak points.
- **5 or less:** general exercises; good for muscle, work capacity and joints, but don't expect direct transfer.

:::tip[In plain words]
Score each exercise on the five questions: same range, hardest in the same spot, similarly heavy, pushed with the same intent, and the same kind of muscle action. High scores carry over most. Low scores are still useful for building muscle, but they won't move your total directly.
:::

## What the transfer research does and doesn't show

- **Specificity is real.** Range, direction, equipment and contraction mode all shape where strength gains appear (Pallarés et al., 2021; Haugen et al., 2023; Douglas et al., 2017; Barbalho et al., 2020).
- **Low-correspondence exercises can still help.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 18 academy rugby players over 5 weeks, training only the rear-foot-elevated split squat improved back squat 1RM about as much as training the back squat did (Speirs et al., 2016). <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 15 trained people, the front squat recruited muscles as effectively as the back squat, with lower knee compressive forces (Gullett et al., 2009). Less specific exercises can transfer, especially in less specialised athletes and over short periods, and can reduce joint stress.
- **The five criteria themselves are untested as a system.** <Evidence level="practice" /> No study has scored exercises on all five criteria and then tested whether the score predicts transfer to powerlifting. Treat the score as structured judgement.
- **Transfer likely shrinks with training age.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Coaches generally find that general exercises keep transferring to beginners but less so to advanced lifters, which is why advanced programs lean on special exercises (Siff; Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith). This fits the evidence on specificity but has not been tested directly.

:::tip[In plain words]
Research agrees that strength is specific: you get strongest at what you train. But general exercises still help, especially for newer lifters, and they can be easier on the joints. The scoring system is a helpful guide, not a proven formula.
:::

## Correspondence through the season

<Evidence level="practice" /> As a meet approaches, the share of training from high-correspondence exercises rises. These proportions are typical coaching ranges, consistent with block periodization ([Part 2 · Chapter 6](/lets-git-it/building-programs/06-block-periodization/)) and Sheiko's emphasis on special exercises:

| Phase | Competition lifts | Special exercises (score 6–9) | General exercises (score ≤ 5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| General preparation (12+ weeks out) | ~30–40% | ~25–35% | ~30–40% |
| Specific preparation (5–12 weeks out) | ~45–60% | ~25–35% | ~15–25% |
| Pre-competition (1–4 weeks out) | ~70–90% | ~10–20% | ~0–10% |

Percentages refer to each lift's weekly hard sets, including accessories for the muscles involved.

:::tip[In plain words]
Far from a meet, do plenty of general and special exercises to build muscle and fix weak spots. As the meet gets closer, do more and more of the real competition lifts, until they make up almost all of your training in the last few weeks.
:::

## Sample programs

Loads use the RPE chart from [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg. Special and general exercises are given as RPE only.

### Program 1: Squat selection by phase (intermediate, squat 180 kg)

Male lifter, 83 kg, low-bar squatter, squat 180 kg. Weak out of the hole (diagnosed as in [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/advanced/05-weak-point-analysis/)). The same lifter's squat work in two phases.

**General preparation, 14 weeks out**

| Day | Exercise | Score | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Competition squat | 10 | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~137.5 kg) |
| 1 | Leg press | 5 | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Front squat | 7 | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Split squat | 4 | 3 × 8 per leg | RPE 8 |
| 4 | Pause squat (3 s) | 7 | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 |

Weekly squat-pattern sets: 17. Competition 4 (24%), special 7 (41%), general 6 (35%).

**Specific preparation, 7 weeks out**

| Day | Exercise | Score | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Competition squat | 10 | 1 × 2 @ RPE 8, then 3 × 3 | Top ~160 kg; back-offs ~150 kg |
| 3 | Pause squat (2 s) | 7 | 4 × 3 | RPE 7.5 |
| 3 | Leg press | 5 | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 4 | Competition squat | 10 | 4 × 3 | RPE 7 (~150 kg) |

Weekly squat-pattern sets: 15. Competition 8 (53%), special 4 (27%), general 3 (20%).

**Why it's built this way.** The general phase uses lower-scoring exercises to build quads and work capacity, with only a modest share of competition squats. As the meet nears, competition squats take the majority, the pause squat stays because it targets the diagnosed weak point, and the front squat and split squat drop out. The general-phase competition share sits a little below the table's typical range on purpose, because this lifter's priority is quad muscle.

### Program 2: A 12-week bench press sequence (intermediate, bench 120 kg)

Same lifter, bench press 120 kg. Each block moves one step up the correspondence scale while keeping the competition bench in every block.

| Block | Weeks | Main special exercise (score) | Secondary exercise (score) | Competition bench |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1–4 | Close-grip bench, 4 × 6 @ RPE 7 (7) | Dumbbell bench press, 3 × 10 @ RPE 8 (4) | 2 days: 4 × 6 @ RPE 7 (~92.5 kg); 4 × 5 @ RPE 7.5 (~95 kg) |
| 2 | 5–8 | Long-pause bench (3 s), 4 × 4 @ RPE 7.5 (8) | Close-grip bench, 3 × 5 @ RPE 7.5 (7) | 2 days: 4 × 4 @ RPE 7.5 (~100 kg); 1 × 1 @ RPE 8 (~110 kg) + 3 × 3 @ RPE 7 (~100 kg) |
| 3 | 9–12 | Long-pause bench (3 s), 3 × 2 @ RPE 7.5 (8) | — | 3 days: 1 × 1 @ RPE 8–8.5 (~110–112.5 kg) + 3 × 2 @ RPE 7 (~102.5 kg); 4 × 3 @ RPE 7 (~100 kg); 3 × 2 @ RPE 7 (~102.5 kg) |

**Why it's built this way.** Block 1 builds triceps and chest with exercises that are only moderately specific. Block 2 swaps the dumbbell work for a high-scoring special exercise that targets off-the-chest strength, and adds a heavy single. Block 3 is almost entirely competition bench plus one high-scoring special exercise, ready for a peak (Part 4). At each step the lifter keeps the competition bench, so specificity never drops out.

### Program 3: Special exercises for a sumo deadlifter (female lifter, 4 days)

Female lifter, 63 kg class, sumo deadlift 150 kg, squat 120 kg, bench press 65 kg. Sumo and conventional deadlifts differ in trunk angle, thigh angle and knee and ankle moments, and the conventional lift involves more vertical bar travel and work (Escamilla et al., 2000). So her special exercises must be scored against **her** competition lift, sumo, not the conventional deadlift.

| Exercise | A | R | E | T | M | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Competition sumo deadlift | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | **10** |
| Paused sumo deadlift (just off the floor) | 2 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 1 | **7** |
| Small-deficit sumo (2.5 cm) | 1 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 2 | **7** |
| Conventional deadlift | 1 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 2 | **7** |
| Wide-stance pause squat | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | **5** |
| Romanian deadlift | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0 | **3** |

**Specific preparation week, 8 weeks out**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~97.5 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~50 kg) |
| 2 | Competition sumo deadlift | 1 × 2 @ RPE 8, then 3 × 3 | Top ~135 kg; back-offs ~125 kg |
| 2 | Bench press | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~52.5 kg) |
| 3 | Squat | 4 × 3 | RPE 7.5 (~102.5 kg) |
| 3 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Paused sumo deadlift (2 s just off the floor) | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Bench press | 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~55 kg) |

Weekly deadlift-pattern sets: competition sumo 4, paused sumo 3 (special), Romanian deadlift 3 (general).

**Why it's built this way.** The paused sumo deadlift is the main special exercise because it keeps her stance, start position and dead-start action, while adding time in the hardest region off the floor. The conventional deadlift scores reasonably but has a different range and joint demands, so it is kept for the general phase, if at all. The Romanian deadlift is a general exercise here: it builds the hamstrings and back but corresponds poorly to the sumo pull.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming "same muscles" means "same lift".** Two exercises can train the same muscles and transfer very differently.
- **Building a raw lifter's program around top-range overload.** Boards, blocks and heavy band tension accentuate a region that is rarely the raw lifter's limit.
- **Using light, slow tempos as main strength work.** Low loads score low on dynamics of effort; treat them as technique drills.
- **Scoring variations against the wrong competition lift.** A sumo puller's special exercises are sumo variations.
- **Dropping general exercises entirely.** They still build muscle, work capacity and joint tolerance, especially early in the season.
- **Treating correspondence scores as precise.** They are structured judgement, not measurements; test them against the competition lift's progress.

## Key takeaways

- Exercises sit on a continuum from general, through special, to the competition lift.
- Dynamic correspondence judges an exercise on five criteria: range and direction, accentuated region, dynamics of effort, rate and time of force, and regime of muscular work.
- Research supports specificity of range, direction, equipment and contraction mode; the five-criteria system itself is coaching theory.
- Intent to move fast matters on every rep, even heavy ones.
- Less specific exercises can still transfer, especially for less advanced lifters, and can spare the joints.
- Shift from general to special to competition exercises as the meet approaches.
