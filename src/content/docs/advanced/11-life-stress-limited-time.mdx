## In one minute

Most lifters have periods when they cannot train the way the program wants: exams, busy seasons at work, shift rotations, travel, a new baby. The research is reassuring. **Strength can still improve on a few hard sets per lift per week**, and **strength can be maintained for months on much less**, as long as the loads stay heavy. Time can be saved by cutting exercises, pairing non-competing exercises in supersets, and shortening rests on accessories, but **not** by cutting rests on heavy competition-lift sets. The skill is choosing on purpose between three modes, **progress, maintain or minimum**, and planning the switch before life forces it.

## Why this chapter exists

[Part 1 · Chapter 10](/lets-git-it/foundations/10-recovery-sleep-stress/) showed that high life stress is linked to smaller strength gains and slower recovery, and that stressed people tend to train less. This chapter is about what to **program** when time or recovery capacity drops for weeks or months. Two questions decide everything:

1. **How little training still makes progress?** (the minimum effective dose)
2. **How little training keeps what you have?** (the maintenance dose)

## The minimum effective dose for strength

- **Single sets work in trained men.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review and meta-analysis (6 studies) found that **one set per exercise, 1–3 times per week**, was enough to produce significant 1RM gains in resistance-trained men. Squat 1RM rose by about **17.5 kg** and bench press by about **8 kg** on average. The sets used 6–12 reps at about 70–85% 1RM, taken to failure, for 8–12 weeks. The authors called these gains **"suboptimal, yet significant"**. Evidence was lacking for the deadlift, for trained women and for highly trained strength athletes (Androulakis-Korakakis et al., 2020).
- **Low volume vs. high volume in trained men.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 34 trained men training 3 times a week for 8 weeks, 1, 3 or 5 sets per exercise produced **similar squat and bench press strength gains**. Muscle growth, however, rose with volume (Schoenfeld et al., 2019). Three sessions of about 13 minutes per week were enough for strength in this study.
- **More volume still helps on average.** <Evidence level="strong" /> Across many studies, strength rises with weekly volume, but with **diminishing returns**: each extra set adds less than the one before (Pelland et al., 2025). Low volume gets a lifter a good share of the gains; high volume adds more, at a higher cost in time and fatigue.

**What about powerlifters?**

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> A multi-part study of powerlifters and coaches (interviews, surveys, and two training studies with 25 intermediate–advanced lifters) suggested that lifters can expect strength gains from about **3–6 working sets of 1–5 reps per lift per week**, spread over **1–3 sessions**, at loads **above 80% 1RM** and **RPE 7.5–9.5**, for 6–12 weeks. Single reps at RPE 9–9.5 alone were less likely to produce meaningful gains; adding **2–3 back-off sets at about 80% of the single's load** may produce more. The trained powerlifters used a squat–bench–deadlift frequency of **2–3–1** sessions per week (Androulakis-Korakakis et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In a 10-lifter pilot study before a national IPF-affiliated meet, a "daily max" approach (one single per lift at RPE 9–9.5) improved the totals of some lifters and lowered others'. The authors suggested it may suit some beginner–intermediate lifters but not all (Androulakis-Korakakis et al., 2018).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> Over the long run, minimal training keeps working, but it plateaus. In records from 14,690 adults (mostly not athletes) doing one session a week of single sets to failure, strength rose by about 30–50% in the first year and had practically plateaued by 1–2 years (Steele et al., 2023).

:::tip[In plain words]
You don't need a huge program to get stronger. A few hard, heavy sets of each lift every week will still move your strength up, especially if you're not yet very advanced. More training adds more, but each extra set gives you less than the last one did. When time is short, the first few sets are the ones that count.
:::

## The maintenance dose

- **Intensity is the key variable.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A review of maintenance research concluded that strength and muscle size (at least in younger people) can be maintained for **up to 32 weeks with as little as one session a week and one set per exercise**, as long as the **relative load stays the same**. Older adults may need up to two sessions a week and 2–3 sets per exercise to keep muscle size. There were not enough data to make specific recommendations for athletes (Spiering et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 70 adults who trained 3 days a week for 16 weeks, then spent 32 weeks at **one-third or one-ninth** of the training dose, both reduced doses kept the muscle gained in younger adults (20–35), but not in older adults (60–75). Strength was largely retained in both age groups (Bickel et al., 2011).
- **Stopping completely costs more over time.** <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 103 studies found that stopping resistance training reduces all aspects of strength, with **larger losses the longer the break**, and larger losses in people over 65 (Bosquet et al., 2013).
- **Short breaks cost little.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 power athletes who stopped training for **14 days**, bench press (−1.7%) and squat (−0.9%) did not change significantly. Eccentric knee strength fell by 12% and type II muscle fibres shrank by about 6% (Hortobágyi et al., 1993). <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 14 untrained young men doing bench press training, a program with 3-week breaks after every 6 weeks of training produced similar muscle and strength gains over 24 weeks to continuous training (Ogasawara et al., 2013).

**What this means for coaches:** <Evidence level="practice" /> in a maintenance block, **cut volume and frequency, not load**. Keep at least one heavy exposure (about RPE 8) per competition lift per week, or every 10 days for deadlifts. A week or two off for illness, a holiday or a family event costs little; months off cost more.

:::tip[In plain words]
Keeping strength is much easier than building it. One or two short sessions a week can hold your strength for months, as long as you still lift heavy. Cut the number of sets, not the weight on the bar. A week or two off won't undo your progress.
:::

## Time-efficient methods

### What to cut first

<Evidence level="practice" /> A narrative review on time-efficient strength training recommended prioritising **multi-joint exercises** and **weekly volume over frequency**. It suggested a minimum of about **4 weekly sets per muscle group**, and recommended keeping warm-ups specific to the exercise (Iversen et al., 2021). For powerlifters, cut in this order:

| Order | Cut | Keep |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Isolation accessories, extra variations | Competition lifts |
| 2 | Long general warm-ups (keep 3–5 min plus ramp-up sets) | Specific warm-up sets on the barbell |
| 3 | Back-off volume (from e.g. 4–5 sets to 2–3) | Top sets at RPE 7.5–9 |
| 4 | Training days (from 4–5 to 2–3) | Each competition lift at least 1–2× per week |
| Never | Load on heavy competition-lift sets | — |

<Evidence level="strong" /> When total weekly volume is equal, training frequency made **no clear difference** to strength gains (Grgic et al., 2018). Two longer sessions can therefore replace four shorter ones if the hard sets are kept.

:::tip[In plain words]
When you're short on time, drop the small extra exercises first and keep squat, bench and deadlift. Do fewer sets if you must, but keep the weights heavy. Two good sessions a week can cover a lot of ground.
:::

### Supersets and paired sets

A **superset** pairs two exercises done back to back, or alternated with short rests, to fit more work into less time.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 19 studies (313 participants) found that, compared with traditional sets, supersets allowed **similar total reps and volume load in shorter sessions**, with **similar long-term gains in maximal strength and muscle size**. Supersets felt harder (higher RPE) and produced more lactate. **Agonist–antagonist pairs** (for example a press with a row) allowed more reps than traditional sets, while pairs using **similar muscles** reduced volume load (Zhang et al., 2025).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 14 men, superset and tri-set sessions were more time-efficient than traditional sets. However, jump performance was still reduced 24 hours later, while it had recovered after traditional sets. The authors suggested extra recovery may be needed (Weakley et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> An earlier review concluded that agonist–antagonist paired sets **may** be a time-efficient way to build strength, but the evidence was equivocal (Robbins et al., 2010). The time-efficiency review judged that supersets, drop sets and rest-pause training roughly halve training time, but are **probably better for muscle growth than for maximal strength** (Iversen et al., 2021).

**Rules for powerlifters:** <Evidence level="practice" />

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Pair a **competition lift** with a low-fatigue exercise for an unrelated area during its normal rest (e.g. bench press with a leg curl or calf raise) | Superset two competition lifts, or a competition lift with anything that tires the same muscles, grip or trunk |
| Pair two **accessories** freely (e.g. dumbbell row + split squat; triceps + biceps) | Shorten the rest of the competition lift to fit the partner exercise in |
| Keep the partner exercise easy enough (RPE 7–8) that it doesn't drain the next main set | Pair anything with a heavy deadlift or heavy squat; the whole body needs that rest |

:::tip[In plain words]
Supersets mean doing two exercises back to back to save time. They work well for extra exercises. Don't use them to rush your heavy squats, benches or deadlifts. For those, you can slip a small, unrelated exercise into the rest period, but still take the full rest.
:::

### Rest periods

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 23 studies found that **robust strength gains are possible even with short rests (under 60 s)**, but that **rests longer than 2 minutes** seem needed to maximise strength in trained lifters (Grgic et al., 2018b).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 21 trained men, 3-minute rests produced larger squat and bench press 1RM gains than 1-minute rests over 8 weeks (Schoenfeld et al., 2016).

<Evidence level="practice" /> **Practical rule:** keep **3+ minutes** between heavy competition-lift sets. Cut accessory rests to **60–90 seconds**, or superset them. That is where most of the time is saved.

:::tip[In plain words]
Rest fully between your heavy sets: about three minutes or more. Short rests are fine for the small extra exercises, and that's where you can save time.
:::

## Planning around life

### Predictable vs. unpredictable stress

<Evidence level="practice" /> Predictable stress (exam periods, tax season, a wedding, a planned trip) should be **planned for in the training calendar**. Unpredictable stress (illness in the family, a sudden deadline) is handled **in the moment** with RPE caps and a minimum session.

| Situation | Mode | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Normal life, 3–5 sessions available | **Progress** | Regular block (Parts 2–3) |
| Busy period, 2–3 shorter sessions, sleep OK | **Progress (low volume)** | 3–6 hard sets per lift per week at RPE 7.5–9 (Program 1 or 2) |
| Exams, work peak, new baby, poor sleep for weeks | **Maintain** | 1–2 sessions, heavy but few sets (Program 3) |
| Crisis week, illness in the family, no sleep | **Minimum** | One 20–30-minute session: one squat or deadlift exercise and bench press, 2–3 sets at RPE 7; or rest |
| After the busy period | **Rebuild** | One week at about 70% of normal volume, then back to the regular block |

<Evidence level="practice" /> **Sequence blocks around the calendar.** Put high-volume or overreaching blocks in calm periods, and plan a maintenance block for the known busy weeks. Don't schedule a peak for a meet in the week after final exams.

:::tip[In plain words]
If you know a busy time is coming, plan a lighter training block for it in advance. If something unexpected happens, do a short, easy-ish session or take a rest day. Save your hardest training for the calm stretches.
:::

### Shift work

No studies have tested powerlifting programs in shift workers, so the following is coaching practice. <Evidence level="practice" />

- **Anchor training to waking, not to the clock.** Train 1–4 hours after the main sleep period ends, whatever time that is.
- **Avoid hard sessions straight after a night shift**, before sleeping. Train after sleep instead, or make it a rest day.
- **Use flexible days, not fixed weekdays.** Write the week as "Session A, Session B, Session C" and fit them into the roster with at least one day between sessions where possible.
- **Watch caffeine.** Shift workers often use caffeine heavily. Keep the same cut-offs relative to the planned sleep: ideally about 9 hours for coffee and about 13 hours for a typical pre-workout dose (Gardiner et al., 2023).
- **Use RPE.** Readiness varies more across a rotating roster; RPE-based loading ([Part 3 · Chapter 1](/lets-git-it/advanced/01-rpe-autoregulation/)) adjusts automatically.

### Travel

- <Evidence level="practice" /> An expert consensus on travel fatigue and jet lag in athletes agreed that both can affect performance and illness risk, but noted that evidence on the best management is lacking. It offered pre-flight, in-flight and post-flight "toolboxes" built on circadian principles, such as using light exposure and sleep timing to adjust to the new time zone (Janse van Rensburg et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> For lifters: a trip of up to a week can simply be a deload or a break, because short breaks cost little strength (Hortobágyi et al., 1993). For longer trips, find a gym in advance and run the maintenance template. If only dumbbells are available, train hard on dumbbell presses, split squats, single-leg Romanian deadlifts and rows, then return to the barbell with one lighter week.

:::tip[In plain words]
If you work shifts, train after you've slept, not at a fixed clock time, and fit sessions around your roster. If you travel for a week, treat it as a rest; for longer trips, find a gym and do a short maintenance program.
:::

## Sample programs

Loads come from the RPE chart in [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg. Programs 1 and 2 use the default intermediate lifter: squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg.

### Program 1: Two sessions per week (low-volume progress)

For a lifter with only two free evenings a week, about 70 minutes each, and decent sleep. Built on the powerlifting minimum-dose findings: a heavy top set plus a few back-off sets, 3–8 hard sets per lift per week (Androulakis-Korakakis et al., 2021).

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Squat | 1 × 3, then 2 × 4 | RPE 8 (~155 kg), then RPE 7.5 (~147.5 kg) |
| A | Bench press | 1 × 3, then 3 × 5 | RPE 8 (~102.5 kg), then RPE 7.5 (~95 kg) |
| A | Pull-up (paired with bench back-offs, in the rest period) | 3 × 6–8 | RPE 8 |
| A | Split squat + dumbbell row (superset, 60–90 s rest) | 2 × 10 each | RPE 8 |
| B | Deadlift | 1 × 2, then 2 × 3 | RPE 8 (~187.5 kg), then RPE 7.5 (~177.5 kg) |
| B | Squat | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 (~150 kg) |
| B | Bench press (2 s pause) | 4 × 4 | RPE 7.5 |
| B | Leg curl (paired with paused bench, in the rest period) | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |

**Weekly hard sets:** squat 6, bench press 8, deadlift 3.

**Why it's built this way:**

- Each lift gets a top set at RPE 8 and back-off sets at RPE 7–7.5, with loads above 80% 1RM. This is the structure most associated with gains at minimal doses in powerlifters. Single top sets alone are less likely to be enough.
- Squat and bench press are trained twice a week, and deadlift once.
- The accessories are paired with competition lifts or with each other, so they add little time.
- **Progression:** when a top set at RPE 8 goes up by 2.5–5 kg across two weeks, back-offs follow automatically (they are set from the day's top set). Deload every 4–6 weeks, or when top sets drop for two sessions running.
- **Expectation:** steady but slower progress than a full 4-day program, especially for muscle growth (Schoenfeld et al., 2019).

### Program 2: Three sessions of 45 minutes

For a lifter who can train three times a week but only for 45 minutes, for example at lunch or before work.

**Time budget per session:** 5 min general warm-up → 18–20 min first lift (ramp-up sets + work sets, 3 min rests) → 12–14 min second lift with a paired accessory → 6–8 min accessory superset.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~145 kg) | 3 min |
| 1 | Bench press, paired with barbell row | 4 × 5 + 4 × 8 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) + RPE 8 | 90 s between each exercise |
| 1 | Back extension + dumbbell curl (superset) | 2 × 12 each | RPE 8 | 60 s |
| 2 | Deadlift | 3 × 3 | RPE 7.5 (~177.5 kg) | 3–4 min |
| 2 | Bench press, paired with face pull | 3 × 3 + 3 × 15 | RPE 8 (~102.5 kg) + RPE 8 | 90 s between each exercise |
| 2 | Dumbbell press + leg curl (superset) | 2 × 10 each | RPE 8 | 60 s |
| 3 | Squat | 1 × 2, then 2 × 4 | RPE 8 (~160 kg), then RPE 7 (~145 kg) | 3 min |
| 3 | Close-grip bench press, paired with ab wheel | 3 × 6 + 3 × 10 | RPE 7 + RPE 8 | 90 s between each exercise |
| 3 | Romanian deadlift | 2 × 8 | RPE 7 | 2 min |

**Weekly hard sets:** squat 7, bench press 10 (including close-grip), deadlift 5 (including Romanian deadlift).

**Why it's built this way:**

- Heavy competition-lift sets keep **full 3+ minute rests**, because longer rests give larger strength gains in trained lifters (Grgic et al., 2018b; Schoenfeld et al., 2016).
- Bench press is **paired with a pulling or core exercise** (agonist–antagonist or unrelated), which saves time without reducing bench volume (Zhang et al., 2025). Squats and deadlifts are not paired: they need whole-body rest.
- Accessories are supersets with short rests, because that is where time is saved at the least cost.
- **If the session runs late:** drop the last superset first, then one back-off set. Never rush the heavy sets.

### Program 3: A maintenance block (1–2 sessions per week, 4–8 weeks)

**Lifter:** 30-year-old male nurse on rotating shifts, 74 kg class. Squat 160 kg, bench press 105 kg, deadlift 190 kg. Normally trains 4 days a week (about 12 squat, 15 bench and 7 deadlift hard sets). For the next 6 weeks he has a heavy roster plus a family commitment, so he aims to **keep** his strength.

Sessions are labelled A and B and placed into the roster after a main sleep, at least 2 days apart. About 40 minutes each.

| Session | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Squat | 1 × 3, then 1 × 5 | RPE 8 (~137.5 kg), then RPE 7 (~125 kg) |
| A | Bench press | 1 × 3, then 2 × 5 | RPE 8 (~90 kg), then RPE 7 (~82.5 kg) |
| A | Chin-up | 2 × 6–8 | RPE 8 |
| B | Deadlift | 1 × 3, then 1 × 3 | RPE 8 (~165 kg), then RPE 7 (~160 kg) |
| B | Bench press | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 (~85 kg) |
| B | Squat | 2 × 3 | RPE 7 (~135 kg) |

**Weekly hard sets:** squat 4, bench press 6, deadlift 2. That is about a third of his normal volume, with the same top-set intensity.

**Rules:**

| Week situation | Do |
|---|---|
| Two sessions possible | A and B |
| Only one session possible | Session A plus 1 × 3 deadlift at RPE 7 (~160 kg) |
| No session possible, or straight off nights with no sleep | Rest; one missed week costs little |
| Top sets feel 1+ RPE heavier for two weeks in a row | Check sleep and stress first; keep the loads, drop the back-off sets |

**Why it's built this way:**

- Intensity is kept and volume is cut, which is the pattern that maintains strength for months (Spiering et al., 2021).
- Each competition lift gets a heavy exposure at about RPE 8 every week, so the skill of handling heavy loads stays sharp.
- **Returning:** in the first week back, run his normal program at about 70% of the usual sets. In week 2, return to full volume. Expect top sets to be back to normal within 1–3 weeks; if they are not, check sleep before blaming the break.

## Common mistakes

- **All or nothing.** Skipping training entirely because the full program doesn't fit. Two short heavy sessions keep strength; zero sessions slowly lose it.
- **Cutting load instead of volume.** Light "pump" sessions during busy times don't maintain maximal strength; heavy top sets do.
- **Rushing heavy sets to save time.** Short rests on competition lifts lower the quality of the work. Save time on accessories instead.
- **Supersetting the wrong things.** Pairing squats with lunges or deadlifts with rows tires the same muscles and lowers the main lift.
- **Scheduling a high-volume or peaking block over exams or a work peak.** Put hard blocks in calm periods; plan maintenance for busy ones.
- **Training hard straight after a night shift.** Sleep first, then train.
- **Expecting full progress from a minimal program.** Low volume maintains strength and can still build it, but muscle growth and long-term progress are slower.

## Key takeaways

- A few hard sets per lift per week, roughly 3–6 sets of 1–5 reps above 80% 1RM, can still build strength, though gains are smaller than with more volume.
- Strength can be maintained for months with 1–2 sessions per week if the loads stay heavy. Cut volume, not intensity.
- Short breaks (1–2 weeks) cost little strength; long breaks cost more, especially for older lifters.
- Save time by dropping isolation work, pairing non-competing exercises and shortening accessory rests. Keep 3+ minute rests on heavy competition-lift sets.
- Plan progress, maintain and minimum modes around the calendar. Shift workers should anchor training to waking time, and travellers can treat short trips as a deload.
