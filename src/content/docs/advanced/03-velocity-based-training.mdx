## In one minute

Velocity-based training (VBT) uses a device to measure how fast the bar moves and uses that speed to guide training. Because bar speed falls in a very regular way as the load gets heavier, a lifter's **load–velocity profile** shows how heavy a weight is for them today, and **velocity loss** within a set shows how much fatigue has built up. Meta-analyses find VBT builds maximal strength about as well as percentage-based training, with a small advantage in some analyses, but almost none of the studies used competitive powerlifters. Predicting a true 1RM from bar speed is not reliable enough to replace testing, and cheaper devices can be inaccurate. Used well, VBT is an objective check on RPE, a readiness test and a way to stop sets at the right time.

## What is measured

| Measure | What it is | Use in powerlifting |
|---|---|---|
| **Mean concentric velocity (MV)** | Average bar speed over the lifting (upward) phase of a rep | The standard measure for squat, bench press and deadlift |
| **Mean propulsive velocity (MPV)** | Average speed over only the part of the lift where the bar is being accelerated | Used in much of the research, especially for the bench press at lighter loads |
| **Peak velocity** | Fastest instant of the rep | Better suited to jumps and Olympic lifts; less useful for the competition lifts |

<Evidence level="practice" /> For powerlifting, use **mean concentric velocity** and keep the same measure for the life of a lifter's data. Velocities are in metres per second (m/s).

:::tip[In plain words]
VBT puts a sensor on the bar or the lifter and measures how fast each rep moves on the way up. Heavier weights move slower, and tired muscles move the bar slower. That speed number tells you how heavy the weight really is for you today and how tired you are getting.
:::

## The load–velocity profile

**The relationship.** As load rises, bar speed falls almost in a straight line.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 120 strength-trained men on the bench press, bar speed and %1RM were almost perfectly related (R² = 0.98). After 6 weeks of training, 1RM rose by about 9% on average but the speed at each *percentage* of 1RM stayed the same (González-Badillo & Sánchez-Medina, 2010). Speed therefore tells you the relative load, even as the lifter gets stronger.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 20 strength-trained men, the deadlift showed a similarly close load–velocity relationship (R² ≈ 0.97) (Morán-Navarro et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 15 powerlifters, bar speed was very strongly related to %1RM and strongly related to RPE on all three lifts. Because of differences between lifters, the authors recommended building an **individual** profile rather than using group values (Helms et al., 2017).

**Speeds differ a lot between lifts.**

| Study | Lifters | Mean speed at 1RM |
|---|---|---|
| Helms et al., 2017 | 15 powerlifters | Squat ≈ 0.23 m/s; bench ≈ 0.10 m/s; deadlift ≈ 0.14 m/s |
| González-Badillo & Sánchez-Medina, 2010 | 120 strength-trained men | Bench ≈ 0.16 m/s |
| Morán-Navarro et al., 2021 | 20 strength-trained men | Deadlift ≈ 0.24 m/s |

The deadlift values differ between studies (0.14 vs 0.24 m/s), which is one more reason to profile each lifter rather than borrow numbers.

**Individual or group profiles?** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 6 weeks of squat training, setting loads from each lifter's own profile and from a group-average profile both improved 1RM (about 10% and 7%) with no significant difference between groups, though the individual approach produced larger changes overall (Dorrell et al., 2020b).

:::tip[In plain words]
For each lift, there's a straight-line link between how heavy the bar is and how fast you can move it. Measure a few warm-up weights and you get your own "speed map". Then the speed of any rep tells you roughly what percentage of your max you're lifting. Make your own map; other people's numbers can be quite different.
:::

## The minimal velocity threshold, and why velocity can't replace a max test

The **minimal velocity threshold (MVT)** is the average speed of a lifter's slowest successful rep, usually their 1RM. In theory, extending the load–velocity line down to the MVT predicts the 1RM. In practice this is unreliable:

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 17 strength-trained men who tested their squat 1RM on three days, the true 1RM was very stable (variation about 2%), but the speed of the 1RM varied a lot (variation about 22%). Every velocity-based 1RM prediction differed significantly from the true 1RM, and the authors advised against using the method to adjust daily training loads (Banyard et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 11 resistance-trained athletes on the deadlift, velocity-predicted 1RMs were reliable from day to day but consistently **overestimated** the true 1RM, with large differences between athletes, while the speed of the 1RM varied by about 16% (Ruf et al., 2018).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 40 young and middle-aged trained men, using published "reference" MVT values to predict 1RM gave errors of roughly 6–20% across the squat, bench press and row; the authors concluded the 1RM should be tested directly (Fernandes et al., 2021).

**What this means.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Use velocity to track **trends** and to set **training** loads in the 70–90% range, where speeds are higher and more stable. Use real 1RM tests, meets or RPE-based e1RMs from heavy sets ([Chapter 1](/lets-git-it/advanced/01-rpe-autoregulation/)) to judge the max. Never choose competition attempts from a velocity prediction alone.

:::tip[In plain words]
In theory, your speed map can predict your max. In practice, how fast your true max moves changes a lot from day to day, so the prediction can be off by many kilograms. Use speed to guide your training weights, not to decide what you can lift on the platform.
:::

## Velocity loss

Velocity loss is the percentage drop in speed from the fastest rep of a set to the current rep, used to end sets at a chosen level of fatigue. It is covered in detail, with the training studies, in [Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/advanced/02-fatigue-based-autoregulation/). The key points:

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Velocity loss allowed during training did not change strength gains in a meta-regression, while more velocity loss was linked with more muscle growth (Jukic et al., 2023). Another meta-analysis found a small strength advantage for limits of 25% or less (Hickmott et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> Bench press speed drops sooner within a set than squat speed (Izquierdo et al., 2006), so the same velocity-loss limit means a different closeness to failure on each lift.

:::tip[In plain words]
As you get tired during a set, the bar slows down. You can choose to end every set once it has slowed by, say, 20%. Stopping earlier keeps you fresh; stopping later builds more muscle.
:::

## Devices and how far to trust them

| Device type | How it works | Reliability (research summary) |
|---|---|---|
| **Linear position transducer (LPT)** | A cable attached to the bar measures its movement directly | Most accurate and reliable group overall (Weakley et al., 2021) |
| **Inertial sensor (IMU)** | Accelerometer worn on the bar or arm | Generally poorer reliability and validity than LPTs (Thompson et al., 2020) |
| **Phone or video app** | Tracks bar or plate movement from video | One app was comparable to the best LPT for the squat in one study (Thompson et al., 2020); depends heavily on filming setup |

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 44 studies found linear position transducers more accurate and reliable than other device types. It also warned that many validation studies did not use a gold-standard reference, so errors for some devices may be under- or over-reported (Weakley et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> Comparing devices against 3D motion capture in the back squat and power clean, a GymAware LPT was the most reliable and valid device; the MyLift app performed similarly in the squat; PUSH showed some bias; and Beast Sensor and Bar Sensei were the least reliable and valid (Thompson et al., 2020). Device models and software change, so check for newer validation studies before buying.

**Practical requirements** <Evidence level="practice" />:

- Use **one device per lifter**, mounted the same way every time. Readings from different devices are not interchangeable.
- Record the **fastest rep** of each warm-up and the **first rep** of each working set as the reference.
- Keep technique constant: same depth, same bench pause, same belt and equipment. A deeper squat or longer pause changes the speed.
- Lift every rep with **maximal intent**. VBT only works if the lifter tries to move the bar fast; this is also good training ([Part 2 · Chapter 7](/lets-git-it/building-programs/07-conjugate-concurrent/)).
- Expect some noise. Before reacting to a change in speed, learn the lifter's normal session-to-session variation (see Program 1).

:::tip[In plain words]
The best devices use a cable clipped to the bar. Cheaper wearable sensors are often less accurate. Whatever you use, use the same one, set it up the same way, and push every rep as fast as you can, or the numbers mean little.
:::

## What the research says: VBT vs percentage-based training

**Meta-analyses.**

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Six studies (124 participants): no significant difference between VBT and percentage-based training in back squat 1RM; on average about 3 kg in favour of VBT (Liao et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Seven studies (139 participants): a small overall advantage for VBT in maximal strength, clearer in athletes (Zhang et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A network meta-analysis (14 studies, 311 participants) ranked velocity-loss training above both general VBT and traditional training; general VBT and traditional training ranked about the same (Held et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of load autoregulation (RPE and velocity) found no significant difference from standard percentage loading for 1RM (Hickmott et al., 2022).

**Individual studies in trained people.**

| Study | Participants | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Dorrell et al., 2020 | 16 trained men, 6 weeks | Both improved; bench press improved more with VBT (8% vs 4%), with less total volume lifted |
| Banyard et al., 2021 | 24 resistance-trained men, 6 weeks of squats | Both improved 1RM; a small, unclear 1RM difference favoured percentages; VBT improved jump and sprint more |
| Orange et al., 2020 | 27 academy rugby league players, 7 weeks in season | Both improved squat 1RM; VBT sessions felt less stressful |
| Shattock & Tee, 2022 | 20 amateur rugby union players, two 6-week blocks | VBT produced larger squat and jump improvements than RPE-based loading |

**Bottom line.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> VBT builds maximal strength about as well as percentage-based training, possibly slightly better. Velocity-loss limits appear to be the most useful part. No trial has tested VBT in competitive powerlifters over a full training cycle, and most used squats or bench press with athletes from other sports, so the results should be applied to powerlifting with care.

:::tip[In plain words]
Training by bar speed works about as well as training by percentages, perhaps a little better. The part with the most support is using speed to decide when to stop a set. Most studies were done on other athletes, not powerlifters, so it's a useful tool, not a magic one.
:::

## Practical setup for powerlifters

<Evidence level="practice" /> A sensible way to add VBT without overhauling a program:

1. **Build the profile.** On 3 sessions over 2 weeks, record the fastest rep at 4–5 warm-up loads from about 50% to 90% of 1RM. Average the sessions and fit a straight line.
2. **Pick a standard load.** Choose a load around 75–80% of 1RM for each lift and record its speed at every session. This is the **readiness check**.
3. **Set velocity zones** for the main work from the profile (see Program 2). Adjust the load by 2.5–5 kg when the first rep falls outside the zone.
4. **End sets by velocity loss** using the phase targets in [Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/advanced/02-fatigue-based-autoregulation/).
5. **Use velocity to calibrate RPE.** If the lifter calls a set RPE 7 but it moved at their usual RPE 9 speed, believe the speed and discuss the rating ([Chapter 1](/lets-git-it/advanced/01-rpe-autoregulation/)).
6. **Rebuild the profile** every block or two, and after any technique change.

**Lift-specific notes** <Evidence level="practice" />:

- **Squat:** the most consistent lift for VBT; depth must be the same every rep.
- **Bench press:** measure the press after a competition-style pause. Speed falls quickly near the max, so the useful range is smaller.
- **Deadlift:** heavy deadlifts start slowly and can grind for a long time; speeds near the max are low and variable. Use VBT mainly for the 70–85% range and for velocity loss on volume work.

:::tip[In plain words]
Start simple: measure your warm-ups to make your speed map, check the speed of one standard weight each session to see how ready you are, and use speed to know when to stop a set. You don't need to change the whole program to get value from a device.
:::

## Limitations

<Evidence level="practice" /> These are coaching observations, except where a study is cited.

- **1RM prediction is unreliable** (Banyard et al., 2017; Ruf et al., 2018; Fernandes et al., 2021).
- **Device error.** Cheaper devices can be off by more than the changes you are trying to detect (Thompson et al., 2020).
- **Technique changes speed.** Depth, pause length, bar path and equipment all change velocity, not only fatigue or readiness.
- **Speed is not everything.** A slow lifter with great technique and grit can still make a lift that their numbers say is impossible. Speed does not measure skill or competition-day arousal.
- **Distraction and obsession.** Some lifters chase numbers on the screen and lose focus on technique. Some coaches drown in data. Track a few numbers well.
- **Cost and access.** Good devices are expensive. A well-calibrated RPE system ([Chapter 1](/lets-git-it/advanced/01-rpe-autoregulation/)) achieves much of the same at no cost; RPE and speed are closely related in powerlifters (Helms et al., 2017).

## Sample programs

Lifter: squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. **The velocity numbers below are an illustrative profile for this one lifter, not reference values**; build your own.

**Example profiles (mean concentric velocity, fastest rep):**

| Squat load | Speed | | Bench load | Speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 kg | 0.85 m/s | | 70 kg | 0.80 m/s |
| 120 kg | 0.71 m/s | | 85 kg | 0.61 m/s |
| 140 kg | 0.56 m/s | | 100 kg | 0.42 m/s |
| 160 kg | 0.42 m/s | | 110 kg | 0.29 m/s |

From these, each 0.01 m/s is about **1.4 kg** on this lifter's squat and about **0.8 kg** on the bench press.

### Program 1: Building the profile and a daily readiness check

**Weeks 1–2 (profiling).** On each of three sessions, warm up to the day's work using the loads above, 1–3 reps per load with maximal intent, and record the fastest rep. Average the three sessions. Then record the speed of the **standard load** (squat 140 kg × 1, bench 100 kg × 1) on every main session for 10 sessions to learn its normal range.

**Example result:** squat 140 kg averages 0.56 m/s; the usual session-to-session range is about ±0.03 m/s.

**Readiness rules from week 3** <Evidence level="practice" /> (thresholds are examples; set them from your lifter's own normal range):

| Standard-load speed vs. average | Interpretation | Session adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Within ±0.03 m/s | Normal day | Train as planned |
| 0.04–0.07 m/s slower | Moderately fatigued | Reduce top-set load about 5%; use the lower fatigue target |
| 0.08 m/s or more slower | Very fatigued, or technique/setup changed | Check setup; if it's real, swap heavy work for volume at RPE ≤ 7 and review recovery |
| 0.04 m/s or more faster | Good day | Allow the top set at the upper end of its RPE range |

**Sample week using it:**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat: readiness single | 1 × 1 | 140 kg (record speed) |
| 1 | Squat | 1 × 3 @ 8, then 3 × 3 at −3% | RPE 8 (~155 kg), adjusted by readiness |
| 1 | Bench press: readiness single | 1 × 1 | 100 kg (record speed) |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 1 × 2 @ 8, then 2 × 4 | RPE 8 (~187.5 kg), then ~170 kg |
| 3 | Squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press: readiness single | 1 × 1 | 100 kg (record speed) |
| 3 | Bench press | 1 × 1 @ 8, then 4 × 3 | RPE 8 (~110 kg), then ~100 kg |

**How it is built and why:** the program is unchanged from a normal RPE-based week; velocity only adds an objective readiness check. The standard single costs almost nothing and catches fatigue earlier than waiting for missed reps.

### Program 2: A velocity-zone strength block (squat and bench press, 4 weeks)

Loads are chosen so that the **first rep** of each set falls in the target zone; sets end at the velocity-loss limit or the maximum reps, whichever comes first.

| Week | Squat zone (first rep) | Expected squat load | Bench zone (first rep) | Expected bench load | Velocity-loss limit | Sets × max reps |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.55–0.60 m/s | ~135–140 kg | 0.55–0.60 m/s | ~85–90 kg | 20% squat / 25% bench | 5 × 6 |
| 2 | 0.50–0.55 m/s | ~140–147.5 kg | 0.48–0.53 m/s | ~90–95 kg | 20% | 5 × 5 |
| 3 | 0.42–0.47 m/s | ~152.5–160 kg | 0.38–0.43 m/s | ~100–102.5 kg | 15% | 5 × 3 |
| 4 | Deload: ≥ 0.60 m/s | ≤ ~135 kg | ≥ 0.60 m/s | ≤ ~85 kg | 10% | 3 × 3 |

**Worked example (week 2 squat):** first set at 145 kg, first rep 0.52 m/s: in the zone. The 20% limit is 0.52 × 0.8 ≈ 0.42 m/s. Rep 4 moves at 0.43 m/s, rep 5 at 0.41 m/s: the set ends after rep 5 (the maximum anyway). If the next set's first rep is 0.49 m/s (below the zone), take off 2.5–5 kg.

**Calculations:** load = (intercept − target speed) ÷ slope, from the lifter's profile. For example, squat at 0.55 m/s: (1.575 − 0.55) ÷ 0.00725 ≈ 141 kg; bench at 0.55 m/s: (1.687 − 0.55) ÷ 0.01267 ≈ 90 kg. These coefficients come from the example profile above.

**How it is built and why:** speed zones replace percentages, so loads follow the lifter's readiness each session. Velocity-loss limits fall from 20–25% to 15% as the block becomes heavier, following the pattern in [Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/advanced/02-fatigue-based-autoregulation/). The bench press uses a slightly higher limit in week 1 because its speed drops sooner within a set.

### Program 3: Velocity as a check on RPE (deadlift and bench press, 4 weeks)

For a lifter who trains by RPE but whose ratings are inconsistent, or who rates the deadlift poorly. Train with the normal RPE program; record the speed of every top set.

**Weeks 1–2: build the lifter's RPE–speed table.** Log the top-set speed and RPE. Example for this lifter's deadlift singles and doubles:

| Deadlift top set | RPE | Speed (first rep) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 @ 7 (~187.5 kg) | 7 | 0.38 m/s |
| 1 @ 8 (~192.5 kg) | 8 | 0.33 m/s |
| 2 @ 8 (~187.5 kg) | 8 | 0.34 m/s |
| 1 @ 9 (~200 kg) | 9 | 0.26 m/s |

**Weeks 3–4: use it.**

| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| Rated RPE 8 and speed matches RPE 8 (±0.03 m/s) | Accept; set back-offs as planned |
| Rated RPE 8 but speed matches the lifter's RPE 9 | Treat as RPE 9; no more top sets; reduce back-offs 3–5% |
| Rated RPE 8 but speed matches RPE 7 | Allow one more top set 2.5–5 kg heavier; discuss the rating |
| Speed is far outside the table for any rating | Check setup and technique before changing anything |

**Calculations:** deadlift top sets from e1RM 210 kg: 1 @ 7 = 210 × 0.892 ≈ 187.5 kg; 1 @ 8 = 210 × 0.922 ≈ 192.5 kg; 2 @ 8 = 210 × 0.892 ≈ 187.5 kg; 1 @ 9 = 210 × 0.955 ≈ 200 kg.

**How it is built and why:** RPE and bar speed are closely related in powerlifters (Helms et al., 2017), so a personal RPE–speed table turns velocity into a lie detector for ratings without changing the program. It is especially useful on the deadlift, where speed near the max is low and effort is hard to judge. After a block, most lifters rate more consistently, and the device becomes optional.

## Common mistakes

- **Predicting meet attempts from bar speed.** The speed of a max varies too much between days; use test days, meets and e1RMs from heavy sets.
- **Borrowing someone else's velocity table.** Profiles differ between lifters and studies; build your own.
- **Changing devices or mounting mid-block.** Numbers from different setups are not comparable.
- **Ignoring technique changes.** A higher squat or a shorter pause looks like a "fast" day.
- **Not lifting with maximal intent.** Submaximal effort makes every reading meaningless.
- **Reacting to tiny changes.** Learn the lifter's normal range before adjusting anything.
- **Using cheap, unvalidated devices for decisions.** If the device's error is larger than the change you care about, it can't guide training.

## Key takeaways

- Bar speed falls in a near-straight line as load rises, so a personal load–velocity profile shows how heavy a weight is for the lifter today.
- Velocity-predicted 1RMs are unreliable; use velocity for training loads, readiness and stopping sets, not for maxes or attempts.
- Velocity loss is the best-supported VBT tool: modest limits give similar strength with less fatigue.
- Linear position transducers are the most reliable devices; many cheaper sensors are not accurate enough for decisions.
- VBT builds strength about as well as percentage-based training, perhaps slightly better, but research in powerlifters is lacking.
- Start small: a profile, a readiness single and velocity-loss limits, and use velocity to calibrate RPE.
