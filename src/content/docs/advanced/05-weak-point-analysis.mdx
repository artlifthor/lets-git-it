## In one minute

A weak point is a **cause**, not a place: the bar slowing near the chest is the symptom, and the cause may be technique, a muscle that can't produce enough force at that position, or simply too little practice. Every lift has a **sticking region** where leverage is poor, so a slow spot alone proves little; what matters is a pattern that repeats across sessions and agrees with other evidence. Coaches diagnose by combining video, where reps slow and fail, how variations compare with the competition lift, and the training log. They then choose the **closest variation that targets the cause**, keep the competition lift as the main work, and run it for a block with a clear test of whether it worked. Direct evidence that weak-point training improves the competition lift is limited, so each choice should be treated as an experiment.

## Symptom, cause and type

Before choosing any exercise, sort what you see into one of four types. Each needs a different fix.

| Type | What it looks like | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Technical fault** | Position changes at every load, even light ones (bar drifts, hips rise early) | Technique practice: tempo, pauses, cues, lighter loads |
| **Force deficit in a region** | Technique holds until near-max, then the bar slows in the same region every time | Overload that region: pauses, dead-stops, a longer or shorter range |
| **Muscle deficit** | A muscle group is clearly underdeveloped; the lift breaks where that muscle does most of the work | Hypertrophy work for that muscle, usually with accessories |
| **Training gap** | The lift (or one part of it) has had less practice or volume than the others | More frequency or volume on the competition lift itself |

<Evidence level="practice" /> This classification is a coaching framework (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith; Sheiko). The categories overlap: a weak muscle often produces a technical fault under heavy load.

:::tip[In plain words]
Where the bar slows down is a clue, not the answer. Ask why it slows: bad technique, not enough strength in that spot, a small muscle, or not enough practice. Each one has a different fix.
:::

## What the research says about sticking regions

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A review describes the sticking point as the position where the lift becomes disproportionately harder, where failure usually happens, and where form is most likely to break down. It arises from the combination of muscle force capacity and changing mechanics through the range (Kompf & Arandjelović, 2016). A second review compared the observational research on sticking points in the bench press, squat and deadlift, covering both similarities and differences between the lifts (Kompf & Arandjelović, 2017). <Evidence level="practice" /> Because the lifts differ, don't assume a finding about one lift's sticking region applies to another.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Bench press.** In 12 recreational lifters, every 1RM bench press showed a sticking period: a slowing of the bar that began about 0.2 s after the press started and lasted about 0.9 s. Muscle activity changed across the phases before, during and after it (van den Tillaar & Ettema, 2010).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Squat.** In 15 trained men performing a 6RM squat, only 10 showed a sticking region on the last rep, and it was much shorter than in the bench press (van den Tillaar et al., 2014).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 25 recreationally trained lifters doing 3RM squats, the sticking region coincided with **more forward torso lean, a longer hip moment arm and a larger share of the work done by the hips**. The authors suggested this is a mechanically poor position for producing force (Larsen et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 10 trained powerlifters squatting 5RMs with matched depth and stance, high-bar and low-bar squats showed **no differences in bar or joint movement** around the sticking region, though muscle activity differed (van den Tillaar et al., 2020).

**What this means for coaches:**

1. **A sticking region is normal.** Almost every maximal bench press has one, and many squats do. Its existence does not prove a weakness.
2. **What matters is change and comparison:** a sticking region that is getting longer, moving, or appearing earlier than the lifter's usual pattern; or one that comes with a position change.
3. **Mechanics and muscles interact.** The squat sticking region coincides with a hip-dominant, forward-leaning position, so "weak out of the hole" may be a hip, quad or position problem, and the video has to tell you which.

:::tip[In plain words]
Almost everyone's bar slows down somewhere in a heavy lift, because of how the joints line up. A slow spot only matters if it keeps happening in the same place, is getting worse, or comes with your position falling apart.
:::

## The diagnostic process

Part 2 introduced four sources: ratios, sticking points, video and training history. Advanced diagnosis uses the same sources more systematically, and asks them to agree.

### Step 1: Collect

- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Video every top set for 2–3 weeks**, not a single session. Use the same angle each time: side-on at hip height for squat and deadlift; side-on or 45° from the head end for bench press.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Film a **moderate set (RPE 6–7)** and a **heavy set (RPE 8.5+)** of the same lift. Faults present at both loads are technical; faults that appear only when heavy are usually force or muscle deficits.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Bar-path apps** can help. In 10 athletes performing snatches, a smartphone app's measures of bar path agreed closely with a 3-D motion-capture system, with small biases for some measures (Balsalobre-Fernández et al., 2020). It was tested in the snatch, not the powerlifts, so use apps to compare a lifter with themselves rather than as precise measurements.

### Step 2: Locate

Mark **where** the bar slows or the position changes, using landmarks you can see on video:

| Lift | Landmarks to use |
|---|---|
| Squat | Bottom (below parallel), first 10 cm up, thighs parallel, halfway, lockout |
| Bench press | Chest, 2–5 cm off, mid-range (about halfway), last third, lockout |
| Deadlift | Floor, mid-shin, knee, just above the knee, lockout |

### Step 3: Read the failed and grinding reps

<Evidence level="practice" /> Hard reps reveal more than easy ones. Look across several heavy sets and rep-outs:

| Pattern | Likely meaning |
|---|---|
| Reps slow in the **same region** on every hard set | Force deficit in that region |
| The **position changes first** (hips rise, bar drifts), then the bar slows | Technical or muscle deficit causing the position change |
| Different spot each time | Fatigue, inconsistent technique, or noise: keep collecting |
| Rep speed drops quickly across a set, failure comes early in the set | Work capacity or fatigue, not a positional weakness |
| Fails only on the first rep after the walkout or the pause | Setup, bracing or pause handling: technique |

### Step 4: Compare with variations and other lifts (coaching heuristics)

<Evidence level="practice" /> Comparing a lifter's variations with their competition lift can point to a weak region. The ratios below are **common coaching rules of thumb, not research norms**, and they vary with body proportions and technique. The most useful comparison is with the lifter's **own** past ratios.

| Comparison | Common rule-of-thumb range | If clearly lower than usual, ask whether… |
|---|---|---|
| Front squat ÷ back squat | ~0.80–0.85 | Quads or upright position limit the squat |
| Pause squat (2–3 s) ÷ squat | ~0.85–0.90 | The lifter depends on the bounce out of the hole |
| Close-grip bench ÷ competition bench | ~0.90–0.95 | Triceps or the top half limit the bench |
| Long-pause bench ÷ competition bench | ~0.90–0.95 | Strength off the chest without a stretch reflex is low |
| Deficit deadlift ÷ deadlift | ~0.85–0.95 | Strength from the lowest position is low |
| Block pull (just below the knee) ÷ deadlift | Usually above 1.0 | A ratio near or below 1.0 points to a lockout limit |

For between-lift ratios (bench ÷ squat, deadlift ÷ squat) based on normative competition data, see [Part 2 · Chapter 1](/lets-git-it/building-programs/01-assessing-the-lifter/). <Evidence level="moderate" /> Those benchmarks come from over 800,000 raw, drug-tested competition entries (van den Hoek et al., 2024), but the ratios derived from them are still coaching heuristics.

### Step 5: Check the log for training gaps

<Evidence level="practice" /> If the log shows the "weak" lift or region has simply had less practice, fix the gap before looking for a special variation. A lifter who has trained the deadlift once a week for six months while squatting three times may not have a weak deadlift; they have an undertrained one.

### Step 6: Confirm

<Evidence level="practice" /> Call it a weak point only when **at least two sources agree** (for example, video and a variation ratio). Write the diagnosis as one sentence: *"Bar slows 5–10 cm off the chest on every set above RPE 8; long-pause bench is 88% of competition bench; triceps and lockout are strong."*

:::tip[In plain words]
Film your heavy sets for a few weeks. Note where the bar slows, whether your position changes first, and how your variations compare with your competition lift. When two or three of those clues point to the same spot, you've found a real weak point. Write it down in one sentence.
:::

## Choosing the variation: specificity vs. targeting

Every variation trades **specificity** (how much it resembles the competition lift) against **targeting** (how much it overloads the weak region). The more you change, the more you target, and the less directly it transfers.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Range of motion transfer is limited.** A meta-analysis of 16 studies found that full-range training produced larger strength gains than partial-range training (Pallarés et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 50 trained men over 10 weeks, full-range bench press training improved full, two-thirds and one-third range bench press the most. Partial training produced smaller gains the shorter its range (Martínez-Cava et al., 2022). Strength built in a short range does not reliably carry over to the full lift.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 17 male students, deep-squat training improved both deep and shallow squats by about 20%, but shallow-squat training improved the deep squat by only about 9% (Bloomquist et al., 2013).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Stance changes the lift.** In national-level powerlifters, sumo and conventional deadlifters differed in trunk and thigh angles and in knee and ankle moments, and vertical bar distance and mechanical work were about 25–40% greater in the conventional lifters (Escamilla et al., 2000). A sumo lifter's variations should usually be sumo variations.

### The decision rules

<Evidence level="practice" /> These rules follow from the transfer evidence above and from common coaching practice (Siff; Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith):

1. **Change one thing.** A good weak-point variation is the competition lift with a single change (a pause, a tempo, a start position, a grip), aimed at the diagnosed region.
2. **Match the fix to the cause.**
   - Technical fault → tempo or pause at the point where position is lost, at moderate loads.
   - Force deficit in a region → pause or dead-stop start in that region, at heavier loads.
   - Muscle deficit → accessory hypertrophy work for that muscle, plus a variation that emphasises it.
   - Training gap → more practice of the competition lift.
3. **Prefer full range.** Use partial-range lifts (pins, boards, blocks) as a supplement for one slot, never as the main variation.
4. **Keep the competition lift the main work.** <Evidence level="practice" /> As a rule of thumb, at least half of a lift's weekly hard sets should stay on the competition lift during a weak-point block.
5. **Write a test before you start.** Decide now what result will count as success at the end of the block.

| Diagnosed cause | First choice (more specific) | Second choice (more targeted) |
|---|---|---|
| Squat: forward lean out of the hole | Tempo squat (3-1-0) | Front squat or safety-bar squat |
| Squat: slow out of the hole, position holds | Pause squat (2–3 s) | Pin squat from the bottom |
| Bench: slow 2–10 cm off the chest | Long-pause bench | Spoto press (pause just above the chest) |
| Bench: slow at mid-range | Pause at the sticking height | Dead-stop pin press at the sticking height |
| Bench: slow at lockout | Close-grip bench | Pin or board press (top third) |
| Deadlift: slow off the floor | Paused deadlift (just off the floor) | Deficit deadlift (2–5 cm) |
| Deadlift: hips rise first | Tempo or paused deadlift at moderate load | Front squat, leg press (quads) |
| Deadlift: slow above the knee | Paused deadlift at the knee | Block pull just below the knee |

:::tip[In plain words]
Pick a version of the lift that changes just one thing and makes you work hardest in your weak spot. Keep doing the normal competition lift as your main exercise. The closer the variation is to the real lift, the better it carries over.
:::

## Programming the weak-point block

### Dose and placement

<Evidence level="practice" /> Typical structure for one lift during a 4–6 week block:

| Element | Typical choice |
|---|---|
| Competition lift | 50–70% of the lift's weekly hard sets |
| Weak-point variation | 25–40% of the lift's weekly hard sets, replacing (not adding to) some competition sets |
| Accessory for the muscle involved | 2–3 sets, 2–3 times a week, if a muscle deficit is part of the diagnosis |
| Placement | Variation after the competition lift on the same day, or as the main lift on a separate day |
| Effort | Variations at RPE 6–8; accessories at RPE 7–9 |

### Evaluation at the end of the block

<Evidence level="practice" /> Judge three things, and use the noise rules from [Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/advanced/04-individual-response/):

| Question | How to check |
|---|---|
| Did the **competition lift** improve? | Top-set e1RM trend across the block |
| Did the **weak region** change? | Same-angle video: shorter slow phase, position held |
| Did the **variation** improve? | Its own e1RM or load at the target RPE |

| Result | Interpretation | Next block |
|---|---|---|
| Variation ↑, competition lift ↑, video better | Working | Keep it, or progress to a slightly more specific version |
| Variation ↑, competition lift flat | Poor transfer, or diagnosis wrong | Re-check the diagnosis; choose a closer variation |
| Variation flat, competition lift flat | Not enough stimulus, or fatigue | Check volume and recovery before changing exercises |
| Competition lift ↑, video unchanged | Lifter got stronger overall | Fine; keep training, re-assess the weak point later |

:::tip[In plain words]
Give a weak-point exercise four to six weeks, then check three things: did your competition lift go up, does the slow spot look better on video, and did the variation itself get stronger? If only the variation improved, it isn't carrying over, so try a closer version.
:::

## Rotation strategies

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review found that planned variation can help, while excessive, random variation may hold gains back (Kassiano et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 49 physically active people, varying exercises while keeping the load scheme constant gave larger squat 1RM gains than the other approaches tested over 12 weeks (Fonseca et al., 2014).

<Evidence level="practice" /> Common rotation patterns:

| Strategy | How it works | Suits | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **One variation per block** | Same variation for 4–6 weeks, then reassess | Most lifters; clearest test of whether it worked | Staleness in long blocks |
| **Progressive specificity** | Each block's variation is one step closer to the competition lift (e.g. pin squat → pause squat → competition squat) | Approaching a meet | Moving on before the weak point has improved |
| **A/B alternation** | Two variations for the same weak point alternate by block | Lifters who stall on any single exercise | Harder to tell which one works |
| **Short rotation (1–3 weeks)** | Max-effort-style rotation of several variations | Off-season, experienced lifters, variety-seekers | Little time for measurable progress on each |

**Retirement rule:** <Evidence level="practice" /> drop a variation after **two blocks without improvement in the competition lift**, even if the variation itself got stronger. Keep a "proven" list of variations that have worked for this lifter before; individual response applies to exercise choice too ([Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/advanced/04-individual-response/)).

:::tip[In plain words]
Change weak-point exercises on a plan, not on a whim. Usually one per block is best, so you can see whether it helped. If an exercise hasn't helped your competition lift after two blocks, drop it. Keep a list of the ones that worked for you.
:::

## Sample programs

Intermediate lifter, male, 83 kg, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg, four days a week. Each program shows a 4-week weak-point block for one lift. The other two lifts follow a normal plan (not shown in full). Competition-lift loads use the RPE chart from [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/); variations are given as RPE only.

### Program 1: Squat — forward lean out of the hole

**Diagnosis (three sources agree):**

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Video | At RPE 8.5+, hips rise before the chest in the first 10–15 cm; at RPE 6 the position holds |
| Failed reps | Last reps of heavy triples slow at the same point, after the chest has dropped |
| Variation ratio | Front squat 135 kg ÷ back squat 180 kg = 0.75 (below his usual ~0.80) |
| Log | Squat trained 2× per week, no front squats or quad accessories for 6 months |

**One-sentence diagnosis:** a position break under heavy load, likely linked to quad strength and upright posture, not a technique fault at all loads.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~145 kg) | +2.5 kg | +2.5 kg | 3 × 4, RPE 6 |
| 1 | Leg press | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 | RPE 8 | RPE 8.5 | 2 × 10, RPE 7 |
| 3 | Tempo squat (3-1-0) | 3 × 4 | RPE 6.5 | RPE 7 | RPE 7.5 | 2 × 4, RPE 6 |
| 3 | Front squat | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 | RPE 7.5 | RPE 8 | 2 × 5, RPE 6 |
| 4 | Squat | 1 × 2 @ RPE 8, then 3 × 3 | Top ~160 kg; back-offs ~150 kg | Same RPE | Same RPE | 2 × 3 at ~145 kg |

Weekly squat-pattern hard sets (weeks 1–3): competition squat 8 (4 + 4), tempo squat 3, front squat 3 = **14**, with 57% on the competition lift. Leg press is counted as an accessory.

**Test at week 4–5:** a heavy triple filmed from the same angle. Success = hips and chest rise together on the last rep at a load equal to or above the previous best, and front squat ratio back toward 0.80.

**Why it's built this way.** The tempo squat teaches the position at moderate loads (technical side); the front squat and leg press build the quads and upright strength (muscle side). Both are full range. The competition squat keeps most of the volume, so specificity isn't lost.

### Program 2: Bench press — mid-range stall

**Diagnosis:**

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Video | Bar leaves the chest fast, then slows about halfway up on every set above RPE 8; elbows and bar path stay consistent |
| Failed reps | Two missed singles this year, both stopped at mid-range |
| Variation ratios | Long-pause bench 112.5 kg (0.94), close-grip 110 kg (0.92): both in his usual range |
| Log | Bench trained 3× per week; volume steady |

**One-sentence diagnosis:** a force deficit at mid-range with good technique; off-the-chest and lockout strength are normal.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~97.5 kg) | +2.5 kg | +2.5 kg | 3 × 4, RPE 6 |
| 2 | Bench with 2 s pause at mid-range (on the way up) | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 | RPE 7.5 | RPE 8 | 2 × 3, RPE 6 |
| 2 | Dumbbell bench press | 3 × 8 | RPE 8 | RPE 8 | RPE 8.5 | 2 × 8, RPE 7 |
| 3 | Bench press | 1 × 1 @ RPE 8, then 3 × 3 | Top ~110 kg; back-offs ~100 kg | Same RPE | Same RPE | 2 × 3 at ~97.5 kg |
| 4 | Dead-stop pin press at sticking height | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 | RPE 7.5 | RPE 8 | 2 × 3, RPE 6 |
| 4 | Bench press | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) | Same RPE | Same RPE | 2 × 5, RPE 6 |

Weekly bench-pattern hard sets (weeks 1–3): competition bench 11 (4 + 4 + 3), paused mid-range 3, pin press 3 = **17**, with 65% on the competition lift.

**Test:** at the end of week 4, an RPE 8–8.5 single and a mid-range-paused triple, compared with week 1. Success = competition single e1RM up beyond noise (about 5 kg for this lifter) and a shorter slow phase on video.

**Why it's built this way.** The pause at mid-range is a full-range lift with extra time and effort at the weak height. The pin press is the one partial-range slot, and it starts at the sticking height, so it overloads exactly that region. Partial work is limited to 3 sets because short-range strength carries over poorly (Martínez-Cava et al., 2022). If the competition bench has not moved after this block and the next, retire the pin press.

### Program 3: Deadlift (conventional) — slow off the floor

**Diagnosis:**

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Video | Bar breaks the floor slowly on all heavy sets; once past mid-shin it accelerates through lockout; back angle holds |
| Failed reps | Last rep of heavy triples sometimes doesn't leave the floor |
| Variation ratios | 5 cm deficit deadlift 180 kg (0.86, low for him); block pull (just below the knee) 230 kg (1.10) |
| Log | Deadlift trained once a week; no deficit or paused pulls for a year |

**One-sentence diagnosis:** a force deficit from the floor with sound position, plus a frequency gap.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Deadlift | 1 × 2 @ RPE 8, then 2 × 3 | Top ~187.5 kg; back-offs ~177.5 kg | Same RPE | Same RPE | 2 × 3 at ~170 kg |
| 2 | Paused deadlift (2 s just off the floor) | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 | RPE 7.5 | RPE 8 | 2 × 3, RPE 6 |
| 4 | Deficit deadlift (2.5 cm) | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 | RPE 7.5 | RPE 8 | 2 × 4, RPE 6 |
| 4 | Leg press | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 | RPE 8 | RPE 8.5 | 2 × 10, RPE 7 |

Weekly deadlift-pattern hard sets (weeks 1–3): competition deadlift 3, paused 3, deficit 3 = **9**, now spread over two days. Here the competition lift is only a third of the sets. That is a deliberate exception for a lift trained once a week before; the second deadlift day adds frequency, and the next block shifts back toward the competition lift.

**Test:** in week 4–5, a heavy double and a deficit triple. Success = the bar breaks the floor faster on video at the same load, deficit ratio back toward 0.90, and competition e1RM up.

**Why it's built this way.** Both variations are full-range (the deficit slightly longer than competition range) and start in the weak position. A small 2.5 cm deficit keeps the start position close to his competition setup. Leg press supports quad strength for the floor break. Fixing the frequency gap (one day to two) may help as much as the variations, so if progress comes, credit both.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a normal sticking region as a weakness.** Nearly every heavy bench press slows somewhere; look for change, repetition and agreement between sources.
- **Diagnosing from one video.** Use 2–3 weeks of heavy sets from the same angle.
- **Fixing a training gap with fancy variations.** If the lift has been undertrained, train it more first.
- **Using partial-range lifts as the main variation.** Pins, boards and blocks belong in one supplementary slot.
- **Adding weak-point work on top of full volume.** Replace some competition sets; don't just pile on.
- **Judging a variation by its own progress.** The variation getting stronger means nothing unless the competition lift follows.
- **Rotating too fast.** A new variation every week can't be evaluated.
- **Applying conventional deadlift fixes to a sumo puller.** The lifts differ mechanically; use sumo variations.

## Key takeaways

- A weak point is a cause (technique, force in a region, muscle, or practice), not just a slow spot.
- Sticking regions are normal; diagnose only from repeated patterns that at least two sources confirm.
- Lift and variation ratios are coaching heuristics; compare lifters mainly with their own history.
- Choose the closest full-range variation that targets the cause; use partial ranges sparingly.
- Keep the competition lift as the main work and set a test before the block starts.
- Rotate on a plan, usually one variation per block, and retire what doesn't move the competition lift.
