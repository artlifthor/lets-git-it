## In one minute

**Concurrent training** here means doing endurance training ("cardio") and strength training in the same program. Early research found that adding a lot of endurance work could blunt strength gains, which became known as the **interference effect**. Newer meta-analyses show the effect is smaller and more specific than gym folklore suggests: maximal strength and muscle size are usually little affected, while **explosive strength** is more sensitive, and **trained men's lower-body strength** is the most at risk, mainly when both are done in the same session. A powerlifter who keeps cardio moderate, low-impact, and separated from heavy lower-body sessions can gain the health, work-capacity and body-weight benefits with little or no cost to the total.

## Two meanings of "concurrent"

This chapter is about **cardio plus lifting**. The other meaning, training several strength qualities in the same week (Soviet concurrent training, Westside), is covered in [Part 2 · Chapter 7: Conjugate and concurrent methods](/lets-git-it/building-programs/07-conjugate-concurrent/).

| Term | In this chapter |
|---|---|
| **Endurance or aerobic training** | Continuous work such as cycling, brisk or incline walking, running, rowing or swimming, usually 20–60 min |
| **High-intensity interval training (HIIT)** | Short hard efforts (about 15 s to 4 min) separated by easy recovery |
| **Interference effect** | Smaller gains in strength, power or muscle when endurance training is added to strength training, compared with the same strength training alone |
| **Conditioning** | General fitness that lets a lifter recover between sets and sessions and handle long training days |

## The interference effect: where the idea came from

<Evidence level="emerging" /> The classic study put three groups through 10 weeks of training: strength only (5 days per week), endurance only (6 days per week), or both. The combined group gained leg strength at the same rate as the strength group for about 7 weeks, then levelled off and declined in weeks 9–10, while its aerobic fitness rose just as much as the endurance group's (Hickson, 1980). The endurance load in that study was large: 40 minutes a day, six days a week, on top of five strength sessions.

**Why might interference happen?** <Evidence level="practice" /> Several explanations have been proposed (Fyfe et al., 2014):

- **Fatigue.** Tired legs from cycling or running lower the quality of the next squat or deadlift session, so the strength stimulus shrinks.
- **Competing signals.** Endurance exercise switches on cellular signals that can inhibit the pathways that build muscle protein. A review of the mechanisms noted that human studies had **not** found this molecular interference after a single combined session, so it may not explain much in practice (Fyfe et al., 2014).
- **Recovery budget.** Both kinds of training draw on the same sleep, food and time. Too much of either leaves less for the other.

:::tip[In plain words]
An old study found that doing a lot of cardio on top of heavy lifting stopped strength gains after a couple of months. The likely reasons are simple: tired legs lift worse, and there is only so much recovery to go around.
:::

## What the meta-analyses say

### Maximal strength

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Little or no interference on average.** An updated meta-analysis of 43 studies in healthy adults found that adding aerobic training to strength training did **not** compromise maximal strength or muscle size (Schumann et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Trained lifters are more vulnerable.** A meta-analysis of 27 studies on leg press and squat 1RM found that concurrent training reduced lower-body strength gains in **trained** individuals, but not in untrained or moderately trained people. In the trained group, the effect appeared only when both kinds of training were done **in the same session**; with separate sessions the difference was small and not significant (Petré et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Sex may matter.** A meta-analysis of 59 studies (1,346 participants) found small interference for **lower-body** strength in men but not in women, and no sex difference for upper-body strength (Huiberts et al., 2024). The authors noted that more studies on women and on highly strength-trained athletes are needed.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> An older meta-analysis of 21 studies found effect sizes for strength gains of 1.76 with strength training alone, 1.44 with concurrent training and 0.78 with endurance training alone; both strength-only and concurrent training clearly beat endurance-only training (Wilson et al., 2012).

These findings disagree somewhat, so the honest summary is: **interference on maximal strength is small or absent for most people, but trained men's lower-body strength can suffer, especially when cardio and lifting share a session.** Most competitive powerlifters fall into that "trained" group.

:::tip[In plain words]
For most people, some cardio does not stop them getting stronger. Experienced lifters, especially men, may lose a little leg strength gain, mostly when they do cardio and lifting in the same session.
:::

### Muscle size

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Whole-muscle size was not reduced by adding aerobic training in the 43-study meta-analysis (Schumann et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> A companion meta-analysis of 15 studies that measured **individual muscle fibres** found a small negative effect of concurrent training on fibre growth, borderline in statistical terms. Running, but not cycling, was linked to less growth of type I (slow-twitch) fibres (Lundberg et al., 2022).

### Explosive strength and power

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Explosive strength is the most consistently affected quality. Schumann et al. (2022) found a small reduction in explosive strength gains, larger when both kinds of training were done in the same session than when they were at least 3 hours apart. Wilson et al. (2012) also found smaller power gains with concurrent training than with strength training alone.

For powerlifters, maximal strength matters more than jump height. Still, bar speed out of the hole and off the floor is part of a heavy lift, so the explosive-strength finding is a reason to keep hard conditioning away from heavy lower-body days.

:::tip[In plain words]
Cardio doesn't usually shrink your muscles. The thing it dulls most is explosiveness, especially if you do both on the same day. Keep hard cardio away from your heavy leg days.
:::

## Modality: cycling, running, or something else?

The research here is **mixed**, so treat it as a tie-breaker, not a rule.

| Study | Finding on modality |
|---|---|
| Wilson et al. (2012), 21 studies | Running, but not cycling, was linked to smaller gains in strength and muscle size |
| Lundberg et al. (2022), 15 studies | Running, but not cycling, was linked to less type I fibre growth |
| Sabag et al. (2018), HIIT studies | Lower-body strength gains were slightly smaller with HIIT. Only cycling HIIT showed a non-significant trend towards smaller gains (p = 0.074). |
| Schumann et al. (2022), 43 studies | No difference between cycling and running |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Why coaches still lean towards cycling, incline walking, rowing or swimming for powerlifters:

- **Low impact.** Running has a large eccentric, pounding component that can leave the legs sore. Many powerlifters are also heavy, which adds joint stress per stride.
- **Easy to dose.** A bike or incline treadmill makes intensity easy to control and keep low.
- **Low skill and low injury risk.** A heavy lifter who rarely runs has a higher chance of shin or calf problems if they start running often.

A lifter who enjoys running and has built up to it gradually can keep running. The evidence does not say running is harmful, only that, if anything, it may cost a little more lower-body adaptation.

:::tip[In plain words]
Studies disagree about whether cycling or running is better for lifters. Cycling, brisk walking on an incline, rowing or swimming are easy on the joints and easy to control, so they're the usual choice. If you like running and your joints handle it, that's fine too.
:::

## Timing and sequencing

This is where the evidence is most useful in practice.

- **Same session: lift first.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> Two meta-analyses found better lower-body dynamic strength when strength training came **before** endurance training in the same session. One reported a pooled 1RM advantage of about 4 kg (Murlasits et al., 2018); the other, about 7% more strength change (Eddens et al., 2018). The order made no difference to aerobic fitness in either analysis, or to muscle size in the second.
- **Separate them if you can.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 58 amateur rugby players over 7 weeks, doing aerobic work immediately after strength training produced smaller bench press and half-squat gains than separating the two by 6 or 24 hours. The authors advised at least 6 hours between the two (Robineau et al., 2016). <Evidence level="moderate" /> This fits the meta-analyses above, where interference in trained people appeared mainly in same-session designs (Petré et al., 2021; Schumann et al., 2022).

<Evidence level="practice" /> **Priority order for scheduling cardio** (best first):

1. On a **non-lifting day**, or on an upper-body-only day.
2. **6 hours or more** after a lifting session (for example, lift in the morning, cycle in the evening).
3. **Straight after** lifting, in the same session.
4. **Before** lifting. Avoid this, especially before heavy squats or deadlifts.

Also avoid **hard leg conditioning the day before** a heavy squat or deadlift session. Easy cardio the day before is usually fine.

:::tip[In plain words]
Lift first, cardio second. Better still, do cardio on a different day or several hours apart. Never do hard cardio right before heavy squats or deadlifts.
:::

## Dose: frequency, duration, intensity

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **More endurance work, more interference.** Wilson et al. (2012) found that longer and more frequent endurance sessions were linked to smaller gains in muscle size, strength and power. The relationship was correlational, across different studies.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> However, the larger, later meta-analysis found no difference between more than 5 and fewer than 5 concurrent sessions per week (Schumann et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> For a powerlifter whose goal is strength, a sensible ceiling is about **2–4 sessions and 60–150 minutes of planned cardio per week**, most of it easy. Raise it in the off-season or during fat loss, and cut it back during the peak.

**Intensity zones for lifters.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Heart-rate zones vary between people; the talk test is simple and needs no equipment.

| Zone | How it feels | Use for powerlifters |
|---|---|---|
| **Easy** | Can speak in full sentences; breathing a little faster | Most cardio: health, recovery, weight management |
| **Moderate** | Can speak in short phrases | Small amounts, mainly in the off-season |
| **Hard (intervals)** | A few words at most | 1 session per week at most, away from heavy leg days; drop in the peak |

:::tip[In plain words]
A little cardio goes a long way. Two to four sessions a week, mostly at a pace where you can still talk, is plenty for a powerlifter. Do more when you're far from a meet or trying to lose fat, and less when you're peaking.
:::

## Why a powerlifter should do some conditioning

### Health

- <Evidence level="practice" /> The World Health Organization's 2020 guidelines advise all adults to do **150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity or 75–150 minutes of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity per week**, or an equivalent mix, plus muscle-strengthening activity (Bull et al., 2020). Lifting alone covers the second part, not the first.
- <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 33 cohort studies (over 100,000 participants) found that higher cardiorespiratory fitness was associated with lower risk of death from all causes and of cardiovascular events. Each 1-MET higher fitness level was linked to about 13% lower all-cause mortality risk (Kodama et al., 2009). These are observational data in the general population, not powerlifters, but there is no reason to think lifters are exempt.

Brisk walking counts as moderate activity. A lifter who walks 30 minutes on 5 days already meets the lower end of the guideline.

### Work capacity and recovery between sets

<Evidence level="practice" /> A review concluded that aerobic fitness appears to help recovery between repeated bouts of high-intensity exercise, through faster phosphocreatine resynthesis and a larger aerobic response (Tomlin & Wenger, 2001). The studies it covered were mostly sprint-type and intermittent exercise, not heavy lifting with long rests. Coaches commonly report that fitter lifters handle high-volume blocks and long sessions better, but this has not been tested directly in powerlifters.

### Weight management

<Evidence level="practice" /> Cardio burns energy, which makes a calorie deficit easier to reach without cutting food as hard. Easy, low-impact cardio is the least likely to interfere with lifting. Energy balance, protein and safe rates of loss are covered in [Part 1 · Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/foundations/11-nutrition/). Making weight for a meet is covered in Part 4, Chapter 4.

### Long meet days

<Evidence level="practice" /> A full powerlifting meet can last many hours: weigh-in, warm-ups for three lifts, nine attempts, and long waits. Basic aerobic fitness makes the day less draining and may help the deadlift session late in the day. No study has tested this; it is a reasonable expectation from coaching experience.

**Philippine context.** <Evidence level="practice" /> In hot, humid weather, easy cardio becomes much harder at the same pace. Train in the cooler early morning or evening, or indoors with a fan or air-conditioning, and keep the talk test as the guide rather than a fixed pace. Hydration and heat safety are covered in [Part 1 · Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/foundations/11-nutrition/).

:::tip[In plain words]
Cardio is good for your heart and long-term health, may help you recover between hard sets, makes fat loss easier, and helps on a long meet day. You don't need much, and easy cardio gives most of the benefit.
:::

## Cardio across the training year

<Evidence level="practice" /> The amount of conditioning should follow the training phase, like everything else in the plan.

| Phase | Planned cardio per week | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Off-season / general preparation | 2–4 sessions, 90–150 min, mostly easy; 1 interval session allowed | Build fitness here; it is the cheapest time to do so |
| Fat-loss phase | 3–5 sessions, 120–200 min, almost all easy; daily walking | Prefer easy, low-impact cardio over more intervals |
| Specific strength block | 2–3 sessions, 60–90 min, easy | Keep the habit; drop intervals if lower-body sessions suffer |
| Peaking (last 3–4 weeks) | 1–2 sessions, 20–30 min, easy only | Maintenance and stress relief, nothing that leaves the legs tired |
| Meet week | Walking only | |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Building fitness in the off-season and keeping a small dose of easy cardio through the peak keeps the habit alive and avoids starting from zero after every meet.

:::tip[In plain words]
Do most of your cardio when you're far from a meet, keep it easy while you're losing fat, and cut it to a couple of short, easy sessions when you're peaking. In meet week, just walk.
:::

## Sample programs

Intermediate lifter: squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg, bodyweight about 83 kg. RPE-to-kg conversions use the chart in [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/). "Easy" means the talk test: full sentences possible.

### Program 1: Off-season week with conditioning (4 lifting days, 3 cardio sessions)

Goal: build work capacity and general fitness while gaining muscle and strength.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~137.5 kg) |
| Mon | Bench press | 4 × 8 | RPE 7 (~85 kg) |
| Mon | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| Tue | Bench press | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| Tue | Barbell row | 4 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| Tue (evening) | Cycling, easy | 40 min | Talk test: full sentences |
| Wed | Cardio only: incline walk or rowing, easy | 40 min | Full sentences |
| Thu | Deadlift | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~170 kg) |
| Thu | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 |
| Fri | Bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7.5 |
| Fri | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 8 | RPE 8 |
| Fri | Bike intervals (after lifting) | 6 × 1 min hard / 2 min easy, plus 5 min warm-up and cool-down | Hard: a few words at most |
| Sat or Sun | Walk | 30–45 min | Easy |

**Why it's built this way:** about 135–155 minutes including the weekend walk, mostly easy. That reaches the WHO minimum (150 min of moderate activity, with vigorous minutes counting double) if the easy sessions are brisk enough to count as moderate. The only hard session sits on the upper-body day, after lifting, with two full days before the next squat session. Wednesday's cardio is on a rest day and is low-impact. Cycling and incline walking keep leg soreness low.

### Program 2: Fat-loss week (5 lifting days, daily walking, 3 easy sessions)

Goal: lose about 0.5% of bodyweight per week to sit comfortably inside the weight class, while keeping strength. Calories and protein follow [Part 1 · Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/foundations/11-nutrition/).

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 1 × 3 @ RPE 8, then 3 × 3 | ~155 kg, then ~145 kg |
| Mon | Bench press | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| Mon (evening) | Cycling, easy | 30 min | Full sentences |
| Tue | Bench press | 4 × 4 | RPE 7.5 |
| Tue | Upper-back and arm accessories | 3 × 10–12 | RPE 8 |
| Tue | Incline walk, easy | 45 min | Full sentences |
| Wed | Deadlift | 3 × 3 | RPE 7.5 (~177.5 kg) |
| Thu | Bench press | 1 × 3 @ RPE 8, then 3 × 3 | ~102.5 kg, then ~97.5 kg |
| Thu | Upper-back and arm accessories | 3 × 10–12 | RPE 8 |
| Fri | Squat | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 (~145 kg) |
| Fri | Deadlift variation (deficit or pause) | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| Sat | Swim, cycle or row, easy | 45 min | Full sentences |
| Daily | Walking | Gradually raise daily steps from the lifter's current baseline | Easy |

**Why it's built this way:** in a deficit, recovery is tighter, so all planned cardio is **easy and low-impact**, and there are no intervals. The heavy deadlift sits alone on Wednesday, two days after Monday's squat and two days before Friday's. Lifting volume drops slightly while intensity stays high, so the lifter keeps practising heavy weights. Most of the extra energy use comes from walking, which adds very little fatigue. If top-set RPE climbs by 1 or more at the same load for two sessions, reduce cardio first, then the deficit.

### Program 3: Specific preparation week, 5 weeks before a meet (minimal cardio)

Goal: keep the cardio habit and some fitness without taking anything from heavy lifting.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 1 × 2 @ RPE 8, then 3 × 3 | ~160 kg, then ~150 kg |
| Mon | Bench press (competition pause) | 4 × 3 | RPE 7.5 |
| Tue | Bench press (competition pause) | 1 × 2 @ RPE 8, then 3 × 3 | ~107.5 kg, then ~100 kg |
| Tue (evening) | Cycling, easy | 25 min | Full sentences |
| Wed | Rest or 30 min walk | | Easy |
| Thu | Deadlift | 1 × 2 @ RPE 8, then 2 × 3 | ~187.5 kg, then ~175 kg |
| Thu | Squat | 3 × 2 | RPE 7 (~155 kg) |
| Sat | Bench press | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 (~100 kg) |
| Sat (after lifting) | Cycling, easy | 20 min | Full sentences |

**Why it's built this way:** cardio drops to about 45 minutes of easy cycling, both sessions on days without heavy squats or deadlifts the next day. There are no intervals: explosive strength is the quality most sensitive to interference, and the heavy singles and doubles need fresh legs. In the last 1–2 weeks before the meet, walking alone is enough.

## Common mistakes

- **Avoiding all cardio "to protect gains".** For most lifters, moderate cardio costs little or nothing, and it brings real health benefits.
- **Doing hard intervals before or the day before heavy squats and deadlifts.** Same-session and back-to-back hard work is where interference shows up most.
- **Choosing high-impact work without building up to it.** A heavy lifter who suddenly starts running several times a week risks sore legs and overuse problems. Start with cycling or incline walking.
- **Using cardio as the main fat-loss tool.** Most of a deficit should come from food; cardio helps, but large amounts in a deficit add fatigue.
- **Keeping the off-season dose in the peak.** Cut cardio to short, easy sessions in the last 3–4 weeks.
- **Ignoring the heat.** In hot weather, use the talk test, not pace, and hydrate.

## Key takeaways

- The interference effect is real but usually small; maximal strength and muscle size are rarely much affected.
- Explosive strength, and lower-body strength in trained men, are the most sensitive, mainly when cardio and lifting share a session.
- Lift first, or better, separate cardio by 6+ hours or put it on non-lifting or upper-body days.
- Cycling, incline walking, rowing and swimming are low-impact, easy-to-dose choices; the running vs cycling evidence is mixed.
- About 2–4 mostly easy sessions per week fits a strength program and moves the lifter toward health guidelines.
- Match the cardio dose to the phase: most in the off-season or fat-loss phase, least in the peak.
