## In one minute

The principles in this manual apply to every lifter, but masters, female and youth lifters need some specific adjustments. **Masters lifters** (40+) still gain strength well with heavy loads, but usually need lower volume, more space between hard sessions, longer warm-ups and careful exercise choice. **Female lifters** gain muscle and lower-body strength at a similar rate to men, and on average the menstrual cycle has a trivial or unclear effect on strength, so programs should respond to each woman's own symptoms rather than the calendar. **Sub-juniors** (14–18) can train safely and effectively with heavy loads when they are well supervised and technique comes first. **Weight class** decisions should rest on muscle and long-term strength, not on how much a lifter can cut.

## IPF age divisions

<Evidence level="practice" /> The IPF Technical Rules set the age divisions. Age is counted by **calendar year**: a lifter moves into a division on 1 January of the year they reach the starting age (sub-juniors start on their 14th birthday). Always check the current rulebook before entering lifters, because entry rules change.

## Masters lifters

### How strength changes with age

- **Powerlifting performance falls steadily, not suddenly.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In a cross-sectional analysis of top US age-group records, powerlifting performance declined **linearly** with age in men and women. Squat and bench press declined at a similar rate, and the decline was smaller than in Olympic weightlifting (Anton et al., 2004). These are records from different people at different ages, not the same lifters followed over time.
- **Powerlifters peak late.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In world-championship powerlifters (2003–2017), the average age of peak performance was **35 years (SD 7)**, about 9 years later than in weightlifters. Lifters improved by about 12% over the five years before their peak (Solberg et al., 2019).
- **Older adults still respond well to training.** <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 47 studies (1,079 older adults) found that resistance training improved strength on every exercise tested, by roughly 24–33%. **Higher-intensity training produced greater improvement** (Peterson et al., 2010). <Evidence level="strong" /> A second meta-analysis in healthy older adults found large strength gains. The biggest effects came with longer programs (50–53 weeks) and loads of **70–79% 1RM** (Borde et al., 2015). Both reviews mostly studied untrained older adults, not masters powerlifters.
- **Age does not stop the response.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In pooled data from 287 untrained adults aged 19–78, age did not change how much strength or muscle people gained (Ahtiainen et al., 2016).

:::tip[In plain words]
You lose a little strength each decade, slowly and steadily. But older lifters still get stronger with training, and heavy-ish training works best for them too. Many powerlifters do their best lifting in their mid-thirties, and masters lifters can keep setting records for their own age group for many years.
:::

### Recovery

- <Evidence level="practice" /> Older athletes commonly report that they recover more slowly. A review found that studies on aging and recovery are hard to interpret: participants' activity levels are rarely matched, and the exercise used to cause damage is often unlike real training (Fell & Williams, 2008). A later narrative review concluded that the limited research suggests **there may be an age effect** on recovery after a session, and that inactivity explains much of the loss of performance with age (Borges et al., 2016).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> Stopping training costs older lifters more. A meta-analysis of training cessation found the loss of strength was **larger in people over 65** (Bosquet et al., 2013). In a 32-week maintenance trial (70 adults), reduced training kept muscle size in young adults (20–35) but not in older adults (60–75), although strength was largely kept in both groups (Bickel et al., 2011).

**What this means for coaches:** <Evidence level="practice" /> plan for slower recovery, but measure it in your lifter instead of assuming it. Older lifters should avoid long layoffs, because they lose more and regain it more slowly.

:::tip[In plain words]
Many older lifters need a bit more time between hard sessions. The research isn't certain how much, so watch how you actually feel and perform. The one thing older lifters should really avoid is stopping training for long stretches.
:::

### Programming adjustments

<Evidence level="practice" /> The NSCA position statement on older adults recommends resistance training as a powerful way to slow the loss of strength and muscle with age. Programs should be individualised and should progress gradually (Fragala et al., 2019). For masters powerlifters, coaches commonly adjust these variables:

| Variable | Typical adjustment for masters lifters | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Intensity** | Keep it heavy: most work at RPE 7–8, heavy singles at RPE 8–9 | Heavier training produced larger strength gains in older adults (Peterson et al., 2010) |
| **Volume** | Start at the low end of the intermediate range (e.g. 6–10 hard sets per lift per week) and add only if recovery allows | Recovery may be slower; joints tolerate less total work |
| **Frequency** | 2× per week per lift is often enough; 72 hours between hard sessions for the same lift | Spreads stress; leaves room for recovery |
| **Effort** | Avoid grinding reps to failure on the competition lifts | Failure adds fatigue without extra strength (see [Part 1 · Chapter 3](/lets-git-it/foundations/03-core-training-principles/)) |
| **Warm-up** | Longer: 10 minutes of general warm-up, then more ramp-up sets | Lifters often report stiff joints early in a session |
| **Exercise choice** | Swap painful variations freely (e.g. safety-bar squat, high-handle trap-bar deadlift, slight grip changes on bench) | Keep training stress high while joints stay comfortable |
| **Deloads** | Plan one every 3–4 weeks rather than every 5–6 | Fatigue may build faster |
| **Long-term** | Avoid long breaks; use maintenance blocks during busy periods | Older lifters lose more during time off (Bosquet et al., 2013) |

<Evidence level="practice" /> **Medical clearance:** masters lifters with known heart, metabolic or kidney disease, or with symptoms such as chest pain or unusual breathlessness, should see a doctor before heavy training. Heavy lifting raises blood pressure briefly during each rep.

:::tip[In plain words]
Older lifters should still lift heavy, but do a bit less total work, warm up longer, and space out the hard days. Swap any exercise that hurts for one that doesn't. If you have heart problems or worrying symptoms, see a doctor before you start heavy training.
:::

## Female lifters

### Sex differences that matter for programming

- **Absolute strength.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A narrative review found that males become clearly stronger than females from about age 15. In adults, the gap is **larger in the upper body than the lower body**. It comes mainly from greater muscle mass and larger type II fibres, not from better muscle activation (Nuzzo, 2023).
- **Response to training.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis found that men and women gained **muscle size and lower-body strength to a similar degree** on the same program. Women gained more **relative upper-body strength**, a moderate effect. Most participants were untrained, and the studies were short (Roberts et al., 2020).
- **Fatigue during a set.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> Women are usually **less fatigable** than men in sustained isometric contractions at the same relative intensity. The difference depends on the task, the muscle and the contraction speed (Hunter, 2014). <Evidence level="moderate" /> However, a meta-regression of 269 studies found that sex had **little influence** on how many reps people could do at a given percentage of 1RM (Nuzzo et al., 2024). The evidence on whether women can handle more reps at a given percentage is therefore mixed.
- **Recovery after damaging exercise.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> The same narrative review found **no sex difference** in strength loss or soreness after muscle-damaging exercise (Nuzzo, 2023).

**What this means for coaches:** <Evidence level="practice" /> program by the individual, not by sex. RPE-based loading ([Part 3 · Chapter 1](/lets-git-it/advanced/01-rpe-autoregulation/)) takes care of most differences in how many reps a lifter can do at a given percentage. Many female lifters are relatively weaker in the bench press than in the squat and deadlift, so extra bench practice and upper-body muscle often pay off.

:::tip[In plain words]
Women gain muscle and leg strength at about the same rate as men, and often gain upper-body strength faster in relative terms. Women don't need a "female program". Use the same principles, set loads by RPE, and pay extra attention to the bench press if it lags.
:::

### The menstrual cycle

This is an area where online advice runs well ahead of the evidence.

- **Effects on performance are trivial or unclear on average.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 78 studies found that exercise performance **might be trivially reduced** in the early follicular phase (around menstruation) compared with other phases. Because the effect was trivial, the studies varied widely and many were of low quality, the authors concluded that general phase-based guidelines cannot be made. They recommended an individual approach instead (McNulty et al., 2020). <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of strength measures specifically found **small or trivial, non-significant** differences between phases. Its authors also urged caution because the studies had a high risk of bias (Blagrove et al., 2020).
- **Phase-based training: disputed.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> A critical review argued, from a small number of training studies, that concentrating resistance training in the **follicular phase** may be superior to luteal-phase training for strength and muscle (Kissow et al., 2022). <Evidence level="practice" /> Another review group disagreed. They found highly variable results and poor checking of cycle phase in many studies, and concluded it is **premature** to say that cycle phase meaningfully affects strength performance or long-term adaptation (Colenso-Semple et al., 2023). Neither side has strong data. Phase-based programming is not supported well enough to recommend it as a default.
- **Hormonal contraceptives.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis found that oral contraceptive users **might** have slightly lower performance on average than naturally menstruating women, but the effect was trivial and most studies were of moderate to very low quality. The authors recommended an individual approach (Elliott-Sale et al., 2020).

**A practical approach:** <Evidence level="practice" />

1. **Track, don't assume.** Interested lifters can log cycle day and symptoms (cramps, fatigue, poor sleep, heavy bleeding) next to their training. After 2–3 cycles, check for a personal pattern.
2. **Let RPE absorb bad days.** If symptoms make a session hard, the RPE target lowers the load automatically. Use the same reduced-session rules as for a bad night's sleep ([Part 1 · Chapter 10](/lets-git-it/foundations/10-recovery-sleep-stress/)).
3. **Plan around a clear personal pattern only.** If a lifter reliably feels worse for 1–2 days each cycle, schedule lighter technique work or accessory days there when the calendar allows.
4. **Refer red flags.** Missing periods (when not pregnant or on a method that stops bleeding), very heavy bleeding, or severe pain should be checked by a doctor. Missing periods can be a sign of **low energy availability**. The IOC consensus on Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) describes how eating too little for the training load can harm both health and performance, in women and in men (Mountjoy et al., 2023).

:::tip[In plain words]
Research shows the menstrual cycle makes, on average, little or no difference to strength. Some women do feel worse on certain days, though. Track how you feel, let RPE lower the weights on bad days, and only plan around your cycle if you see a clear pattern of your own. If your period stops or is very heavy or painful, see a doctor.
:::

### Pregnancy and after birth

<Evidence level="practice" /> Pregnancy and the postpartum period need individual guidance from the lifter's obstetric care provider or another qualified health professional. The Canadian guideline on physical activity in pregnancy concluded that prenatal physical activity has moderate benefits and identified no harms in the outcomes it reviewed (Mottola et al., 2018). It is a general-population guideline, not a heavy-lifting protocol. The coach's job is to adapt training to the health professional's advice, not to give medical advice. Returning to heavy lifting after birth, including pelvic floor concerns, should also be guided by a health professional.

:::tip[In plain words]
Staying active during pregnancy is generally good, but how hard and how heavy to train is a decision for you and your doctor or midwife, not your coach. The same goes for getting back to heavy lifting after the baby arrives.
:::

## Youth and sub-junior lifters

### Is it safe and effective?

- **It works.** <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of resistance training in children and adolescents found a large overall effect on strength. The ability to gain strength **increased with age and maturity**, without a noticeable boost during puberty itself (Behringer et al., 2010). <Evidence level="strong" /> In youth athletes aged 6–18, a meta-analysis of 43 studies found moderate gains in strength and jumping. The most effective programs for strength lasted **more than 23 weeks** and used **5 sets per exercise, 6–8 reps, 80–89% 1RM, and 3–4 minutes' rest** (Lesinski et al., 2016).
- **It is relatively safe when supervised.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A review of 22 training studies in pre- and early-pubertal youth found that supervised training did **not harm growth** in height or weight. Only three injuries were reported in the ten studies that tracked them (Malina, 2006). <Evidence level="moderate" /> A review of injuries in young lifters, including weightlifters and powerlifters, found that most reported injuries were **accidental** and linked to poor technique, too-heavy loads or a lack of qualified supervision (Faigenbaum & Myer, 2010).
- **Position statements support it.** <Evidence level="practice" /> The NSCA (Faigenbaum et al., 2009) and an international consensus endorsed by many professional bodies (Lloyd et al., 2014) both support youth resistance training when it is properly designed and supervised by qualified professionals.

:::tip[In plain words]
Teenagers can lift weights safely and get much stronger, and heavy weights are fine with good coaching. Most injuries in young lifters come from poor technique, weights that are too heavy, or nobody watching, not from lifting itself.
:::

### Programming for sub-juniors

<Evidence level="practice" />

| Priority | How |
|---|---|
| **Technique first** | Load goes up only while technique stays consistent on video. Use more sets of fewer reps so every rep is good. |
| **Heavy, but rarely maximal** | Most work at RPE 6–8; singles at RPE 8 or below in training; true maxes at competitions. |
| **Supervision** | A qualified coach at every heavy session; spotters or safety bars on squat and bench. |
| **Broad base** | Keep some general athletic work (jumps, sprints, carries, other sports). Long-term development matters more than this year's total. |
| **No weight cutting** | Compete in whatever class the lifter naturally falls into; growth will move them up anyway. |
| **School and sleep** | Teenagers need 8–10 hours of sleep (see [Part 1 · Chapter 10](/lets-git-it/foundations/10-recovery-sleep-stress/)). Plan lighter weeks around exams. |
| **Growth spurts** | During rapid growth, coordination can temporarily worsen and some lifters report aches near the knees or heels. Reduce load or swap exercises, and refer persistent pain to a health professional. |

:::tip[In plain words]
Young lifters should learn great technique, lift fairly heavy but rarely all-out, and always have a coach watching. Don't make them cut weight. Keep them playing other sports and sleeping enough. They have years ahead of them.
:::

## Weight-class considerations

### Bodyweight, muscle and strength

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 20 elite male powerlifters, squat, bench press and deadlift performance were very strongly related to **fat-free mass** (correlations of about 0.86–0.95). The authors concluded that powerlifting performance may be limited by how much fat-free mass a lifter can build (Brechue & Abe, 2002).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 54 male powerlifters, heavier lifters had more muscle **and** more fat, plus larger girths and bone breadths (Keogh et al., 2007).

<Evidence level="practice" /> Muscle is what moves a lifter's total. Extra fat adds bodyweight but little strength. Absolute strength rises with bodyweight, but not in direct proportion, which is why points formulas such as IPF GL are used ([Part 1 · Chapter 1](/lets-git-it/foundations/01-what-is-powerlifting/)).

:::tip[In plain words]
Stronger lifters usually carry more muscle. Gaining muscle almost always raises your total, while gaining fat mostly just raises the number on the scale.
:::

### Choosing a class

<Evidence level="practice" /> Use this decision guide:

| Situation | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Novice or early intermediate | Compete at your current walking weight. Build muscle first; choose a class later. |
| Walking weight sits **0–2% above** a class limit | Compete in the lower class with a small, planned weigh-in strategy (Part 4, Chapter 4: Making weight). |
| Walking weight sits **well above** a class limit and body fat is moderate to high | A slow diet over months into the lower class may suit (see below). |
| Walking weight sits **in the middle or bottom** of a class, and the lifter is lean | Gain slowly toward the top of the class, or into the next class. |
| Sub-junior or junior still growing | Stay at natural bodyweight; let growth decide. |
| Female lifter with missing periods, frequent illness or injuries while dieting | Stop dieting; check for low energy availability with a doctor or sports dietitian (Mountjoy et al., 2023). |

:::tip[In plain words]
Pick a weight class that fits the body you have now, or one you can grow into slowly. Don't pick a class you can only make by starving or dehydrating yourself.
:::

### Gaining into a class

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 39 elite athletes over 8–12 weeks, a planned surplus increased bodyweight more than eating freely, but the extra gain was mostly **fat**; lean-mass gains and 1RM gains did not differ between groups (Garthe et al., 2013).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Aim for about **0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per week**, with more advanced lifters at the lower end (Iraki et al., 2019). This matches [Part 1 · Chapter 11](/lets-git-it/foundations/11-nutrition/). For an 83 kg lifter, that is about 0.2–0.4 kg per week.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Pair the gaining phase with a higher-volume, hypertrophy-focused block, and give it time: moving up a full class usually takes many months, not one block.

### Moving down a class

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Strength can be kept in a moderate deficit, but muscle gain is impaired. A meta-analysis found a deficit of about 500 kcal per day prevented lean-mass gains (Murphy & Koehler, 2022).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 24 elite athletes, losing about **0.7% of bodyweight per week** allowed lean-mass gains. A faster rate (about 1% per week achieved) did not (Garthe et al., 2011).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Diet during lower-intensity blocks, months before a meet. Never rely on a large acute cut to make a class repeatedly; making weight is covered in Part 4, Chapter 4.

:::tip[In plain words]
Change weight class slowly. Gaining or losing a few hundred grams a week keeps your strength and builds or keeps muscle. Fast changes add fat when you gain and cost muscle when you lose.
:::

## Sample programs

All loads come from the RPE chart in [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg. Accessories are listed by RPE only.

### Program 1: Masters lifter, 3 days per week

**Lifter:** 55-year-old man, Masters 2, 93 kg class. Squat 170 kg, bench press 115 kg, deadlift 200 kg. Eight years of training; stiff knees in the morning; works full time.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | General warm-up (bike or brisk walk, then bodyweight squats and band work) | 10 min | Easy |
| Mon | Squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~132.5 kg) |
| Mon | Bench press | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~90 kg) |
| Mon | Chest-supported row | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| Wed | General warm-up | 10 min | Easy |
| Wed | Deadlift | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 (~162.5 kg) |
| Wed | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| Wed | Leg press | 2 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| Fri | General warm-up | 10 min | Easy |
| Fri | Squat | 1 × 3, then 2 × 3 | RPE 8 (~147.5 kg), then RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) |
| Fri | Bench press | 1 × 3, then 3 × 5 | RPE 8 (~100 kg), then RPE 7 (~90 kg) |
| Fri | Back extension | 2 × 12 | RPE 7 |

**Weekly hard sets:** squat 7, bench press 11 (including close-grip), deadlift 3 (plus leg press and back extensions).

**Why it's built this way:**

- Loads stay heavy (most sets at RPE 7–8), because heavier training gives older lifters larger strength gains (Peterson et al., 2010). Volume sits at the low end, and nothing goes to failure.
- Each lift's hard sessions are at least 48–72 hours apart. Heavy deadlifting happens once a week.
- **Week 4 is a deload:** same exercises, 2 sets each, RPE 6.
- **Joint rule:** if a lift causes joint pain above about 3/10 that lasts into the next day, swap it for the nearest pain-free variation (for example safety-bar squat or a high-handle trap-bar deadlift) for that block. Refer persistent pain to a health professional.
- **Progression:** when all sets at a given RPE feel easier for two weeks running, add 2.5 kg. Expect slow, steady progress.

### Program 2: Female lifter, 4 days per week, with cycle-aware adjustments

**Lifter:** 25-year-old woman, 63 kg class. Squat 120 kg, bench press 65 kg, deadlift 150 kg. Her bench press is weak relative to her other lifts. She has noticed that she often sleeps badly and feels flat on the first 1–2 days of her period.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~50 kg) |
| 1 | Dumbbell row | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 2 | Deadlift | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 (~122.5 kg) |
| 2 | Bench press | 5 × 3 | RPE 8 (~55 kg) |
| 2 | Lat pulldown | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Squat | 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~102.5 kg) |
| 3 | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Pause bench press (2 s) | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × 4 | RPE 6.5 |
| 4 | Dumbbell overhead press | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |

**Weekly hard sets:** squat 10, bench press 16 (including variations), deadlift 6 (including Romanian deadlift).

**Why it's built this way:**

- The structure is the same as any intermediate 4-day program. **Sex does not change the principles** (Roberts et al., 2020).
- Bench press gets 4 sessions and the most sets, plus upper-back work, because it is her weakest lift relative to the others.
- **Cycle-aware rules:** she logs cycle day and symptoms. On low days, RPE sets the load, and she uses the "one short night" rules from [Part 1 · Chapter 10](/lets-git-it/foundations/10-recovery-sleep-stress/) (drop one back-off set if RPE runs 1+ point high). After three cycles, if her log shows a consistent pattern, the coach can place Day 4 (the lightest day) on her usual worst day. **The plan does not change volume or intensity by cycle phase**, because the evidence does not support that (McNulty et al., 2020; Colenso-Semple et al., 2023).
- **Red flags:** missed periods, frequent illness or injury, or falling performance during a diet lead to a referral to a doctor or sports dietitian (Mountjoy et al., 2023).

### Program 3: Sub-junior, 3 days per week

**Lifter:** 16-year-old boy, about 72 kg (74 kg class), training for 18 months. Squat 120 kg, bench press 80 kg, deadlift 150 kg. Trains after school with his coach present.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Jumps (box jump or broad jump) | 3 × 3 | Easy, full effort |
| Mon | Squat | 5 × 4 | RPE 7 (~97.5 kg) |
| Mon | Bench press | 5 × 5 | RPE 7 (~62.5 kg) |
| Mon | Pull-up or assisted pull-up | 3 × 6–8 | RPE 8 |
| Wed | Deadlift | 4 × 3 | RPE 7 (~125 kg) |
| Wed | Bench press (2 s pause) | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| Wed | Goblet squat | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| Wed | Plank and side plank | 3 × 30 s each | — |
| Fri | Squat | 4 × 3 | RPE 8 (~102.5 kg) |
| Fri | Bench press | 4 × 3 | RPE 8 (~70 kg) |
| Fri | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| Fri | Dumbbell row | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |

**Why it's built this way:**

- Four to five sets per lift at roughly 79–86% of 1RM sit close to the dose that worked best for strength in youth athletes (5 sets per exercise at 80–89% 1RM; Lesinski et al., 2016). Sets use 3–5 reps rather than the 6–8 in that analysis, so every rep stays technically sharp.
- **No sets above RPE 8 in training**, and no 1RM tests outside competition. Every heavy set is filmed or watched for technique; if technique slips, the load drops 5–10%.
- Jumps, pull-ups and core work keep him developing as an athlete, not just as a lifter.
- **Exam weeks:** use the reduced week from [Part 1 · Chapter 10](/lets-git-it/foundations/10-recovery-sleep-stress/) (two-thirds of the sets, RPE capped at 7).
- **Progression:** add 2.5 kg to a lift once all sets at its RPE target move cleanly for a week. Bodyweight will rise as he grows; that is expected, not a problem to diet away.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating masters lifters as fragile.** They still need heavy loads to get stronger. Reduce volume and space sessions out; don't remove intensity.
- **Long breaks for older lifters.** Detraining costs older lifters more. Use maintenance blocks instead of stopping.
- **Building a "female program".** Women adapt to the same principles; adjust for the individual, especially a lagging bench press.
- **Programming by cycle phase without data.** Average effects are trivial or unclear. Track symptoms and use RPE; plan around a pattern only if the lifter clearly shows one.
- **Ignoring missed periods during a diet.** This can signal low energy availability and needs a doctor's input.
- **Testing true maxes with teenagers in training, or letting them train unsupervised.** Keep singles at RPE 8 or below and save maxes for competition.
- **Making young or novice lifters cut weight.** Growth and muscle gain matter more than a class advantage.
- **Choosing a class by how much you can cut.** Repeated hard cuts cost training quality; change class slowly over months.

## Key takeaways

- IPF age divisions: sub-junior 14–18, junior 19–23, Masters 1–4 at 40+, 50+, 60+ and 70+, counted by calendar year.
- Strength declines slowly with age, but older adults still gain strength well, especially with heavier loads. Masters lifters need lower volume, more spacing and longer warm-ups, not light weights.
- Women gain muscle and lower-body strength like men, often gain more relative upper-body strength, and recover similarly after damaging exercise.
- On average, menstrual-cycle effects on strength are trivial or unclear; track symptoms and let RPE adjust the load.
- Supervised, technique-first heavy training is safe and effective for sub-juniors.
- Strength follows fat-free mass. Gain at about 0.25–0.5% and diet at about 0.7% of bodyweight per week, and don't build a career on repeated hard cuts.
