## In one minute

A good session has four parts: a short **general warm-up** to raise body temperature, **specific warm-up sets** that ramp up to the working weight, the **main work** done most important first, and **accessories** last. Warming up improves performance in most studies, and the specific warm-up sets matter most for a powerlifter because they rehearse the lift and prepare the body for heavy loads. Short static stretches (under about 60 seconds per muscle) do little harm, while long holds before lifting can reduce strength for a while. Rest 3–5 minutes between heavy sets, and plan the session so the competition lifts get your freshest effort. Most powerlifting sessions fit in 60–90 minutes; a well-planned 45-minute session still works.

## Why warm up

<Evidence level="practice" /> A warm-up raises muscle and body temperature, speeds up the body's energy systems, primes the nervous system, and gets the lifter mentally ready (McGowan et al., 2015).

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 32 high-quality studies found that warming up **improved performance in 79% of the outcomes measured**, with little evidence that it harms performance. The authors noted that few studies were well-controlled randomized trials (Fradkin et al., 2010).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of upper-body warm-ups (31 studies) concluded that **warm-ups using heavy loads improve strength and power** performance (McCrary et al., 2015).

:::tip[In plain words]
Warming up gets your body and mind ready to lift. It rarely hurts and usually helps, so don't skip it. For heavy lifting, the most important part is doing lighter sets of the lift itself before your real sets.
:::

## General vs. specific warm-up

- **General warm-up:** low-intensity whole-body activity (bike, rower, brisk walk, light dynamic movements) to raise body temperature.
- **Specific warm-up:** lighter sets of the exercise about to be trained, building up to the working weight.

What the research shows:

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 13 trained participants, adding a **20-minute easy bike ride** before the same specific warm-up raised leg press 1RM by **8.4% on average**, compared with the specific warm-up alone (Abad et al., 2011). A general warm-up may help most before maximal attempts.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 15 men doing 4 sets to failure at 80% 1RM on bench press, squat and arm curl, the number of reps did not differ between no warm-up, a specific warm-up, an aerobic warm-up and a combined warm-up (Ribeiro et al., 2014). For multi-rep sets at moderate loads, the type of warm-up may matter less.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 40 resistance-trained men doing 3 × 6 at 80% 1RM, warm-ups with **only a few light reps were not enough** to get the best bar speed. Squat sets moved fastest after a warm-up set of 6 at 80% of the training load; bench press did best after one set at 40% and one at 80% of the training load (Ribeiro et al., 2020).

**Decision rule** <Evidence level="practice" />: 5–10 minutes of general warm-up, then ramp up with specific sets that get close to the working weight. Spend more time on both before heavy singles or a max test than before a moderate volume day. In a hot, humid gym you will feel warm sooner, so the general part can be shorter, but keep every specific set.

:::tip[In plain words]
Start with a few minutes of easy movement to get warm, like cycling or brisk walking. Then do lighter sets of the lift, getting heavier each time, until you're close to your working weight. The heavier the day, the more carefully you warm up.
:::

## Stretching and foam rolling before lifting

- <Evidence level="strong" /> Long static stretches reduce strength for a short time; short ones barely do. A meta-analysis of 104 studies found an average strength loss of about 5% after static stretching, with the smallest effects when stretches lasted 45 seconds or less (Simic et al., 2013). A systematic review found no meaningful effect with stretches under 30–45 seconds and a likely loss with stretches of 60 seconds or more (Kay & Blazevich, 2012). Another review found a 4.6% loss with 60 seconds or more of stretching per muscle and 1.1% with less than 60 seconds; when dynamic activity followed the stretching, there was no clear effect (Behm et al., 2016).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A later review concluded that short stretches (60 seconds or less per muscle) inside a full warm-up have only a trivial effect, but advised high-performance athletes to use them with care before competition (Chaabene et al., 2019).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of foam rolling found that rolling before exercise slightly improved flexibility but had a negligible effect on strength (Wiewelhove et al., 2019).

**Decision rule** <Evidence level="practice" />: if a lifter needs more range to reach a position (for example squat depth), use short stretches or mobility drills of under 60 seconds per muscle, then do the specific warm-up sets afterwards. Save long stretching sessions for after training or another time. Mobility work in detail is covered in Part 5, Chapter 8 (Mobility and movement preparation).

:::tip[In plain words]
Holding a stretch for a minute or longer right before lifting can make you a little weaker for a while. Short stretches of under a minute are fine if you need them to get into position. Foam rolling won't make you stronger, but it won't hurt either.
:::

## Ramping warm-up sets

<Evidence level="practice" /> There is little research on exactly how many warm-up sets to do. The method below is standard coaching practice: enough sets to rehearse the lift and feel the heavier weight, without tiring the lifter before the work that counts.

**The rules:**

1. Start light: the empty bar for squat and bench, about 60 kg for deadlift (a 20 kg bar with two 20 kg plates sets the right height).
2. As load goes up, **reps go down**: about 8–10 → 5 → 3 → 2 → 1.
3. **Jumps get smaller** as the weight gets heavier.
4. The last warm-up set is about **85–90% of the first working set**.
5. Rest about 1 minute between light warm-ups (just long enough to load the bar) and 2–3 minutes before the last one or two.
6. Warm-ups should feel easy. If one feels heavy, add a set with a smaller jump rather than skipping ahead.

| Step | % of first working weight | Reps |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Empty bar (squat, bench) | 8–10 |
| 2 | ~40% | 5 |
| 3 | ~60% | 3 |
| 4 | ~75% | 2 |
| 5 | ~85–90% | 1 |
| Work | 100% | As programmed |

### Worked ramps (squat 180 kg, bench 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg)

**Squat: 4 × 5 at RPE 7 (~142.5 kg)**

| Set | Load | Reps | % of working weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 kg (bar) | 10 | — |
| 2 | 60 kg | 5 | 42% |
| 3 | 85 kg | 3 | 60% |
| 4 | 107.5 kg | 2 | 75% |
| 5 | 125 kg | 1 | 88% |
| Work | 142.5 kg | 4 × 5 | 100% |

**Bench press: 4 × 5 at RPE 7 (~95 kg)**

| Set | Load | Reps | % of working weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 kg (bar) | 10 | — |
| 2 | 40 kg | 5 | 42% |
| 3 | 57.5 kg | 3 | 61% |
| 4 | 72.5 kg | 2 | 76% |
| 5 | 82.5 kg | 1 | 87% |
| Work | 95 kg | 4 × 5 | 100% |

**Deadlift: 3 × 3 at RPE 7 (~175 kg)**

| Set | Load | Reps | % of working weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 60 kg | 5 | 34% |
| 2 | 105 kg | 3 | 60% |
| 3 | 130 kg | 2 | 74% |
| 4 | 152.5 kg | 1 | 87% |

**The second lift of the day** needs fewer warm-ups because the body is already warm. Drop step 1 or 2 if the lifter feels ready, but keep at least one step near 85–90%, since it rehearses the specific load.

:::tip[In plain words]
Start with the empty bar and add weight in steps, doing fewer reps each time. Your last warm-up should be close to your working weight, maybe one rep. The warm-up should feel easy: its job is to prepare you, not to tire you out.
:::

## Exercise order

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 11 studies found that **strength gains are largest in the exercises done at the start of a session** (Nunes et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A review found that more reps, and so more volume, are done on an exercise when it comes first, whatever its size. The authors recommended ordering exercises by priority (Simão et al., 2012).

**Decision rules** <Evidence level="practice" />:

1. **Most important lift first:** usually the competition lift (or its main variation) that the day is built around.
2. **Heaviest and most technical before lighter and simpler:** competition lifts, then close variations, then accessories.
3. **Two competition lifts on one day:** put the one with the higher priority this block first. If the order never changes, the second lift is always trained tired, so swap the order on another day of the week when it makes sense.
4. **Squat and deadlift on the same day:** usually squat first, with the deadlift work lighter or a variation, because both tire the legs and lower back.
5. **Accessories last,** and the ones closest to failure at the very end.

:::tip[In plain words]
Do the most important lift first, while you're fresh. Then the other main lifts, then the extra exercises. If you always do the same lift second, give it a turn to go first on another day.
:::

## Rest between sets

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 23 studies (491 participants) found that rests longer than 2 minutes appear necessary to get the most strength in trained lifters, while 60–120 seconds may be enough for untrained people (Grgic et al., 2018).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 21 trained men, 3-minute rests produced larger squat and bench press 1RM gains than 1-minute rests over 8 weeks (Schoenfeld et al., 2016).

<Evidence level="practice" /> Use the rest guide from [Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/foundations/04-training-variables/): **3–5 minutes** for heavy competition-lift sets, **2–4 minutes** for moderate sets and variations, **1–2 minutes** for accessories. Start the next set when you are ready, not only when the clock says so; heavy singles may need the full 5 minutes.

:::tip[In plain words]
Rest long enough to do your next set well. For heavy squats, benches and deadlifts that's three to five minutes. For smaller exercises, one to two minutes is enough.
:::

## Session length and pacing

<Evidence level="practice" /> There is no good research on ideal session length for powerlifters. Length is simply the sum of warm-ups, sets and rests, so plan it in advance.

**How to estimate it.** Count about **1 minute per set** plus the rest after it.

**Worked example:** squat 4 × 5 with 4 minutes' rest = 4 × (1 + 4) = 20 minutes, plus 5 warm-up sets at about 1.5 minutes each = about 8 minutes. The squat takes about **28 minutes**. Add the bench (about 25 minutes) and two accessories (about 15 minutes) and a 7-minute general warm-up: about **75 minutes**.

**When time is short, cut in this order:**

1. Drop or shorten accessories (or pair two accessories back to back).
2. Drop a set of each variation.
3. Use fewer, larger warm-up jumps on the second lift.
4. Keep the competition-lift work and its rest periods; that is the last thing to cut.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Pairing an accessory with an unrelated one (for example a row with a leg curl) saves time, because one muscle group rests while the other works. Don't pair competition-lift sets with anything that tires the same muscles.

:::tip[In plain words]
A session is as long as its warm-ups, sets and rests add up to. Work that out before you start. If you're short on time, cut the extra exercises first and protect the main lifts.
:::

## Anatomy of a session

| Phase | Time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **1. General warm-up** | 5–10 min | Bike, rower or brisk walk; light dynamic moves for hips, ankles, upper back |
| **2. Movement prep (if needed)** | 0–5 min | Short mobility drills or stretches under 60 s for the lifter's tight spots |
| **3. Specific warm-up, lift 1** | 8–12 min | Ramp sets to the first working weight |
| **4. Main work, lift 1** | 15–30 min | Competition lift or main variation; 3–5 min rests |
| **5. Specific warm-up and work, lift 2** | 20–30 min | Fewer warm-up sets; 2–4 min rests |
| **6. Accessories** | 10–20 min | 1–2 min rests; pair them to save time |
| **7. Cool-down (optional)** | 0–10 min | Easy movement, longer stretches if the lifter wants them |

:::tip[In plain words]
Every session follows the same pattern: get warm, build up to your working weight, do your main lifts, then finish with the smaller exercises. The main lifts get most of the time and your best energy.
:::

## Sample programs

Intermediate lifter, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. Loads from the RPE chart in [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/).

### Program 1: Full session timeline, squat and bench day (about 80 minutes)

| Clock | Phase | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | General warm-up | Bike, then leg swings, bodyweight squats, band pull-aparts | 7 min | Easy | — |
| 0:07 | Squat warm-up | Squat | 10, 5, 3, 2, 1 | 20, 60, 87.5, 110, 127.5 kg | 1 min, then 2–3 min before the last two |
| 0:17 | Main work | Squat | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~145 kg) | 4 min |
| 0:37 | Bench warm-up | Bench press | 10, 3, 2, 1 | 20, 55, 70, 80 kg | 1 min |
| 0:45 | Main work | Bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) | 3 min |
| 1:01 | Accessories (paired) | Barbell row + back extension | 3 × 10 each, alternating | RPE 8 | 1 min between exercises |
| 1:15 | Cool-down | Easy walk, optional stretching | 5 min | — | — |
| 1:20 | End | | | | |

**Why it's built this way.** The squat comes first because it is this day's priority. The bench needs a short ramp because the lifter is already warm. Accessories are paired because the row and back extension don't compete with each other much, which saves several minutes.

### Program 2: 45-minute session for a busy lifter

A lifter with a 45-minute lunch break, three days a week. This is the deadlift and bench day.

| Clock | Phase | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | General warm-up | Rower or jumping jacks, hip hinges, arm circles | 3 min | Easy | — |
| 0:03 | Deadlift warm-up | Deadlift | 5, 3, 1 | 60, 120, 155 kg | 1–2 min |
| 0:09 | Main work | Deadlift | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 (~175 kg) | 3–4 min |
| 0:22 | Bench warm-up | Bench press | 8, 3, 1 | 20, 60, 80 kg | 1 min |
| 0:26 | Main work | Bench press | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) | 3 min |
| 0:37 | Accessories (paired) | Chin-ups + triceps extension | 2 × 8–10 each, alternating | RPE 8 | 1 min between exercises |
| 0:45 | End | | | | |

**Why it's built this way.** The general warm-up is short and the deadlift ramp has three bigger jumps, because the deadlift sets are moderate (RPE 7) and the lifter has been doing this lift for years. Only two main lifts get trained; accessories are cut to two paired sets. The competition-lift work and its rests are kept intact. Fitting training around a busy life is covered further in Part 3, Chapter 11 (Programming around life stress and limited time).

### Program 3: Warm-up ramps for heavy singles

On heavy-single days (for example an RPE 8.5 single, ~94% 1RM), take more, smaller steps near the top. Each extra single at 85–93% rehearses the heavy weight without tiring the lifter. Meet-day warm-ups follow the same idea and are covered in Part 4, Chapter 6 (Meet day).

| 1 | 20 kg (bar) | 10 | — | 1 min |
| 2 | 60 kg | 5 | 35% | 1 min |
| 3 | 100 kg | 3 | 59% | 2 min |
| 4 | 125 kg | 2 | 74% | 2 min |
| 5 | 145 kg | 1 | 85% | 3 min |
| 6 | 157.5 kg | 1 | 93% | 3–4 min |
| Top | 170 kg | 1 | 100% | — |

**Bench press: top single ~112.5 kg (RPE 8.5)**

| Set | Load | Reps | % of top single | Rest after |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 kg (bar) | 10 | — | 1 min |
| 2 | 50 kg | 5 | 44% | 1 min |
| 3 | 70 kg | 3 | 62% | 2 min |
| 4 | 85 kg | 2 | 76% | 2 min |
| 5 | 97.5 kg | 1 | 87% | 3 min |
| 6 | 105 kg | 1 | 93% | 3 min |
| Top | 112.5 kg | 1 | 100% | — |

**Deadlift: top single ~197.5 kg (RPE 8.5)**

| Set | Load | Reps | % of top single | Rest after |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 70 kg | 5 | 35% | 1 min |
| 2 | 120 kg | 3 | 61% | 2 min |
| 3 | 150 kg | 2 | 76% | 2 min |
| 4 | 170 kg | 1 | 86% | 3 min |
| 5 | 185 kg | 1 | 94% | 3–4 min |
| Top | 197.5 kg | 1 | 100% | — |

**How to use it.** If the 85% single feels slow or heavy, the lifter is not at their best today: stop at the 93% step and call it the top single, or follow the RPE rules from [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/). The deadlift has one fewer step because each heavy deadlift single is more tiring than a squat or bench single. The deadlift starts at 70 kg instead of the empty bar, because the bar has to sit at plate height; the heavy steps are the same.

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping the specific warm-up** and going straight from the empty bar to the working weight. Big jumps leave the lifter unprepared for the heavy sets.
- **Warming up too hard.** Warm-up sets with many reps close to the working weight cause fatigue before the sets that count.
- **Long static stretches right before heavy lifting.** Holds of 60 seconds or more can temporarily reduce strength.
- **Cutting rest on heavy sets to save time.** Cut accessories instead.
- **Always training the same lift second.** It never gets your best effort; rotate the order where possible.
- **Not planning session length.** Sessions that run long get rushed at the end, usually on the work that matters.

## Key takeaways

- Warm-ups improve performance in most studies; the specific warm-up sets matter most for powerlifters.
- Do 5–10 minutes of general warm-up, then ramp with heavier, lower-rep sets to about 85–90% of the working weight.
- Short stretches (under 60 seconds per muscle) are fine; long holds before lifting can reduce strength.
- Order exercises by priority: the most important lift first, accessories last.
- Rest 3–5 minutes on heavy sets; plan session length in advance and cut accessories first when short on time.
