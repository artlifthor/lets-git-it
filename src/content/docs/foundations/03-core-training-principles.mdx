## In one minute

Every good powerlifting program, whatever its name, follows the same seven principles: **specificity** (train what you will be tested on), **overload** (keep asking for a bit more), **fatigue management** (don't let tiredness bury progress), **SRA** (time sessions to recovery), **variation** (change things on purpose, not at random), **phase potentiation** (order training blocks so each sets up the next), and **individual difference** (adjust for the lifter in front of you). Coaches use them to judge any program and to fix their own. Specificity and overload drive progress; the other five keep it going for years.

## 1. Specificity

**Adaptations match the training.** To get better at a heavy squat, bench press and deadlift, a lifter must regularly train those lifts, with competition technique, at heavy loads.

- <Evidence level="strong" /> Meta-analyses comparing light and heavy loads found that **heavy-load training produced larger gains in one-rep max strength**, while muscle growth was similar across a wide range of loads (Schoenfeld et al., 2017; Lopez et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Exercises sit on a ladder from most to least specific. Training should move up the ladder as a meet approaches (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith; Siff).

| Rung | Examples (squat) | Main use |
|---|---|---|
| **Competition lift** | Back squat to competition depth and commands | Skill and maximal strength |
| **Close variation** | Pause squat, tempo squat, squat with a different bar position | Fix weak points, add volume with similar skill |
| **Related exercise** | Front squat, belt squat, leg press | Build muscle and work capacity |
| **General exercise** | Lunges, leg extensions, sled work | Muscle, joint health, conditioning |

:::tip[In plain words]
You get good at what you practise. To lift heavy in a competition, you have to practise the competition lifts, the competition way, with heavy weights, and do it more as the meet gets closer.
:::

## 2. Overload

**The stimulus must keep progressing.** A lifter adapts to whatever they do repeatedly; to keep adapting, something must increase over time: load, reps, sets, or effort.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis found that doing **more weekly sets** produced larger strength gains than doing fewer (Ralston et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In an 8-week study, adding reps each week and adding load each week produced similar strength and muscle gains (Plotkin et al., 2022). Overload does not have to mean more weight every session.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Novices can add load almost every session; intermediate and advanced lifters progress over weeks or months, which is why their programs need periodization (Rippetoe & Baker).

:::tip[In plain words]
Your body gets used to whatever you keep doing. To keep improving, you have to ask a little more of it over time: a bit more weight, a few more reps, or a few more sets.
:::

## 3. Fatigue management

**Hard training creates fatigue; too much of it stops progress and raises injury risk.** Good programs give enough stimulus to adapt while keeping fatigue under control.

- <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis found **no extra strength gain from training to failure** compared with stopping short of it. When training volume was not matched, stopping short of failure actually produced slightly *better* strength gains (Grgic et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> Training to failure slowed recovery by 24–48 hours compared with the same work done short of failure (Morán-Navarro et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Tools: keep most competition-lift sets at RPE 6–9, cap weekly volume, deload regularly, and lower training stress when life stress is high.

:::tip[In plain words]
Training until you can't do another rep makes you a lot more tired without making you any stronger. Stop most sets with a rep or two left in the tank, and take easy weeks so tiredness doesn't pile up.
:::

## 4. Stimulus–recovery–adaptation (SRA)

**Time each lift's hard sessions to its recovery.** Covered in detail in [Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/foundations/02-stress-recovery-adaptation/). In short: bench press recovers fastest and is usually trained 3–4 times per week, squat 2–3 times, and heavy deadlift 1–2 times.

:::tip[In plain words]
Train a lift again once you've recovered from the last hard session, not before and not long after.
:::

## 5. Variation

**Change the program on purpose to keep adapting and to stay healthy, but not so much that skill and progress are lost.**

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review found that **planned, systematic variation** of exercises can improve muscle and strength gains, while **excessive, random variation** (changing exercises too often, or rotating exercises that do the same job) may hold gains back (Kassiano et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Vary exercises every block (every 3–6 weeks), not every session. Keep the competition lifts in every block. Choose variations for a reason, such as a weak point, an injury, or a different rep range.

:::tip[In plain words]
Changing your training can help, but only if you change it for a reason and not too often. Keep the main lifts in all the time, and swap the extra exercises every few weeks.
:::

## 6. Phase potentiation

**Order training phases so each one prepares the next.** For example, a phase that builds muscle and work capacity makes the next, heavier phase more productive, which in turn sets up the peak.

- <Evidence level="strong" /> Periodized training, which plans phases in sequence, produces larger strength gains than non-periodized training (Williams et al., 2017; Moesgaard et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> The specific idea that each phase **enhances** the next is coaching theory; it has not been tested directly in powerlifters (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith). Block periodization ([Part 2, Chapter 6](/lets-git-it/building-programs/06-block-periodization/)) is built on it.

:::tip[In plain words]
Plan training in stages, like building a house: foundation first, then walls, then the roof. Building muscle first makes the heavy strength phase work better, and that sets you up to peak.
:::

## 7. Individual difference

**Lifters respond differently to the same program.** Age, sex, training history, body proportions, recovery capacity, life stress and preferences all matter.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In pooled data from 287 untrained men and women aged 19–78, strength changes after resistance training ranged from −8% to +60%. About 7% of participants were classed as low responders for strength and 29% for muscle size, regardless of age or sex (Ahtiainen et al., 2016).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Start from a sound general program, then adjust using the lifter's response: track performance, RPE and recovery, and change one thing at a time.

:::tip[In plain words]
Two people can follow the exact same program and get very different results. A good coach starts with a solid plan, watches how the lifter responds, and adjusts it for that person.
:::

## How the principles rank

<Evidence level="practice" /> When principles conflict, coaches generally rank them like this (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith):

1. **Specificity**: without it, strength won't show up on the platform.
2. **Overload**: without it, there is nothing to adapt to.
3. **Fatigue management**: without it, overload can't continue.
4. **SRA**: decides frequency.
5. **Variation**: keeps progress and joints healthy over months.
6. **Phase potentiation**: improves long-term planning.
7. **Individual difference**: tunes everything to the lifter.

## Sample programs

Intermediate lifter, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. RPE targets from [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/).

### Program 1: A 4-week block, annotated

Main work only (accessories omitted). Each week adds load while RPE rises; week 4 is a deload.

| Week | Squat (day 1) | Bench press (day 1 and 3) | Deadlift (day 2) | Principles at work |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 × 5 @ 140 kg (RPE ~7) | 4 × 6 @ 90 kg (RPE ~7) | 3 × 3 @ 172.5 kg (RPE ~7) | **Specificity** (competition lifts, moderate-heavy), **fatigue management** (RPE 7 start) |
| 2 | 4 × 5 @ 145 kg (RPE ~7.5) | 4 × 6 @ 92.5 kg (RPE ~7.5) | 3 × 3 @ 177.5 kg (RPE ~7.5) | **Overload** (+2.5–5 kg) |
| 3 | 4 × 5 @ 147.5 kg (RPE ~8) | 4 × 6 @ 95 kg (RPE ~8) | 3 × 3 @ 182.5 kg (RPE ~8) | **Overload**, still short of failure |
| 4 | 2 × 5 @ 130 kg | 2 × 6 @ 85 kg | 2 × 3 @ 160 kg | **Fatigue management** (deload), **SRA** |
| Next block | Swap pause squat for tempo squat; move to sets of 3–4 | Swap close-grip for long-pause bench | Swap deficit for block pulls | **Variation** (planned), **phase potentiation** |

**Individual difference:** if a week's RPE comes in 1+ higher than the target, repeat the week's loads instead of adding weight.

### Program 2: Far from a meet vs. close to a meet

The same lifter, 12 weeks out and 3 weeks out. Note how specificity rises as the meet approaches.

**12 weeks out (general preparation)**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| 1 | Leg press | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 2 | Deadlift | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 |
| 2 | Dumbbell press | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 2 | Barbell row | 4 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Front squat | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Close-grip bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Bench press | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Back extension, lunges | 3 × 12 each | RPE 8 |

**3 weeks out (specific preparation)**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 1 × 2 @ RPE 8, then 3 × 3 | ~155 kg back-offs |
| 1 | Bench press (competition pause) | 1 × 2 @ RPE 8, then 3 × 3 | ~102.5 kg back-offs |
| 2 | Deadlift | 1 × 2 @ RPE 8, then 2 × 3 | ~177.5 kg back-offs |
| 3 | Squat | 3 × 2 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Bench press (competition pause) | 4 × 2 | RPE 7.5 |
| 4 | Bench press | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 |

Fewer exercises, lower reps, heavier loads, all with competition commands: specificity and phase potentiation in action.

## Common mistakes

- **Copying a program without checking it against the principles.** Ask of any program: is it specific, does it progress, and does it manage fatigue?
- **Going to failure to "make sure it's hard enough".** Effort close to failure is enough; failure itself adds fatigue, not strength.
- **Changing exercises every week.** Too much variation stops skill and strength from building.
- **Staying general right up to the meet.** Specificity must increase as the competition approaches.
- **Ignoring the lifter's response.** If the plan isn't working for this lifter, change it.

## Key takeaways

- Seven principles underlie every effective program: specificity, overload, fatigue management, SRA, variation, phase potentiation, individual difference.
- Heavy training on the competition lifts is essential for maximal strength.
- Progress requires gradual increases, and adding reps or sets works as well as adding load.
- Training to failure adds fatigue without adding strength; stop most sets short of it.
- Vary exercises on purpose, every few weeks, while keeping the competition lifts constant.
- Start from sound principles, then adjust to the individual.
