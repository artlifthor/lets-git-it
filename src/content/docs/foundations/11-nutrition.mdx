## In one minute

Nutrition supports training; it does not replace it. The order of importance is clear: **total energy intake** (eating more, the same, or less than you burn) first, then **daily protein** (about 1.6 g per kg of bodyweight, up to about 2.2 g/kg), then enough **carbohydrate and fat** to train hard and stay healthy, then **hydration**, and only then **timing** and **supplements**. Strength can be kept, and often still built, while losing weight slowly, but gaining muscle is much harder in a deficit. Of the hundreds of supplements sold, only **creatine** has strong evidence for strength, with **caffeine** a well-supported second (clearer for bench press than for lower-body lifts). Because IPF powerlifting is drug tested, every supplement is also a doping risk and must be checked before use.

## What matters most

<Evidence level="practice" /> Coaches rank nutrition priorities like a pyramid. Fix the bottom before worrying about the top.

| Priority | What it is | Why it matters for a powerlifter |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Energy balance** | Total calories in vs. out | Decides whether bodyweight goes up, stays, or goes down |
| **2. Protein** | Grams per day | Builds and keeps muscle |
| **3. Carbohydrate and fat** | The rest of the calories | Fuel for hard sessions; health |
| **4. Hydration** | Fluid and salt | Dehydration costs strength |
| **5. Timing** | When you eat around training | Small extra gains once 1–4 are in place |
| **6. Supplements** | Creatine, caffeine | Small, real effects; doping risk |

:::tip[In plain words]
Get the basics right first: eat the right amount for your goal and eat enough protein. Timing and supplements are the last few percent, not the foundation.
:::

## 1. Energy balance

**Energy balance** is calories eaten minus calories burned. It decides what bodyweight does over weeks.

| State | Bodyweight | Effect on strength and muscle | When to use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Surplus** (eating more than you burn) | Rises | Best conditions for building muscle; some fat gain is normal | Off-season, moving up a class, lifters who need more muscle |
| **Maintenance** | Stable | Strength keeps rising in most lifters; muscle gain slower | Most of the year for lifters happy with their class |
| **Deficit** (eating less than you burn) | Falls | Strength can be kept or still improve; muscle gain is impaired | Moving down a class, reducing body fat, slowly and well before a meet |

**What the evidence says**

- **Deficits hurt muscle gain more than strength.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of resistance-training studies lasting at least 3 weeks found that an energy deficit impaired gains in lean mass, but strength gains were not clearly different from training without a deficit. In its meta-regression, a deficit of about 500 kcal per day was enough to prevent lean-mass gains (Murphy & Koehler, 2022).
- **Lose weight slowly.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 24 elite athletes who did 4 strength sessions a week while dieting, the group targeting about 0.7% of bodyweight loss per week gained lean mass, while the group targeting 1.4% per week (achieved about 1.0%) did not (Garthe et al., 2011).
- **Gain weight slowly too.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 39 elite athletes over 8–12 weeks, a planned surplus (about 3,600 kcal per day) increased bodyweight more than eating freely, but the extra gain was mainly fat: fat mass rose more, lean-mass gain was not different between groups, and 1RM strength improved in both (Garthe et al., 2013).
- **The ideal surplus is unknown.** <Evidence level="practice" /> A review concluded that the exact surplus needed for the best muscle gain has never been worked out in lifters (Slater et al., 2019). Common recommendations are a surplus of about 10–20% above maintenance, aiming for about **0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per week**, with more advanced lifters at the lower end (Iraki et al., 2019). For dieting, about **0.5–1% of bodyweight per week** is a common target (Helms et al., 2014); the slower end protects muscle and strength better.

**How to find maintenance in practice.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Formulas only give a rough guess. A better method: weigh yourself in the morning, 3–7 times per week, and track the **weekly average**. Log what you normally eat for 2–3 weeks. If the weekly average is stable, that intake is your maintenance. Adjust by about 200–300 kcal per day and watch the trend for another 2 weeks.

| Goal | Weekly bodyweight change (83 kg lifter) | Adjust calories when… |
|---|---|---|
| Gain | +0.2 to +0.4 kg | Weight is flat for 2+ weeks (add ~200 kcal), or rising faster than ~0.5 kg/week (remove ~200 kcal) |
| Maintain | Within ±0.3 kg | Trend drifts more than ~1 kg over a month |
| Lose | −0.4 to −0.8 kg | Weight is flat for 2+ weeks (remove ~200 kcal), or strength drops session after session |

Changing weight class and **making weight** for a meet (water loading, acute cuts) are covered in Part 4, Chapter 4: Making weight. Do not confuse a slow diet over months with a cut in the final days.

:::tip[In plain words]
If you eat more than you burn, you gain weight; less, you lose it. You can usually keep your strength while losing weight slowly, but it's hard to build muscle while dieting. Weigh yourself often, look at the weekly average, and change your food by small amounts.
:::

## 2. Protein

Protein supplies the building blocks for muscle. For lifters, the main question is **how much per day**; how it is spread out is a smaller, secondary question.

**How much**

- <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 49 studies (1,863 participants) found that extra protein during resistance training increased gains in strength and muscle. Gains in fat-free mass levelled off at a total intake of about **1.62 g/kg/day**. The effect was larger in resistance-trained people and smaller with increasing age (Morton et al., 2018). <Evidence level="practice" /> Many coaches use **1.6–2.2 g/kg** as the working range: 1.6 g/kg covers most lifters, and the upper end adds a margin for individual differences.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A later meta-analysis of 74 randomised trials found that more protein gave small extra gains in lean mass and lower-body strength in people doing resistance training (the authors rated the strength evidence as low certainty); in adults under 65, the benefit appeared at intakes of **1.6 g/kg/day or more** (Nunes et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> The ISSN position stand recommends **1.4–2.0 g/kg/day** for most exercising people, and notes that **2.3–3.1 g/kg/day** may help keep lean mass in resistance-trained lifters who are dieting (Jäger et al., 2017).

**How to spread it out**

- <Evidence level="practice" /> The ISSN recommends about **0.25 g/kg** of high-quality protein per meal, or roughly **20–40 g**, spread every 3–4 hours (Jäger et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 24 trained men, 80 g of whey protein taken as 4 × 20 g every 3 hours after training raised muscle protein synthesis over 12 hours more than 8 × 10 g or 2 × 40 g (Areta et al., 2013). This measured a short-term response, not muscle growth over months.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> A tracer study found that 100 g of protein after exercise produced a larger and longer (more than 12 hours) muscle-building response than 25 g (Trommelen et al., 2023). The common claim that "the body can only use 30 g per meal" is not supported. A large meal is not wasted; it just works more slowly.

<Evidence level="practice" /> **Practical rule:** reach the daily total first, then split it into 3–5 meals of roughly 0.3–0.5 g/kg each. Whole foods are the default; protein powder is simply a convenient food.

**Daily protein targets by bodyweight**

| Bodyweight | Minimum useful (1.6 g/kg) | Upper useful (2.2 g/kg) | Dieting (2.3–2.6 g/kg) | Per meal, 4 meals (~0.4 g/kg) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 kg | 80 g | 110 g | 115–130 g | ~20 g |
| 60 kg | 96 g | 132 g | 138–156 g | ~24 g |
| 70 kg | 112 g | 154 g | 161–182 g | ~28 g |
| 83 kg | 133 g | 183 g | 191–216 g | ~33 g |
| 93 kg | 149 g | 205 g | 214–242 g | ~37 g |
| 105 kg | 168 g | 231 g | 242–273 g | ~42 g |
| 120 kg | 192 g | 264 g | 276–312 g | ~48 g |

<Evidence level="practice" /> For lifters carrying a lot of body fat (common in the heaviest classes), base the target on a reasonable goal weight instead of current weight; otherwise the numbers become unrealistically high.

**Protein in common foods** (approximate values from standard food-composition tables; brands and portions vary. For local foods, the Philippine Food Composition Tables from the Food and Nutrition Research Institute, FNRI, are a useful reference.)

| Food | Portion | Protein |
|---|---|---|
| Chicken breast, cooked, skinless | 100 g | ~30 g |
| Chicken thigh, cooked, skinless | 100 g | ~25 g |

| Canned tuna, drained | 1 can (~120 g) | ~25–28 g |

:::tip[In plain words]
Eat about 1.6 to 2.2 grams of protein for every kilo you weigh, every day. For an 83 kg lifter that's roughly 135–180 g. Split it over 3–5 meals with a solid protein food in each, like chicken, fish, eggs, or monggo. A big protein meal is not wasted.
:::

## 3. Carbohydrate and fat

Once calories and protein are set, carbohydrate and fat fill the rest.

**Carbohydrate**

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 49 studies found that, when lifters had eaten beforehand, carbohydrate intake **did not usually change strength-training performance** in sessions of up to about 10 sets per muscle group. Benefits appeared mainly against fasted conditions and in higher-volume sessions. Longer-term strength gains were not affected by carbohydrate intake in 15 of 17 studies (Henselmans et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Powerlifting sessions are often long and high in volume, especially in accumulation blocks. A common range is **3–5 g/kg/day or more** (Iraki et al., 2019), higher on hard training days and when gaining. Rice-based diets meet this easily: one cup of cooked rice has about 45 g of carbohydrate.

**Fat**

- <Evidence level="practice" /> Recommendations for lifters are about **0.5–1.5 g/kg/day** when gaining (Iraki et al., 2019), or about **15–30% of calories** while dieting (Helms et al., 2014). Very low fat intakes for long periods make the diet harder to stick to and can crowd out nutrient-rich foods; most lifters do well near the middle of these ranges.

**Example daily intakes**

These assume maintenance has been measured as shown in section 1. Numbers are rounded.

| Lifter and goal | Calories | Protein | Fat | Carbohydrate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 83 kg man, gaining (maintenance ~2,900 kcal) | ~3,300 | ~160–185 g (1.9–2.2 g/kg) | ~80 g (~1.0 g/kg) | ~450 g (~5.4 g/kg) |
| 83 kg man, maintaining | ~2,900 | ~160–180 g (1.9–2.2 g/kg) | ~70 g (~0.85 g/kg) | ~380 g (~4.6 g/kg) |
| 83 kg man, dieting (−500 kcal) | ~2,400 | ~190 g (2.3 g/kg) | ~65 g (~25% of kcal) | ~265 g (~3.2 g/kg) |
| 63 kg woman, maintaining (maintenance ~2,200 kcal) | ~2,200 | ~120–135 g (1.9–2.1 g/kg) | ~60 g (~0.95 g/kg) | ~300 g (~4.8 g/kg) |

**Quick conversions:** protein and carbohydrate have about 4 kcal per gram; fat has about 9 kcal per gram.

:::tip[In plain words]
After protein, the rest of your food can come from carbs and fats in whatever balance suits you. Carbs help most when sessions are long and hard, so rice, bread, fruit and root crops are your friends on heavy days. Don't cut fat very low for long.
:::

## 4. Hydration, including training in the heat

Losing body water reduces strength. In a hot, humid gym this happens faster than lifters expect.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 28 studies found that hypohydration (being under-hydrated) reduced **muscle strength by about 5.5%**, muscle endurance by about 8%, and anaerobic power by about 6%. The losses tended to be smaller in trained people and were smaller when water was lost passively, rather than by exercise (Savoie et al., 2015).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> The ACSM position stand advises starting exercise well hydrated, drinking with meals and in the hours before training, and drinking during exercise to avoid losing **more than 2% of bodyweight** as water. Sweat rates vary a lot between people, so it recommends individual plans based on **weighing before and after exercise**. Drinks with electrolytes and carbohydrate can help more than water alone in some situations, for example long sessions with heavy sweating (Sawka et al., 2007).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> An international consensus on exercise in the heat found that the most effective step is **heat acclimatisation**: repeated training in the heat over 1–2 weeks. It also advises starting training hydrated, limiting dehydration during exercise, and using cooling where possible (Racinais et al., 2015). This evidence comes mainly from endurance sports; how much heat itself affects short, heavy lifting sets is less studied.

**Training in hot, humid climates such as the Philippines.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Many gyms are not air-conditioned, and humid air lets sweat evaporate less well, so the body cools less effectively and sweats more. Practical steps:

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Daily habit | Drink regularly through the day; pale-yellow urine is a simple check |
| 2–4 hours before training | Drink with your pre-training meal (about 400–600 mL is a common amount) |
| During training | Sip water between sets; in long, sweaty sessions use a drink with some salt and carbohydrate |
| After training | Replace what was lost: weigh before and after; each 1 kg lost is about 1 L of fluid. Drink a bit more than that over the next few hours, with a salty meal |
| New to a hot gym, or after time in air-conditioning | Expect 1–2 weeks to adapt; keep early sessions slightly easier |
| Signs of heat illness (confusion, dizziness, vomiting, collapse) | Stop training, cool down, and get medical help |

**Sweat-rate check (once, in a typical session):** weigh in minimal clothing before training, note how much you drink, weigh again after (towel dry). Sweat loss ≈ weight lost + fluid drunk. A lifter who loses 1.2 kg while drinking 0.5 L sweated about 1.7 L, so drinking more during that session makes sense.

:::tip[In plain words]
Being even a little dehydrated makes you weaker. In a hot, sticky gym you sweat a lot, so drink through the day, sip water between sets, and weigh yourself before and after a session once to see how much you lose. Losing more than about 2% of your bodyweight in a session is a sign you need to drink more.
:::

## 5. Nutrient timing around training

**Timing matters much less than total daily intake.** It is fine-tuning, not the foundation.

- <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis found that, once total protein intake was taken into account, eating protein close to the training session did **not** improve strength or muscle growth. Total protein intake was the strongest predictor of muscle gain (Schoenfeld et al., 2013).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> The ISSN position stand on nutrient timing ranks **meeting daily protein needs**, spread across the day, as the main priority. Protein from immediately after to 2 hours after training strongly stimulates muscle building, and pre- or post-training meals with protein (with or without carbohydrate) can support strength and body composition. The size and timing of the pre-training meal may affect how much the post-training meal matters (Kerksick et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> The muscle-building effect of a training session lasts **at least 24 hours** (Jäger et al., 2017). There is no 30-minute "anabolic window" that closes.

**What to do around a session**

| When | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **2–4 h before** | Normal meal: protein (0.3–0.5 g/kg) + carbohydrate (1–2 g/kg) + some vegetables | Fuel and amino acids available during training |
| **30–90 min before** (if the last meal was long ago) | Light, easy-to-digest carbohydrate ± some protein: banana, bread, oats with milk | Avoid training fasted; Henselmans et al. (2022) found carbohydrate helped mainly against fasted conditions |
| **During** | Water. In very long or high-volume sessions (roughly 90+ min), or in the heat, a carbohydrate-electrolyte drink is optional | Helps mainly in higher-volume work and heavy sweating |
| **0–2 h after** | Meal with protein (0.3–0.5 g/kg) + carbohydrate | Strong muscle-building response; refills glycogen for the next session |
| **Before sleep** (optional) | ~30–40 g of slow protein, such as milk or casein | ISSN notes it raises overnight muscle protein synthesis (Jäger et al., 2017); useful if daily protein is short |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Training twice in one day, or two hard sessions less than about 8 hours apart, is the main case where carbohydrate timing matters more: eat carbohydrate soon after the first session.

**Myths vs. evidence**

| Myth | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| "You must have a shake within 30 minutes of training." | Daily total matters most; the effect of a session lasts 24+ hours (Schoenfeld et al., 2013; Jäger et al., 2017) |
| "The body can only absorb 30 g of protein per meal." | 100 g gave a larger and longer response than 25 g (Trommelen et al., 2023) |
| "You need 6 small meals a day." | Meal frequency has shown limited effect on weight loss and body composition (Kerksick et al., 2017); 3–5 meals is plenty |
| "Carbs at night make you fat." | <Evidence level="practice" /> Weight change follows total daily energy balance, not the clock. Rice at dinner is fine |
| "Training fasted is better." | For strength, eating beforehand is the safer default; fasted sessions are where carbohydrate showed benefits (Henselmans et al., 2022) |

:::tip[In plain words]
Eat a normal meal a few hours before training and another one within a couple of hours after. Both should contain protein and carbs. There's no magic 30-minute window: hitting your daily totals matters much more than the exact timing.
:::

## 6. Supplements

<Evidence level="practice" /> The IOC consensus statement concluded that only a few performance supplements have good evidence (it named caffeine, creatine, specific buffering agents, and nitrate), that responses vary between individuals, and that some supplements can harm health or cause an anti-doping rule violation (Maughan et al., 2018). For powerlifting, two stand out.

### Creatine monohydrate

- **It works.** <Evidence level="strong" /> Meta-analyses of randomised, placebo-controlled trials found that creatine improved **upper-body strength** (53 studies, including bench press) and **lower-body strength** (60 studies, including squat and leg press) in efforts lasting under 3 minutes, independent of population characteristics, training protocols, and dose (Lanhers et al., 2015; Lanhers et al., 2017).
- **It is safe in healthy people.** <Evidence level="practice" /> The ISSN position stand reports that short- and long-term use (up to 30 g/day for 5 years) has been safe and well tolerated in healthy people (Kreider et al., 2017).
- **Dose.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> **3–5 g per day** (or about 0.1 g/kg) of creatine monohydrate is the recommended dose (Antonio et al., 2021). <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 31 men, 20 g/day for 6 days raised muscle creatine by about 20%; 3 g/day reached the same level in about 28 days (Hultman et al., 1996). Loading is optional: it is faster, not better.
- **Timing.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Any time of day, every day, including rest days. Take it with a meal if it upsets your stomach.
- **Heat.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review found no evidence that creatine impairs heat tolerance or hydration in athletes exercising in the heat (Lopez et al., 2009). It is suitable for hot climates.
- **Body weight.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Creatine draws water into muscle and commonly adds a little body weight in the first weeks. Lifters close to a weight-class limit should start it well before a meet and not stop or start it during the final weeks.

### Caffeine

- **It works, modestly.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis found caffeine improved muscle strength (small effect) and power; the effect was clear for **upper-body** strength but not statistically clear for lower-body strength (Grgic et al., 2018). An umbrella review of 21 meta-analyses found moderate-quality evidence that caffeine improves muscle strength, muscle endurance and power (Grgic et al., 2020).
- **Dose and timing.** <Evidence level="practice" /> The ISSN position stand reports consistent benefits from **3–6 mg/kg**, usually taken **about 60 minutes before** exercise; the minimum effective dose may be as low as 2 mg/kg, and very high doses (around 9 mg/kg) cause frequent side effects without extra benefit. Individual responses vary, partly because of genetics and habitual use (Guest et al., 2021).
- **Sleep cost.** <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 24 studies found caffeine reduced total sleep time by about 45 minutes. To avoid that, it estimated coffee (about 107 mg per 250 mL) should be drunk at least **8.8 hours** before bed, and a standard pre-workout dose (about 217.5 mg) at least **13.2 hours** before bed (Gardiner et al., 2023). Lifters who train in the evening should use less caffeine, or none, on training days, because poor sleep costs more than caffeine gives.

| Bodyweight | Low dose (3 mg/kg) | Upper dose (6 mg/kg) |
|---|---|---|
| 52 kg | ~155 mg | ~310 mg |
| 63 kg | ~190 mg | ~380 mg |
| 83 kg | ~250 mg | ~500 mg |
| 105 kg | ~315 mg | ~630 mg |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Start at the low dose, test it in training (never for the first time at a meet), and save the higher doses for heavy days or competition. Caffeine content of brewed coffee and energy drinks varies widely; tablets give a known dose. Meet-day caffeine use is covered in Part 4, Chapter 6: Meet day.

### Protein powder

<Evidence level="practice" /> Protein powder is a convenient way to reach the daily protein target, not a requirement; whole foods work just as well (Jäger et al., 2017).

### Probably not worth it for powerlifters

| Supplement | Why it's low priority |
|---|---|
| **BCAAs** | <Evidence level="practice" /> If daily protein is adequate, complete proteins already supply them, together with the other essential amino acids that muscle needs |
| **Beta-alanine** | <Evidence level="moderate" /> Benefits are mainly in efforts lasting about 0.5–10 minutes (Saunders et al., 2017); a heavy set of 1–5 reps is much shorter |
| **Citrulline** | <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis found only a small effect on strength and power, and no single study's result was clearly different from zero (Trexler et al., 2019) |
| **"Testosterone boosters"** | <Evidence level="emerging" /> In a review of 50 products, 90% claimed to raise testosterone, but only 24.8% had published data showing an increase, and 10.1% contained ingredients with data suggesting a decrease (Clemesha et al., 2020) |
| **"Fat burners", "pre-workouts" with long ingredient lists** | <Evidence level="practice" /> Little proven benefit beyond their caffeine, unclear doses, and a higher risk of stimulants that are prohibited in competition |

### Anti-doping: every supplement is a risk

IPF powerlifting is drug tested and follows the World Anti-Doping Code (see [Chapter 1](/lets-git-it/foundations/01-what-is-powerlifting/)).

- **Strict liability.** Under the IPF anti-doping rules, a lifter is responsible for any prohibited substance found in their sample, whether or not they meant to take it. "It was in my supplement" is not a defence (IPF, Supplements).
- **Contamination is real.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In an international study, 94 of 634 supplements (14.8%) bought in 13 countries contained anabolic steroids that were not on the label (Geyer et al., 2004). <Evidence level="moderate" /> A later review of 23 analytical studies reported contamination rates between 12% and 58% of supplements tested, with prohormones and stimulants among the substances found (Martínez-Sanz et al., 2017).
- **Caffeine is allowed; some "energy" products are not.** Caffeine itself is not on the WADA Prohibited List, but products sold for energy can contain stimulants that are.

**Before using any supplement:**

1. Ask whether you need it at all. Food first.
2. Check every ingredient and every medication against the current **WADA Prohibited List** (updated each year).
3. Prefer products that are **third-party tested for banned substances**. The IPF refers athletes to the NSF Certified for Sport programme. Testing lowers the risk; it does not remove it.
4. Avoid products with "proprietary blends", hormone or "anabolic" claims, or stimulants you do not recognise.
5. Keep the container and batch number. For questions, ask PAP or check the IPF anti-doping pages.

:::tip[In plain words]
Creatine is the one supplement with strong proof that it helps strength (3–5 g every day). Caffeine also helps, especially for bench press (about 3 mg per kg, an hour before training, and not too late in the day). Most other products are a waste of money. Any supplement can be contaminated with a banned substance, and you are responsible if you test positive, so check before you take anything.
:::

## Sample programs

Three example **days**, not prescriptions. Food values are approximate (rounded from standard food-composition tables); real portions, brands and recipes vary by about ±10–15%. Use them as templates and adjust portions to your own targets.

### Program 1: Gaining phase, training day (83 kg man, ~3,300 kcal)

Target: about 0.25–0.5% bodyweight gain per week. Training at 5:00 pm.

| Time | Meal | Protein | Carbs | Fat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7:00 am | 3 eggs (cooked with ½ tbsp oil), 2 cups rice, 1 banana | 27 g | 119 g | 23 g |
| 12:00 pm | 150 g chicken breast, 2 cups rice, vegetables (e.g. pechay) cooked with 1 tbsp oil | 57 g | 95 g | 20 g |
| 3:00 pm | 80 g oats cooked in 250 mL low-fat milk, 1 banana | 19 g | 93 g | 8 g |
| 5:00–7:00 pm | Training: water; creatine 5 g with any meal | — | — | — |
| 7:30 pm | 200 g fish (e.g. tilapia), 2 cups rice, 1 cup ginisang monggo, vegetables, 1 tbsp oil | 76 g | 134 g | 22 g |
| 9:30 pm | 250 mL whole milk | 8 g | 12 g | 8 g |
| **Total** | **~3,290 kcal** | **~187 g (2.2 g/kg)** | **~453 g (5.5 g/kg)** | **~81 g (1.0 g/kg)** |

**Why it's built this way:** protein in every meal, the biggest carbohydrate meals before and after training, and calories raised mostly through rice, which is cheap, easy to eat in quantity, and familiar. If weight rises too slowly, add half a cup of rice to two meals (~200 kcal).

### Program 2: Maintenance, training day (83 kg man, ~2,900 kcal)

Training at 5:00 pm after work, so caffeine is kept moderate and early.

| Time | Meal | Protein | Carbs | Fat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7:00 am | 80 g oats in 250 mL low-fat milk, 1 banana, 1 tbsp peanut butter | 23 g | 96 g | 16 g |
| 12:00 pm | 150 g chicken thigh (e.g. adobo, skin removed), 2 cups rice, vegetables, ½ tbsp oil | 48 g | 95 g | 20 g |
| 3:30 pm | Tuna sandwich (1 can tuna, 2 slices bread), 1 banana | 34 g | 53 g | 3 g |
| 4:00 pm | Caffeine ~150–200 mg (about 2–2.5 mg/kg), e.g. one tablet or a measured coffee | — | — | — |
| 5:00–6:45 pm | Training: water; electrolyte drink if the gym is hot and you are sweating heavily | — | — | — |
| 7:30 pm | 150 g fish, 2 cups rice, 1 cup monggo, vegetables, 1 tbsp oil | 63 g | 134 g | 21 g |
| 9:30 pm | 250 mL whole milk; creatine 5 g | 8 g | 12 g | 8 g |
| **Total** | **~2,880 kcal** | **~176 g (2.1 g/kg)** | **~390 g (4.7 g/kg)** | **~68 g (0.8 g/kg)** |

**Why it's built this way:** the 3:30 pm snack prevents training on an empty stomach after a long workday. Caffeine is kept near the low end of the useful range (Guest et al., 2021) because 4:00 pm is only about 7 hours before an 11:00 pm bedtime, shorter than the roughly 9–13 hours suggested to avoid lost sleep (Gardiner et al., 2023). If sleep suffers, drop it on weekday sessions.

### Program 3: Filipino home-food day, training day (63 kg woman, ~2,200 kcal)

Built from common local foods. Training at 4:00 pm.

| Time | Meal | Protein | Carbs | Fat |
|---|---|---|---|---|

**Why it's built this way:** it uses ordinary home cooking, with no special "diet" foods. Protein comes from eggs, chicken, fish and monggo; rice and pandesal supply carbohydrate around training. To reduce calories, cut rice first (each cup is about 200 kcal); to raise protein, add an egg or a little more fish.

## Common mistakes

- **Chasing supplements before the basics.** Calories and protein matter far more than any product.
- **Dieting hard during a heavy block.** Large deficits impair muscle gain and make heavy training harder. Diet slowly, and preferably when training stress is lower.
- **Not eating enough protein in a rice-heavy diet.** Rice and bread fill you up but provide little protein; plan a protein food for every meal.
- **Training fasted or after a long gap since the last meal.** A light carbohydrate snack 30–90 minutes before training is an easy fix.
- **Underestimating sweat loss in a hot gym.** Weigh before and after a session at least once; keep water on the platform.
- **Using caffeine late in the day.** A stronger evening session is not worth a shorter night's sleep.
- **Taking untested supplements.** Contamination is common, and the lifter is responsible for anything in their sample.
- **Confusing a slow diet with making weight.** Acute weight-cutting methods belong in the days before a meet and are covered in Part 4.

## Key takeaways

- Energy balance comes first: gain at about 0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per week, diet at about 0.5–1% per week, and judge by the weekly average weight.
- Strength can be kept in a moderate deficit, but muscle gain is impaired; avoid large deficits.
- Eat about 1.6–2.2 g/kg of protein per day, split into 3–5 meals.
- Carbohydrate helps most in long, high-volume sessions; 3–5 g/kg/day suits most powerlifters. Keep fat at moderate levels.
- Being under-hydrated costs strength (about 5% on average); in hot, humid gyms, try to keep sweat losses during a session under about 2% of bodyweight, drink through the day, and weigh before and after training to learn your sweat losses.
- Timing is fine-tuning: eat protein and carbohydrate within a few hours before and after training. There is no 30-minute window.
- Creatine (3–5 g/day) has strong evidence for strength; caffeine (3–6 mg/kg, not too close to bedtime) has moderate evidence, clearest for upper-body strength.
- Every supplement carries a doping risk under strict liability; use third-party-tested products and check the WADA Prohibited List.
