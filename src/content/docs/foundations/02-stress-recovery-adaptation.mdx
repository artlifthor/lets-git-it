## In one minute

Training does not make a lifter stronger during the session; it creates **stress** and **fatigue**, and the lifter adapts during **recovery** afterwards. This cycle of stimulus, recovery and adaptation (**SRA**) sets how often each lift can be trained hard. The **fitness–fatigue model** explains why performance can drop during hard training and rise once fatigue clears, which is the basis for deloads and peaking. How long recovery takes depends on the lift, the load, how close sets go to failure, and the lifter's sleep, food and life stress. A good program lands the next hard session after recovery, not before it and not long after.

## Prerequisites

None. Read [Chapter 1: What powerlifting is](/lets-git-it/foundations/01-what-is-powerlifting/) first if you are new to the sport.

## Stress and adaptation

A hard training session is a **stressor**. It temporarily lowers performance (the lifter is weaker for a while afterwards) and triggers repair and adaptation: more muscle, better coordination of the movement, and stronger tendons and connective tissue over the long term. The idea that the body adapts to a stress it survives comes from general stress theory and is the foundation of every training model in this manual.

:::tip[In plain words]
Lifting doesn't make you stronger in the gym. It tires you out and gives your body a reason to change. You get stronger while you rest, eat and sleep afterwards.
:::

## The SRA curve

**Stimulus → Recovery → Adaptation.** After a session, performance dips, recovers to baseline, and may rise slightly above it before slowly fading if no new stimulus comes.

- **Stimulus:** the training session. Bigger stimulus (more hard sets, heavier loads, closer to failure) means a deeper dip and a longer recovery.
- **Recovery:** fatigue clears and damage is repaired.
- **Adaptation:** the lifter ends up slightly better than before.
- **Involution:** if nothing new happens, the gain slowly fades.

Some processes behind the curve have been measured directly:

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In one small study (6 men), muscle-building activity (muscle protein synthesis) was more than doubled 24 hours after heavy arm training and had almost returned to baseline by 36 hours (MacDougall et al., 1995).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 10 young men starting a training program, much of the early muscle-building activity in the first weeks of training went to repairing muscle damage; it turned into measurable growth once the muscle adapted to the damage (Damas et al., 2016). Unfamiliar training costs more recovery.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In one study of squat and bench press training, sets taken to failure slowed recovery of strength, speed and fatigue markers to 24–48 hours or more, compared with the same total work done short of failure (Morán-Navarro et al., 2017).

<Evidence level="practice" /> Coaches use the SRA idea to set frequency: train each lift again **roughly when it has recovered from the last hard session**. The curve is a simplification, but it is a useful planning tool (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith).

:::tip[In plain words]
After a hard workout you're weaker for a day or two, then you bounce back a little stronger. Train the same lift again once you've bounced back. Too soon and you're still tired; too late and some of the gain has faded.
:::

## The fitness–fatigue model

The fitness–fatigue model treats every session as producing two after-effects at once:

- **Fitness:** the positive adaptation. It builds slowly and lasts a long time.
- **Fatigue:** the negative after-effect. It builds quickly and also clears relatively quickly.

**Performance on any day ≈ fitness − fatigue.**

<Evidence level="practice" /> This explains three things coaches see every day (Chiu & Barnes, 2003):

1. During hard blocks, lifters often perform worse even though they are getting fitter: fatigue is hiding fitness.
2. A short period of easier training (a deload or a taper) lets fatigue drop faster than fitness, so performance rises.
3. Too long an easy period and fitness starts to fade too.

The model is a simplified description, not a precise calculator. Treat it as a way of thinking, not a formula.

:::tip[In plain words]
Hard training makes you fitter and more tired at the same time. Tiredness can hide how strong you really are. Ease off for a short while and the tiredness goes away faster than the strength does, so you lift your best.
:::

## Supercompensation

**Supercompensation** is the name for the short period when performance rises above where it started. <Evidence level="practice" /> It is a useful teaching picture, but real recovery is messier: different qualities (muscle, nervous system, connective tissue) recover at different speeds, so there is rarely one clean "peak day" after every session. In practice, lifters train again before full supercompensation for some qualities and rely on adaptation that builds across many sessions.

:::tip[In plain words]
There's a short window after recovery when you're a little stronger than before. It's real, but it isn't one exact day. Your body bounces back in different ways at different speeds.
:::

## What controls recovery time

<Evidence level="practice" /> These rules of thumb come from coaching practice and the SRA reasoning above (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith; Sheiko):

| Factor | Slower recovery | Faster recovery |
|---|---|---|
| **Lift** | Heavy deadlifts, heavy squats | Bench press, upper-body accessories |
| **Load** | Near-maximal singles and doubles | Moderate loads (around 70–80%) |
| **Proximity to failure** | Sets to failure | Sets with 2–3 reps in reserve |
| **Volume** | Many hard sets | Fewer hard sets |
| **Familiarity** | New exercises, new rep ranges | Movements the lifter does all the time |
| **The lifter** | Bigger, stronger, older lifters | Smaller, lighter, younger lifters |
| **Life** | Poor sleep, low food intake, high stress | Good sleep, enough food, low stress |

This is why bench press is commonly trained 3–4 times a week, squat 2–3 times, and heavy deadlifts 1–2 times.

:::tip[In plain words]
Heavy deadlifts and heavy squats take longer to bounce back from than bench press. Going all-out to failure, doing lots of sets, or trying something new also takes longer. Bad sleep, too little food and stress at work or home slow everything down.
:::

## Deloads

A **deload** is a planned easier week (or few days) to let fatigue clear.

<Evidence level="practice" /> In a survey of strength and physique athletes, deloads lasted about **6–7 days** and came every **5–6 weeks** on average. Athletes cut sets and reps, used lighter loads, and stopped sets further from failure, but usually kept the same training days and exercises (Rogerson et al., 2024). This is a survey of what athletes do, not a test of what works best.

Deload either on a **fixed schedule** (for example every 4–6 weeks) or **when signs of accumulated fatigue appear**:

| Sign | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Performance dropping | Same weights feel 1+ RPE harder for 2 or more sessions in a row |
| Bar speed down | Warm-ups look and feel slow |
| Persistent soreness or joint aches | Not clearing between sessions |
| Motivation down | Lifter dreading sessions, poor focus |
| Sleep disturbed | Trouble sleeping despite being tired |

:::tip[In plain words]
A deload is an easy week on purpose. You still go to the gym and do the same lifts, just lighter and less. It lets the tiredness wear off so you come back stronger.
:::

## Sample programs

All examples are for an intermediate lifter with a squat of 180 kg, a bench press of 120 kg and a deadlift of 210 kg. Loads are approximate RPE equivalents from the chart in [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg. Let RPE, not the kilograms, decide the final load.

### Program 1: 3 days per week, built around recovery time

Bench recovers fastest, so it is trained every session; squat twice; heavy deadlift once.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) |
| Mon | Bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) |
| Mon | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| Wed | Bench press | 5 × 4 | RPE 7.5 (~100 kg) |
| Wed | Deadlift | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 (~170 kg) |
| Wed | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| Fri | Squat | 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~155 kg) |
| Fri | Bench press | 3 × 5 | RPE 7.5 (~95 kg) |
| Fri | Back extension | 3 × 12 | RPE 8 |

**Why it works:** about 48 hours between squat sessions, 4 to 7 days between hard deadlift sessions, and bench never trained to failure, so it recovers in time for the next session.

### Program 2: 4 days per week, more frequency for squat and bench

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 4 | RPE 7.5 (~147.5 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 3 × 3 | RPE 7.5 (~177.5 kg) |
| 2 | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Squat | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press | 5 × 3 | RPE 8 (~102.5 kg) |
| 4 | Bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) |
| 4 | Deficit deadlift (5 cm) | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Pause squat (2 s) | 2 × 5 | RPE 6 |

**Why it works:** the heavy squat day (day 1) is separated from the heavy deadlift day (day 2) by type of lift, and from the second squat day by at least 48 hours. Day 4 keeps lower-body work light so the lifter starts the next week recovered.

### Program 3: Deload week (from Program 2)

Same days and exercises; roughly half the sets, about 10% lighter than the previous week, and stopping at RPE 6 or below.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load (~90% of last week) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 2 × 4 | ~132.5 kg |
| 1 | Bench press | 2 × 5 | ~85 kg |
| 2 | Deadlift | 2 × 3 | ~160 kg |
| 2 | Close-grip bench press | 2 × 8 | RPE ≤6 |
| 3 | Squat | 2 × 5 | ~127.5 kg |
| 3 | Bench press | 3 × 3 | ~92.5 kg |
| 4 | Bench press | 2 × 6 | ~82.5 kg |
| 4 | Deficit deadlift (5 cm) | 2 × 4 | RPE ≤6 |

## Common mistakes

- **Training the same lift hard again before it has recovered,** especially heavy deadlifts and squats on back-to-back days.
- **Taking many sets to failure.** It slows recovery for a day or two longer, without clear extra strength gains (see Chapter 3).
- **Judging a training block by how the lifter performs during it.** Fatigue hides fitness; judge progress after a deload or test.
- **Never deloading, or deloading by stopping training completely.** Reduce volume and intensity, but keep training.
- **Ignoring life outside the gym.** Exams, work stress or poor sleep reduce recovery; adjust training before performance collapses.

## Key takeaways

- Strength is built during recovery, not during the session.
- The SRA curve sets how often each lift can be trained hard: bench recovers fastest, heavy deadlifts slowest.
- Performance ≈ fitness − fatigue: fatigue can hide progress, and short easy periods reveal it.
- Training close to, but not at, failure speeds recovery.
- Plan deloads every few weeks or when fatigue signs appear; keep training, just lighter and less.
