## In one minute

Progressive overload means making training a little harder over time so the body has a reason to keep adapting. There are several ways to do it: add **load**, add **reps**, use **double progression** (reps first, then load), add **sets**, raise **effort**, increase **density**, add **frequency**, or make the **exercise** harder. Research shows that adding reps works about as well as adding load over a few weeks, and that more weekly sets help strength up to a point. Which method to use depends mostly on training age: novices add load every session, intermediates progress week to week, and advanced lifters progress from block to block. Whatever the method, progress only when recovery and technique keep up.

## What overload is (and isn't)

**Overload is a demand the lifter is not yet adapted to.** Once the body adapts, the same training becomes maintenance. To keep improving, the demand has to rise.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> Overload is one of the core principles of training: without a rising demand there is nothing new to adapt to (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith). An expert position stand describes progression as the step-by-step increase of training demand needed for further adaptation, and recommends planned changes in load, reps, sets and frequency as the lifter advances (American College of Sports Medicine, 2009).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> How much stimulus is "enough" changes with experience. A meta-analysis found that untrained people gained the most strength at a mean intensity of about 60% 1RM, training each muscle 3 days per week, while trained people did best at about 80% and 2 days per week (Rhea et al., 2003). A follow-up meta-analysis in competitive athletes pointed to about 85% 1RM, 2 days per week and 8 sets per muscle group (Peterson et al., 2004). These are group averages from older studies, not prescriptions, but they show the trend: **the more trained the lifter, the more stimulus progress needs.**
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Overload is not "harder every session forever". Progression has to stay within what the lifter can recover from; otherwise fatigue grows faster than fitness (see [Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/foundations/02-stress-recovery-adaptation/)).

:::tip[In plain words]
Your body only changes when you give it something a bit harder than it's used to. Once it's used to the work, you need to ask for a little more. The stronger you get, the more it takes to keep improving.
:::

## How fast should strength go up?

Knowing the realistic rate stops coaches from pushing too fast.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In competition records of 1,896 powerlifters (2003–2018), the best yearly gains were about **20–25 kg on the squat, 18–21 kg on the deadlift and 10.5–13 kg on the bench press**. Gains in absolute and relative strength did not differ between men and women, and the strongest men gained more slowly (Latella et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> That works out to well under 1 kg per week on most lifts for a competitive lifter. Only novices can add kilograms every session, and only for a few months (Rippetoe & Baker).

| Training age | Typical rate of progress | Timescale of a "step" |
|---|---|---|
| **Novice** | A few kg per lift per session | Session to session |
| **Intermediate** | A few kg per lift per month | Week to week, or a 3–6 week block |
| **Advanced** | A few kg per lift per year | Block to block, meet to meet |

<Evidence level="practice" /> These timescales come from Rippetoe & Baker's definition of training age: a **novice** recovers from and adapts to one session before the next, an **intermediate** needs about a week of varied training to produce a new best, and an **advanced** lifter needs a month or more. The numbers in the middle column are coaching estimates, not research findings.

:::tip[In plain words]
At the start, you can add weight almost every workout. After a few months, progress takes weeks. After a few years, it takes months. Planning for the speed you are actually at keeps you from getting hurt or frustrated.
:::

## The ways to progress

### 1. Load

**Add weight to the bar while keeping sets and reps the same.** This is the most specific way to progress for a sport tested by maximum load.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> Fixed load jumps work best when the jump is small compared with the lift: 2.5 kg is 1.4% of a 180 kg squat but 4.2% of a 60 kg bench press. Small plates (0.5–1.25 kg) keep load progression going on lighter lifts and for lighter lifters (Rippetoe & Baker).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> An expert position stand suggests a load increase of about 2–10% when a lifter can complete the current load for 1–2 reps more than prescribed (American College of Sports Medicine, 2009).

:::tip[In plain words]
The simplest way to progress is to put more weight on the bar for the same sets and reps. Keep the jumps small, especially on bench press and for lighter lifters, so you don't outrun your strength.
:::

### 2. Reps

**Keep the load the same and do more reps with it.**

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 43 resistance-trained men and women over 8 weeks, adding reps at a fixed load and adding load at fixed reps produced similar muscle growth and strength. Strength gains (Smith machine squat 1RM) slightly favoured adding load, by about 2 kg, a difference of questionable practical importance (Plotkin et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 39 previously untrained young men and women, each leg trained with either load or rep progression for 10 weeks gained similar leg-extension strength and muscle size (Chaves et al., 2024).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> For powerlifters, rep progression is most useful for variations and accessories, and when the next load jump is too big. For the competition lifts, the strength test is a single rep, so most progress eventually has to show up as load.

:::tip[In plain words]
If you can't add weight yet, add a rep. Doing more reps with the same weight also makes you stronger. For the main lifts, though, you'll eventually need to turn those extra reps into more weight.
:::

### 3. Double progression

**Work within a rep range; when every set reaches the top of the range, add load and start again at the bottom.** For example, 3 × 6–8: start at 6 reps, build to 8 on all sets, then add 2.5 kg and drop back to 6.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> This combines rep and load progression and matches the position-stand guidance above: once the lifter beats the target by 1–2 reps, add load (American College of Sports Medicine, 2009). It is the standard method for accessories and for lifts where small plates are not available.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Set a ceiling on effort, for example "stop each set at RPE 9". Otherwise lifters chase reps by grinding to failure, which adds fatigue without adding strength (Grgic et al., 2022).

:::tip[In plain words]
Pick a rep range, like 6 to 8. Keep the same weight until you can do 8 on every set, then add a little weight and go back to 6. It's simple, it works, and it stops you from adding weight before you're ready.
:::

### 4. Sets (volume)

**Add sets across weeks, usually at a fixed load or RPE.** For example, a block that goes 3 → 4 → 5 sets, then a deload.

- <Evidence level="strong" /> More weekly sets produce more strength, but with diminishing returns: each added set helps less than the one before (Pelland et al., 2025; Ralston et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 35 trained lifters who could squat about twice their body mass, 12, 18 and 24 weekly lower-body sets for 8 weeks all increased squat 1RM. The middle dose (18 sets) showed a trend towards the largest squat gain; 24 sets did not do better (Aube et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Use set progression **within** a block, then reset to the lower volume after the deload. Adding sets every block, forever, eventually exceeds what the lifter can recover from (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith).

:::tip[In plain words]
You can make training harder by doing more sets. It helps, but each extra set helps a bit less, and too many just wears you out. Build up sets over a few weeks, take an easier week, then start again.
:::

### 5. Effort (RPE)

**Keep sets and reps similar but let the target RPE rise across a block**, for example RPE 7 → 7.5 → 8 → 8.5. Load rises with it.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 31 trained men over 12 weeks, squat loads set by reps in reserve gave larger front and back squat gains than loads fixed from the starting 1RM, with volume matched. The autoregulated group ended up training at slightly heavier loads, because the loads rose as the lifters got stronger (Graham & Cleather, 2021).
- <Evidence level="strong" /> Pushing effort all the way to failure gives no extra strength compared with stopping short (Grgic et al., 2022). Effort progression should end around RPE 8–9 on the competition lifts, not at failure.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Rising-RPE blocks are common in RPE-based coaching (Tuchscherer). They suit intermediate lifters who rate effort accurately (see [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/)).

:::tip[In plain words]
Another way to progress is to let each week be a little harder than the last: a bit closer to your limit. Stop before you hit your true limit, though. Going all the way to failure tires you out without making you stronger.
:::

### 6. Autoregulated load progression

**Let the lifter's performance on the day set the load.** Two common forms: a top set at a target RPE followed by back-off sets (see [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/)), and "reps-to-target" systems where a final all-out set decides next week's load.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 23 college football players over 6 weeks, a program that adjusted load from each week's performance (autoregulatory progressive resistance exercise) improved bench press 1RM and estimated squat 1RM more than a fixed weekly increase (Mann et al., 2010).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> The advantage is that progression matches the lifter's actual rate of gain. The risk is that eager lifters inflate their effort ratings or turn every session into a test.

:::tip[In plain words]
Instead of deciding the weight weeks in advance, you let how strong you are today decide it. On good days the weight goes up more, on bad days it goes up less. It needs honest effort ratings to work.
:::

### 7. Density

**Do the same work in less time**, usually by cutting rest periods.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 21 trained men, 3-minute rests gave larger squat and bench 1RM gains than 1-minute rests over 8 weeks (Schoenfeld et al., 2016). Shorter rest lowers the load a lifter can handle on the next set.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Density progression is a poor tool for heavy competition-lift work. Use it for accessories, conditioning circuits, or when session time is the real limit.

:::tip[In plain words]
You can make training harder by resting less between sets. For heavy squats, benches and deadlifts that's a bad idea, because you'll lift less. Save it for lighter extra exercises or when you're short on time.
:::

### 8. Frequency

**Train a lift on more days per week.** This is mainly a way to fit in more volume and more practice with fresh sets.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> Add a day for a lift when its volume needs to go up but sessions are already long, or when a lift needs more technical practice. Treat it as a volume decision (see [Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/foundations/04-training-variables/)), not a separate magic ingredient.

:::tip[In plain words]
Training a lift more often lets you do more good-quality work without exhausting yourself in one session. It's mostly a smart way to add volume.
:::

### 9. Exercise difficulty and specificity

**Make the exercise itself harder or more competition-like**: longer pauses, a larger range of motion (deficit deadlift), less help from equipment, or moving from a variation back to the competition lift as a meet approaches.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> This progresses skill and specificity rather than just raw demand. It is mostly used block to block (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith; Siff).

:::tip[In plain words]
You can also progress by making the exercise trickier, like holding a longer pause, or by switching back to the exact competition lift as the meet gets closer.
:::

## Choosing a method by training age

<Evidence level="practice" /> The rules below summarise common coaching practice (Rippetoe & Baker; Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith; Tuchscherer). They are starting points, not laws.

| Stage | How you know | Main progression (competition lifts) | Also use | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Novice** | Can still add load every session with good technique | **Load**, every session, fixed small jumps | Double progression for accessories | Complex periodization; RPE-only loading |
| **Early intermediate** | Session-to-session jumps stall despite good sleep, food and a reset | **Weekly load** progression; heavy/light days | Double progression; RPE caps on top sets | Testing maxes often |
| **Intermediate** | Weekly progress stalls; needs a few weeks to show a new best | **Block-to-block**: sets rise in volume blocks, load and RPE rise in strength blocks | Autoregulated top sets and back-offs | Adding volume every block without limit |
| **Advanced** | New bests come meet to meet | Planned blocks and peaks; small, specific steps | Exercise-difficulty progression; individualised volume | Chasing weekly PRs |

**Decision rules for any lifter:**

1. **Load first, if you can.** If the bar speed and technique hold and the jump is small (under about 2–3% of the lift), add load.
2. **Jump too big? Add reps instead.** If the smallest available jump is too large for the lift, use double progression or smaller plates.
3. **Load stalled but recovering well? Add a set** for the next block.
4. **RPE climbing at the same load?** Do not add anything. Hold the load, or deload. This usually means fatigue, not lost strength.
5. **Stop effort progression at RPE 8–9** on competition lifts.
6. **Use density only for accessories** or when time is the limit.
7. **Change one progression variable at a time** for a given lift, so you can tell what worked.

:::tip[In plain words]
Beginners add weight every workout. Once that stops, add weight every week. Once that stops, plan training in blocks of a few weeks. Whatever stage you're at, if the same weight starts feeling harder, hold back instead of pushing more.
:::

## Sample programs

Intermediate lifter, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg, unless stated. Loads are approximate RPE equivalents from the chart in [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg.

### Program 1: Double progression (bench press, 6 weeks)

**Rule:** 3 sets of 6–8 reps, stop every set at RPE 9 (one rep left). When all three sets reach 8 reps, add 2.5 kg next session and work back up from 6. Shown here once per week; the lifter's other bench sessions stay as planned.

| Week | Load | Reps achieved (set 1, 2, 3) | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 92.5 kg (~77%) | 7, 6, 6 | Keep load |
| 2 | 92.5 kg | 8, 7, 6 | Keep load |
| 3 | 92.5 kg | 8, 7, 7 | Keep load |
| 4 | 92.5 kg | 8, 8, 8 | **Add 2.5 kg** |
| 5 | 95 kg | 7, 6, 6 | Keep load |
| 6 | 95 kg | 7, 7, 6 | Keep load |

**Why:** a 2.5 kg jump on a 92.5 kg bench is about 2.7%, too big to add every week at this stage. Reps carry the progress between load jumps. The RPE 9 cap keeps sets out of failure.

### Program 2: Weekly load wave (squat, two 4-week waves)

**Rule:** sets and reps stay fixed; load rises each week; week 4 is a deload; the next wave starts slightly heavier than the last one did. RPE refers to the first set; later sets will feel harder.

| Week | Squat | Approx. RPE | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 × 5 @ 140 kg | ~7 | Starting point |
| 2 | 4 × 5 @ 145 kg | ~8 | +5 kg |
| 3 | 4 × 5 @ 147.5 kg | ~8–8.5 | +2.5 kg |
| 4 | 2 × 5 @ 125 kg | ~5 | Deload |
| 5 | 4 × 5 @ 142.5 kg | ~7 | Wave 2 starts 2.5 kg above wave 1 |
| 6 | 4 × 5 @ 147.5 kg | ~8 | +5 kg |
| 7 | 4 × 5 @ 150 kg | ~8–8.5 | +2.5 kg; if the first set is above RPE 9, stay at 147.5 kg |
| 8 | 2 × 5 @ 127.5 kg | ~5 | Deload |

**Why:** this is the classic intermediate pattern, progress week to week, then shed fatigue. Starting each wave a little higher spreads progress over months instead of forcing it into every session.

### Program 3: Set progression (deadlift, 4-week block)

**Rule:** load fixed at about RPE 7 for sets of 4 (~170 kg, about 81%); one set added each week; deload in week 4. Next block, raise the load 2.5–5 kg and restart at 3 sets.

| Week | Deadlift | Weekly hard sets (deadlift) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 × 4 @ 170 kg | 3 |
| 2 | 4 × 4 @ 170 kg | 4 |
| 3 | 5 × 4 @ 170 kg | 5 |
| 4 | 2 × 4 @ 150 kg | Deload |
| 5 (next block) | 3 × 4 @ 175 kg | 3 |

**Why:** deadlifts are costly to recover from, so progressing sets at a moderate RPE adds stimulus without grinding heavy reps. If the RPE of the last set climbs past 8.5 in week 3, cap the block at 4 sets.

### Program 4: Mixing methods in one week (4 days)

Different exercises progress in different ways. Competition lifts get load; variations get sets; accessories get double progression; conditioning gets density.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load | How it progresses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~140 kg) | Weekly load wave (Program 2) |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) | +2.5 kg per week while first-set RPE stays at 8 or lower |
| 1 | Leg press | 3 × 10–12 | RPE 8 | Double progression |
| 2 | Deadlift | 3–5 × 4 | RPE 7 (~170 kg) | Set progression (Program 3) |
| 2 | Close-grip bench press | 3–5 × 6 | RPE 7 | +1 set per week, deload week 4 |
| 2 | Barbell row | 3 × 8–12 | RPE 8 | Double progression |
| 3 | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 | +2.5 kg per week |
| 3 | Bench press | 3 × 6–8 | RPE 9 cap | Double progression (Program 1) |
| 4 | Bench press | 1 × 3 @ RPE 8, then 3 × 3 | ~102.5 kg top set; back-offs about 7% lighter (~95 kg) | Autoregulated: the top set sets the back-offs |
| 4 | Back extension + farmer's carry circuit | 3 rounds | Moderate | Density: same work, rest cut from 90 s to 60 s over 4 weeks |

**Why:** one lifter, four progression methods, each matched to what the exercise is for. Only one variable changes per exercise, so the coach can see what is working.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding load every session past the novice stage.** Once session-to-session jumps fail, more forcing leads to grinding, missed reps and form breakdown.
- **Using the same 2.5 kg jump for every lift and every lifter.** On a light bench press or for a lighter lifter it is a much bigger percentage. Use smaller plates or double progression.
- **Chasing reps to failure.** Double progression needs an effort cap, or it turns into failure training.
- **Adding sets every block without end.** Volume has diminishing returns; reset after the deload.
- **Cutting rest on heavy competition-lift sets** to "progress density". It lowers the load you can use.
- **Changing several variables at once** for the same lift. When progress stalls, you won't know why.
- **Ignoring rising RPE.** The same load feeling harder week after week is a fatigue warning, not a cue to push.

## Key takeaways

- Overload means a gradually rising demand; it can come from load, reps, sets, effort, density, frequency or exercise difficulty.
- Adding reps at a fixed load works about as well as adding load over several weeks; double progression combines both.
- More weekly sets help strength with diminishing returns; use set progression within blocks, not without limit.
- Push effort towards RPE 8–9, not failure.
- Novices progress session to session, intermediates week to week or block to block, advanced lifters block to block and meet to meet.
- Hold or deload when RPE rises at the same load.
