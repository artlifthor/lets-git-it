## In one minute

A **percentage of one-rep max (%1RM)** fixes the load in advance from a known max, while **reps in reserve (RIR)** and its partner scale, **RPE**, describe effort: how many more reps the lifter could have done. Percentages are simple and objective, but the number of reps a lifter can do at a given percentage varies a lot between people and lifts. RIR/RPE adjusts to the lifter's day and to individual differences, but it depends on the lifter rating effort honestly and accurately, which takes practice. Most experienced coaches combine the two.

## Percentage of one-rep max (%1RM)

A **one-rep max (1RM)** is the heaviest load a lifter can lift once with good technique. A percentage-based program prescribes loads as a fraction of it: a 180 kg squatter told to do 5 × 5 at 75% uses 135 kg.

**Strengths.** Percentages are easy to write, easy to follow, and leave nothing to judgement during the session. They suit lifters who rate effort poorly and programs that need precise, planned progressions.

**Limits.**

- **Reps at a given percentage vary widely.** <Evidence level="strong" /> A large meta-regression of studies on maximal reps at different percentages of 1RM found big differences between individuals and between exercises at the same percentage. The same 80% might allow 5 reps for one lifter and 9 for another, or more reps on one lift than another (Nuzzo et al., 2024).
- **Training background changes the relationship.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In one small study of 16 athletes, strength-trained athletes completed fewer reps than endurance-trained athletes at 70% and 80% of 1RM, though not at 90% (Richens & Cleather, 2014). A lifter's history affects how hard a percentage really is.
- **The 1RM itself changes.** <Evidence level="practice" /> A max tested weeks ago may be out of date, and day-to-day readiness (sleep, stress, travel, a hard previous session) shifts what a lifter can do on the day. A fixed percentage can therefore feel easy one week and near-maximal the next.

:::tip[In plain words]
A percentage program says "lift 80% of your best". It's simple and clear. But 80% feels different for different people and on different days: for one lifter it's 5 reps, for another it's 9, and it's harder on a day you slept badly.
:::

## Reps in reserve (RIR) and RPE

**Reps in reserve (RIR)** is the number of extra reps a lifter could have completed with good technique when they finished the set. The **RIR-based RPE scale** (rating of perceived exertion) puts this on a 1–10 scale used widely in powerlifting (Zourdos et al., 2016). It was popularised in the sport through Mike Tuchscherer's Reactive Training Systems approach.

| RPE | Meaning | Reps in reserve |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | Maximal effort; no more reps possible | 0 |
| 9.5 | No more reps, but slightly more load was possible | 0 |
| 9 | One more rep possible | 1 |
| 8.5 | One more rep definitely, maybe two | 1–2 |
| 8 | Two more reps possible | 2 |
| 7.5 | Two more reps definitely, maybe three | 2–3 |
| 7 | Three more reps possible | 3 |
| 5–6 | Four to six more reps possible | 4–6 |
| 3–4 | Light effort | — |
| 1–2 | Little to no effort | — |

**How well do lifters rate RIR?**

- **Ratings get more accurate closer to failure.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A review and exploratory meta-analysis found that people predict their remaining reps more accurately as a set approaches failure, and tend to *underestimate* how many reps they have left when far from it (Halperin et al., 2022). A lifter who reports "RPE 6" may be further from failure than they think. <Evidence level="practice" /> In practice, this is why many coaches rely mainly on ratings in the RPE 7–10 range.
- **Experience improves accuracy.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> When the RIR-based scale was introduced, experienced squatters rated near-maximal sets more accurately than novices (Zourdos et al., 2016).

:::tip[In plain words]
RIR asks: "When you stopped, how many more reps could you have done?" RPE turns that into a score out of 10: 10 means nothing left, 9 means one more rep, 8 means two more. People get better at judging this with practice, and they judge it best when the set is hard.
:::

## RPE-to-percentage chart

This chart estimates the percentage of 1RM a lifter is using for a given number of reps at a given RPE. It follows the widely used RTS chart (Tuchscherer). <Evidence level="practice" /> Use it as a **starting estimate**: because reps at a given percentage vary between lifters and lifts (Nuzzo et al., 2024), adjust it once you see how a lifter actually performs.

| Reps | RPE 10 | 9.5 | 9 | 8.5 | 8 | 7.5 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | 100% | 97.8% | 95.5% | 93.9% | 92.2% | 90.7% | 89.2% |
| **2** | 95.5% | 93.9% | 92.2% | 90.7% | 89.2% | 87.8% | 86.3% |
| **3** | 92.2% | 90.7% | 89.2% | 87.8% | 86.3% | 85.0% | 83.7% |
| **4** | 89.2% | 87.8% | 86.3% | 85.0% | 83.7% | 82.4% | 81.1% |
| **5** | 86.3% | 85.0% | 83.7% | 82.4% | 81.1% | 79.9% | 78.6% |
| **6** | 83.7% | 82.4% | 81.1% | 79.9% | 78.6% | 77.4% | 76.2% |
| **7** | 81.1% | 79.9% | 78.6% | 77.4% | 76.2% | 75.1% | 73.9% |
| **8** | 78.6% | 77.4% | 76.2% | 75.1% | 73.9% | 72.3% | 70.7% |
| **9** | 76.2% | 75.1% | 73.9% | 72.3% | 70.7% | 69.4% | 68.0% |
| **10** | 73.9% | 72.3% | 70.7% | 69.4% | 68.0% | 66.7% | 65.3% |

**How to read it:** a set of 5 at RPE 8 is about 81% of 1RM. For a 180 kg squatter, that is about 146 kg, so 145 kg is a sensible first guess. **It also works backwards:** if a lifter squats 160 kg for 3 at RPE 8 (86.3%), their estimated 1RM that day is 160 ÷ 0.863 ≈ **185 kg**.

:::tip[In plain words]
This chart turns "5 reps at RPE 8" into "about 81% of your max", so you can pick a starting weight. It's a good first guess, not an exact rule. Adjust it once you see how the lifter actually moves the bar.
:::

## Do RPE and percentages give different results?

<Evidence level="emerging" /> In a study of trained lifters, programs matched for sets and reps produced similar strength gains whether loads were set by RPE or by percentage, with small differences favouring RPE (Helms et al., 2018). This rests mainly on one small, 8-week study, so neither method can be called clearly superior. The bigger practical difference is that RPE adjusts automatically to the lifter's day, while percentages need the coach to adjust.

<Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 14 studies found that every autoregulated method tested, including RPE/RIR targets, flexible daily undulating periodization, and velocity-based methods, increased 1RM strength (Larsen et al., 2021). Autoregulation is a reliable way to train; it is not shown to be the *only* good way.

## Choosing a method

- **Novices:** <Evidence level="practice" /> use fixed loads or percentages with simple, planned increases, as in linear progression (Rippetoe & Baker). New lifters rate effort poorly, and while loads rise every session the plan already does the adjusting. Teach the RPE scale alongside so they learn it before they need it.
- **Intermediate and advanced lifters:** <Evidence level="practice" /> combine the two. Give a percentage range as a starting point and an RPE target as the limit, for example "3 × 4 at 75–80%, stop the range at RPE 8" (Helms et al., 2016). This keeps the plan's structure while respecting the lifter's day.
- **Top sets and back-off sets:** <Evidence level="practice" /> a common autoregulated structure works up to a single or a small set at a target RPE, then sets the back-off load from that day's performance (Tuchscherer).

:::tip[In plain words]
Beginners should follow simple set weights that go up a little each session, and learn RPE along the way. More experienced lifters do best with a planned weight range plus an RPE limit, so the plan bends a little on good and bad days.
:::

## Sample programs

### Program 1: One week written two ways (intermediate, 4 days)

One training week for a lifter with a squat of 180 kg, a bench press of 120 kg and a deadlift of 210 kg, written two ways. The percentages are approximate RPE equivalents rounded to 2.5 kg; the real link between percentage and RPE differs from lifter to lifter, which is exactly why RPE is useful.

| Day | Exercise | Written with RPE | Written with %1RM |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 1 × 1 @ RPE 8, then 3 × 4 @ RPE 7 | 1 × 1 @ 92% (165 kg), then 3 × 4 @ 81% (145 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 5 @ RPE 7 | 4 × 5 @ 79% (95 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 1 × 3 @ RPE 8, then 2 × 3 @ RPE 7 | 1 × 3 @ 86% (180 kg), then 2 × 3 @ 84% (175 kg) |
| 3 | Squat | 4 × 3 @ RPE 7 | 4 × 3 @ 84% (150 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press | 1 × 1 @ RPE 8, then 3 × 3 @ RPE 7 | 1 × 1 @ 92% (110 kg), then 3 × 3 @ 84% (100 kg) |
| 4 | Deadlift | 4 × 4 @ RPE 7 | 4 × 4 @ 81% (170 kg) |
| 4 | Bench press | 3 × 5 @ RPE 8 | 3 × 5 @ 81% (97.5 kg) |

**How to use it:** with the RPE version, the lifter picks loads in the session so each set lands on the target; the percentage column shows roughly where they should end up. If the lifter's RPE 8 single on day 1 is 172.5 kg instead of 165 kg, they are stronger than the old max suggests, and the back-off sets move up with it.

### Program 2: Learning RPE (novice, 3 days)

For a newer lifter (for example squat about 100 kg, bench press about 60 kg, deadlift about 120 kg). Loads are **fixed for each session**, and the lifter **rates every set** to practise the scale. The RPE rating decides next session's load.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 3 × 5 | RPE 6–7 |
| Mon | Bench press | 3 × 5 | RPE 6–7 |
| Mon | Barbell row | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| Wed | Squat | 3 × 5 | RPE 6–7 |
| Wed | Bench press | 3 × 5 | RPE 6–7 |
| Wed | Deadlift | 2 × 5 | RPE 6–7 |
| Fri | Squat | 3 × 5 | RPE 6–7 |
| Fri | Bench press | 3 × 5 | RPE 6–7 |
| Fri | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |

**Progression rules:**

| If the hardest set was rated… | Next session |
|---|---|
| RPE 7 or lower | Add 5 kg (squat, deadlift) or 2.5 kg (bench) |
| RPE 7.5–8 | Add 2.5 kg (squat, deadlift) or keep the same load (bench) |
| RPE 8.5 or higher | Keep the same load |
| RPE 8.5 or higher twice in a row | Drop 5–10% and build back up |

Once a month, film one set and compare the lifter's rating with how the bar actually moved. This calibrates their RPE before they need it for intermediate programs.

### Program 3: Top set and back-off sets (intermediate template)

The lifter works up to one **top set** at a target RPE, then does **back-off sets** at a fixed percentage below it. Whatever the top set weighs on the day, the back-offs follow. This adjusts the whole session to the lifter's readiness.

| Lift | Top set | Back-off sets | Example (squat 180 / bench 120 / deadlift 210 kg) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Squat | 1 × 3 @ RPE 8 | 3 × 3 at top set −6% | Top 155 kg → back-offs 145 kg |
| Bench press | 1 × 5 @ RPE 8 | 3 × 5 at top set −8% | Top 97.5 kg → back-offs 90 kg |
| Deadlift | 1 × 2 @ RPE 8 | 2 × 3 at top set −8% | Top 187.5 kg → back-offs 172.5 kg |

**On a good day** the squat top set moves well at 160 kg for an RPE 8, so back-offs become 150 kg. **On a bad day** RPE 8 arrives at 150 kg, so back-offs become 140 kg. Both are the right training for that day.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating percentages as exact.** 80% is not the same effort for every lifter or every lift. Watch how sets actually move and adjust.
- **Rating effort by how tired the lifter feels instead of reps left.** RPE in this scale means reps in reserve, not general fatigue or breathlessness.
- **Trusting low RPE ratings too much.** Ratings far from failure are the least accurate; lifters usually have more left than they think.
- **Using RPE with novices who haven't learned it.** Teach and check the scale first, for example by occasionally comparing a rated set against video or a rep-out set.
- **Never re-checking maxes.** Percentages based on an old 1RM drift away from reality as the lifter gets stronger.

## Key takeaways

- %1RM is simple and objective, but reps at a given percentage vary widely between lifters and lifts.
- RIR/RPE describes effort as reps left in the tank and adjusts to the lifter's day.
- Lifters rate RIR best close to failure and get better with experience.
- Early research (mainly one small study) shows similar strength gains with RPE and percentage loading.
- Use fixed loads for novices; combine percentage ranges with RPE limits for intermediate and advanced lifters.
