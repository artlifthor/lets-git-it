## In one minute

Every program is built from the same handful of variables: **volume** (how much work), **intensity** (how heavy), **effort** (how close to failure), **frequency** (how often), **density and rest** (how much time between sets), **tempo** (how fast each rep moves), and **exercise selection and order**. For strength, the evidence is clearest for three of them: heavier loads, enough weekly volume, and frequency used to spread that volume across the week. Changing one variable changes the others, so coaches adjust them together, not one at a time. Counting **hard sets** per lift per week is the simplest reliable way to track volume.

## Volume

**How much work is done.** Three common ways to count it:

| Measure | How to calculate | Good for | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hard sets** | Sets taken to roughly RPE 6–10 | Weekly planning per lift | Ignores reps and load |
| **Total reps** | Sets × reps | Comparing similar loads | Ignores load |
| **Volume load** (tonnage) | Sets × reps × kg | Tracking work over time on the same exercise | Rewards light, high-rep work; misleading across rep ranges |

**Worked example:** 5 × 5 at 142.5 kg = 25 reps × 142.5 kg = **3,562.5 kg** of volume load. 4 × 3 at 155 kg = 12 reps × 155 kg = **1,860 kg**. The second session has about half the tonnage but is heavier and more specific to a max, which is why tonnage alone can mislead.

- <Evidence level="strong" /> Across many studies, **more weekly volume produces more strength, with diminishing returns**. Each extra set adds less than the one before, and the flattening is stronger for strength than for muscle size (Pelland et al., 2025).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> An earlier meta-analysis also found larger strength gains with more weekly sets than with fewer (Ralston et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> A common starting point for intermediate lifters is roughly **8–15 hard sets per competition lift per week** (counting close variations), adjusted by the lifter's response. The idea that each lifter has a minimum effective and a maximum recoverable volume is developed by Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith and covered in Part 2 (volume landmarks).

:::tip[In plain words]
Volume is how much work you do, usually counted as hard sets. More sets generally means more strength, but only up to a point: each extra set helps a bit less. Doing far too many just makes you tired.
:::

## Intensity

**How heavy the load is**, usually expressed as a percentage of one-rep max (%1RM).

- <Evidence level="strong" /> Heavy loads produce **larger gains in one-rep max** than light loads, while muscle growth is similar across a wide range of loads (Lopez et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> For powerlifters, most competition-lift work sits between about **70% and 90%**, with heavier singles and doubles closer to a meet.

How to prescribe and measure intensity is covered in [Chapter 5: Measuring intensity](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/).

:::tip[In plain words]
Intensity means how heavy the weight is. Lifting heavy weights is what makes you good at lifting heavy weights, so powerlifters spend most of their time with fairly heavy loads.
:::

## Effort (proximity to failure)

**How close each set ends to failure**, measured with reps in reserve (RIR) or RPE.

- <Evidence level="strong" /> Training to failure gave **no extra strength** compared with stopping short of it (Grgic et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Most competition-lift sets at **RPE 6–9** (1–4 reps in reserve); accessories can go closer to failure because they cost less recovery.

:::tip[In plain words]
Effort means how close you get to not being able to do another rep. You don't need to go all the way. Stopping with one to three reps left gives the same strength gains with less tiredness.
:::

## Frequency

**How many times per week a lift (or muscle) is trained.**

- <Evidence level="strong" /> Training more often produced larger strength gains, **but when total weekly volume was equal, frequency made no clear difference** (Grgic et al., 2018). A later, larger analysis found strength still rises with frequency, with diminishing returns (Pelland et al., 2025).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Treat frequency mainly as a **tool for spreading volume**: more sessions means fewer sets per session, fresher sets, and more practice of the competition lifts. Typical ranges: bench 2–4×, squat 2–3×, deadlift 1–2× per week.

:::tip[In plain words]
Frequency means how often you train a lift each week. Splitting your sets over more days helps you do more good-quality work without being exhausted in any one session, and gives more practice.
:::

## Density and rest periods

**Density** is how much work fits into a given time. It is mostly controlled by **rest between sets**.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 21 trained men, resting **3 minutes** between sets produced larger squat and bench press 1RM gains than resting **1 minute** over 8 weeks (Schoenfeld et al., 2016).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A review on muscle growth found both short and long rests can work, with a possible advantage for longer rests in trained lifters (Grgic et al., 2017).

<Evidence level="practice" /> A practical guide:

| Type of set | Rest |
|---|---|
| Heavy competition-lift sets (1–5 reps, RPE 8+) | 3–5 min |
| Moderate competition-lift and variation sets (4–8 reps, RPE 6–8) | 2–4 min |
| Accessories (8–15 reps) | 1–2 min |

:::tip[In plain words]
Rest long enough that the next set is a good one. For heavy squats, benches and deadlifts that usually means three to five minutes. For lighter extra exercises, one to two minutes is fine.
:::

## Tempo

**How fast each phase of a rep moves.** Tempo is often written as three numbers: *lowering – pause – lifting*. For example **3-1-X** means a 3-second descent, a 1-second pause, then lift as fast as possible (X).

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis found similar muscle growth with rep durations from **0.5 to 8 seconds**; very slow reps (over 10 seconds) appeared worse (Schoenfeld et al., 2015).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> For strength, lift the bar **with intent to move it fast** on the way up. Use slow lowering phases and pauses as **variations** to teach position and control (tempo squat, pause bench), not on every set.

:::tip[In plain words]
Tempo is the speed of each rep. Lower the weight under control and push it up as fast as you can. Slowing down on purpose is a useful drill for technique, but you don't need to do it on every set.
:::

## Exercise selection and order

- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Selection:** competition lifts and close variations make up most of the work, chosen for specificity and weak points (see [Chapter 3](/lets-git-it/foundations/03-core-training-principles/)).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Order:** do the most important, most skilled and heaviest lift first in the session while the lifter is fresh. Accessories come last.

:::tip[In plain words]
Pick exercises that are the competition lifts or close to them, and do the most important lift first in the session while you're still fresh.
:::

## How the variables trade off

| If you increase… | …usually decrease or watch… |
|---|---|
| Intensity (heavier loads) | Reps per set, total volume |
| Volume (more sets) | Intensity or effort, so recovery keeps up |
| Frequency | Sets per session |
| Effort (closer to failure) | Volume and frequency |
| Density (shorter rests) | Load per set |

## Sample programs

The same weekly volume for an intermediate lifter (squat 180 kg, bench 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg), spread across 3, 4 and 5 days. **Weekly hard sets: squat 12, bench 16, deadlift 7** (including close variations). Loads are approximate RPE equivalents from [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/).

### Program 1: 3 days per week (longer sessions)

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 5 × 5 | RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) |
| Mon | Bench press | 5 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) |
| Wed | Bench press | 6 × 4 | RPE 7.5 (~100 kg) |
| Wed | Deadlift | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~170 kg) |
| Fri | Squat | 4 × 3 | RPE 8 (~155 kg) |
| Fri | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × 5 | RPE 6.5 |
| Fri | Bench press | 5 × 5 | RPE 7.5 (~95 kg) |
| Fri | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |

### Program 2: 4 days per week

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~170 kg) |
| 2 | Bench press | 4 × 4 | RPE 7.5 (~100 kg) |
| 3 | Squat | 4 × 3 | RPE 8 (~155 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press | 4 × 5 | RPE 7.5 (~95 kg) |
| 4 | Pause squat (2 s) | 4 × 4 | RPE 6.5 |
| 4 | Close-grip bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |

### Program 3: 5 days per week (shorter sessions)

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~170 kg) |
| 2 | Bench press | 3 × 4 | RPE 7.5 (~100 kg) |
| 3 | Squat | 4 × 3 | RPE 8 (~155 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) |
| 4 | Bench press | 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~102.5 kg) |
| 4 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 5 | Pause squat (2 s) | 4 × 4 | RPE 6.5 |
| 5 | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 |

**Which to choose?** The weekly work is the same. Choose by schedule and recovery: 3 days suits busy lifters but makes sessions long, so later sets get tired; 4–5 days gives shorter, fresher sessions and more practice, but needs more gym time.

## Common mistakes

- **Judging volume by tonnage alone.** Light, high-rep work inflates tonnage; count hard sets per lift as well.
- **Adding volume without limit.** Gains flatten as sets rise, while fatigue keeps climbing.
- **Cutting rests short on heavy sets** to save time. It lowers the load the lifter can use and the quality of practice.
- **Adding training days without redistributing volume.** More frequency should usually mean fewer sets per session, not more total work all at once.
- **Using slow tempos on everything.** Lift with speed and intent; keep slow tempos for specific variations.

## Key takeaways

- Count weekly **hard sets per lift** to plan volume; more volume helps strength, with diminishing returns.
- Heavy loads build maximal strength best; most powerlifting work sits at about 70–90% 1RM.
- Stop most sets 1–4 reps short of failure.
- Frequency is mainly a way to spread volume; more sessions means fresher, higher-quality sets.
- Rest 3–5 minutes on heavy sets; lift up with speed and intent.
- The variables trade off against each other, so change them together.
