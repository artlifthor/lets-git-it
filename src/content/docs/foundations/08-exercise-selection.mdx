## In one minute

Every exercise in a powerlifting program belongs to one of three groups: the **competition lifts**, **variations** of them, and **accessories**. The competition lifts are the core of the program because strength gains are specific to the movement, range of motion and equipment used in training. Variations and accessories earn their place only if they do a job: fix a weak point, add useful volume, build muscle, work around pain, or fit the equipment available. The closer an exercise is to the competition lift, the more directly it transfers, so the mix shifts towards the competition lifts as a meet approaches. Most lifters need fewer exercises than they think: two to five per session, changed every block, not every week.

## The three groups of exercises

| Group | What it is | Main job | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Competition lifts** | Squat, bench press and deadlift performed exactly as in a meet: same stance, grip, depth, pause and commands | Skill and maximal strength | Competition squat, paused bench press, competition-stance deadlift |
| **Variations** | The competition lift with one thing changed: a pause, a tempo, a range of motion, a grip, a stance or a bar | Target a weak point, add volume with a similar skill, manage fatigue or pain | Pause squat, close-grip bench press, deficit deadlift |
| **Accessories** | Exercises that train the muscles used in the lifts, but with a different movement | Muscle size, weak muscles, joint health, balance between sides | Leg press, dumbbell row, triceps extension, back extension |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Siff called exercises that copy key features of the competition movement "special exercises", and argued they transfer best when they match its movement pattern, range, force and speed (Siff). Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith use the same logic to rank exercises from most to least specific.

:::tip[In plain words]
Your program has three kinds of exercises: the three lifts you compete in, slightly changed versions of them, and extra exercises for your muscles. The competition lifts matter most. Everything else has to earn its place by helping them.
:::

## How to choose an exercise

Run every candidate exercise through five questions, in this order.

### 1. Specificity and transfer: will it carry over to the competition lift?

**Transfer** means how much getting stronger at one exercise improves another. Transfer is highest when the two movements are alike.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Strength gains are specific to the way you train.** A meta-analysis of 13 studies (1,016 participants) found that free-weight training improved free-weight strength tests more than machine training did, while machine training tended to improve machine tests more. In a direct comparison, neither was better overall (Haugen et al., 2023).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Range of motion matters.** A meta-analysis of 16 studies found that full range of motion training produced larger strength gains than partial range of motion (Pallarés et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 17 male students training for 12 weeks, the deep-squat group improved both deep and shallow squat 1RM by about 20%. The shallow-squat group improved its shallow squat by 36% but its deep squat by only 9% (Bloomquist et al., 2013). Strength built in a short range did not carry over well to the full range.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 50 trained men over 10 weeks, full-range bench press training produced the best results in full, two-thirds and one-third range bench press tests. Partial bench training produced smaller gains the shorter its range was (Martínez-Cava et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="strong" /> Heavy loads produce larger 1RM gains than light loads (Lopez et al., 2021), so the competition lifts must be trained heavy at least some of the time.

**Decision rule:** the competition lift, at full competition range, is the default. A variation has to offer something the competition lift cannot. Partial-range exercises (pin press, block pulls) are supplements, never replacements.

:::tip[In plain words]
You get strongest at exactly what you practise: the same movement, the same depth and the same equipment. A half squat mostly makes your half squat stronger. Keep the full competition lifts as the main work and use other exercises to support them.
:::

### 2. Weak points: does it target where the lift breaks down?

A **weak point** is the part of a lift where the bar slows down or the lifter's position breaks. Common examples are coming out of the bottom of the squat, the bar slowing just off the chest in the bench, or the bar leaving the floor in the deadlift.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> Coaches choose a variation that **spends more time, or more load, in the weak position**: a pause or tempo at that point, or a range that starts there (Siff; Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Direct evidence that targeting a sticking point improves the competition lift is limited. Treat each choice as a test: keep it for a block, then check whether the competition lift moved. Diagnosing weak points in detail is covered in Part 5, Chapter 7 (From sticking point to accessory) and Part 3, Chapter 5 (Advanced weak-point analysis).

| Lift | Where it breaks down | Variation to try | Accessory to try |
|---|---|---|---|
| Squat | Out of the bottom | Pause squat, pin squat from the bottom | Leg press, split squat |
| Squat | Chest falls forward | Tempo squat, front squat, safety-bar squat | Back extension, upper-back rows |
| Bench | Just off the chest | Long-pause bench, Spoto press | Dumbbell bench press, dips |
| Bench | Lockout | Close-grip bench, pin or board press | Triceps extensions |
| Deadlift | Off the floor | Deficit deadlift, paused deadlift (just off the floor) | Leg press, front squat |
| Deadlift | At or above the knee | Paused deadlift (at the knee), block pull | Romanian deadlift, hip thrust, back extension |

:::tip[In plain words]
Watch where the bar slows down or your position falls apart. Pick an exercise that makes you work harder in that exact spot, give it a few weeks, and check whether your competition lift improved. If it didn't, try something else.
:::

### 3. Injury and pain: can the lifter do it without making things worse?

- <Evidence level="practice" /> If a lift hurts, move **one rung down the ladder** to the closest version that is pain-free, instead of dropping the movement altogether. Examples: a safety-bar squat when low-bar squatting irritates a shoulder; a close-grip or floor press when wide-grip bench irritates a shoulder; a block pull when a deficit deadlift aggravates the lower back.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Pain that persists, worsens, or comes with numbness, weakness or swelling needs a qualified health professional. Training around injury is covered in Part 5, Chapter 10 (Programming around injury).

:::tip[In plain words]
If an exercise hurts, find the closest version that doesn't, and keep training the pattern. If the pain sticks around or gets worse, see a health professional.
:::

### 4. Equipment: what does the gym actually have?

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Machines and free weights produce similar strength and muscle gains overall; each tends to improve its own kind of test more (Haugen et al., 2023). A barbell, a rack and a bench cover nearly everything a powerlifter needs. Machines are a convenience, not a requirement.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> If a gym lacks something, substitute by **function**: what muscle and what movement did the missing exercise train? A leg press can be replaced by a split squat; a cable triceps pushdown by a close-grip push-up or a barbell triceps extension.

:::tip[In plain words]
You don't need a fancy gym. A barbell, plates, a rack and a bench are enough to get very strong. If an exercise isn't possible where you train, swap it for one that works the same muscles in a similar way.
:::

### 5. The lifter: level, recovery and preference

- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Novices** improve on the competition lifts alone plus a few basic accessories; they need practice, not variety (Rippetoe & Baker).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Intermediate and advanced lifters** use more variations because they need new ways to overload, more targeted work on weak points, and ways to manage fatigue (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> Enjoyment matters. In 21 trained men over 8 weeks, randomly varying exercises each session gave similar strength and muscle gains to a fixed program and improved motivation to train (Baz-Valle et al., 2019). A lifter who likes their program is more likely to keep doing it.

:::tip[In plain words]
Beginners should keep it simple and practise the main lifts. More experienced lifters need more tools. And it helps to include exercises you actually enjoy, because you'll keep showing up.
:::

## The specificity ladders

Each ladder runs from most specific (top) to least specific (bottom). <Evidence level="practice" /> Rungs and examples follow common coaching practice (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith; Siff; Sheiko). Exact placement of an exercise can differ by lifter: for a low-bar squatter, a high-bar squat is a close variation; for a high-bar squatter, a low-bar squat is.

### Squat

| Rung | Exercise | What it trains | Use it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Competition** | Back squat with competition bar position, stance, depth and commands | Skill and maximal strength | Always; heaviest work near a meet |
| **2. Close variation** | Pause squat (2–3 s at the bottom) | Position and strength out of the bottom; no bounce | Weak out of the hole; technique work |
| | Tempo squat (3–5 s down) | Control, bar path, staying balanced | Lifter loses position on the way down |
| | Pin squat (start from pins at depth) | Starting strength from a dead stop | Weak out of the hole |
| | The other bar position (high bar / low bar) | Same pattern, more knee or more hip emphasis | Adding volume with a slightly different stress |
| **3. Related exercise** | Front squat, safety-bar squat | Quads and upper back; upright torso | Chest falls forward; shoulder or elbow pain with the back squat |
| | Belt squat, hack squat, leg press | Leg muscle with less lower-back load | Building muscle; lower back is tired or sore |
| **4. General exercise** | Split squat, lunges, leg extension, leg curl, back extension, sled | Muscle, side-to-side balance, joint tolerance | Off-season, accessories, warm-ups |

### Bench press

| Rung | Exercise | What it trains | Use it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Competition** | Paused bench press with competition grip, arch and commands | Skill and maximal strength | Always; heaviest work near a meet |
| **2. Close variation** | Long-pause bench (2–3 s on the chest) | Strength off the chest; tightness | Weak off the chest; lifter sinks the bar |
| | Close-grip bench (about shoulder width) | Triceps and lockout | Weak at lockout; elbow or shoulder irritation with a wide grip |
| | Spoto press (pause 2–3 cm above the chest) | Control and strength just off the chest | Weak off the chest |
| | Larsen press (feet up, no leg drive) | Upper-body strength and stability without leg drive | Adding volume with less lower-back and hip involvement |
| | Touch-and-go bench | Same pattern, heavier for reps | Adding volume (less specific: no pause) |
| | Pin press or board press (partial range) | Top-half strength; heavier-than-max loads | Weak at lockout; only as a supplement |
| **3. Related exercise** | Dumbbell bench press, incline bench, weighted dips, overhead press | Chest, shoulder and triceps muscle | Building muscle; more range of motion |
| **4. General exercise** | Triceps extensions, flyes, rows, pulldowns, face pulls | Muscle, upper-back base, shoulder health | Accessories in every block |

### Deadlift

| Rung | Exercise | What it trains | Use it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Competition** | Deadlift with competition stance (conventional or sumo) | Skill and maximal strength | Always; heaviest work near a meet |
| **2. Close variation** | Paused deadlift (just off the floor or at the knee) | Position and strength at the pause point | Weak or out of position at that point |
| | Deficit deadlift (standing on a 2–5 cm platform) | Strength off the floor; longer range | Slow off the floor |
| | Block pull or rack pull (bar raised) | Lockout strength; heavier-than-max loads | Weak at the knee or lockout; only as a supplement |
| | Tempo deadlift (slow lowering) | Control of bar path and position | Bar drifts away from the body |
| **3. Related exercise** | Romanian deadlift, stiff-leg deadlift, good morning | Hamstrings, glutes, lower back | Building the hip hinge; weak at lockout |
| | Opposite-stance deadlift, trap-bar deadlift | Similar pattern, different leverages | Adding volume; managing fatigue or pain |
| **4. General exercise** | Back extension, hip thrust, leg curl, rows, pull-ups, farmer's carry | Muscle, grip, upper back | Accessories in every block |

:::tip[In plain words]
Think of each lift as a ladder. The top rung is the lift exactly as you'll do it in a meet. Each rung down changes a bit more and carries over a bit less. Spend more time near the top as the meet gets closer, and use the lower rungs for muscle, weak spots and sore joints.
:::

## How often to change exercises

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review found that planned variation can help, while excessive, random variation may hold gains back (Kassiano et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 49 physically active people training twice a week for 12 weeks, the group that kept the load scheme constant but varied the exercises improved squat 1RM more than groups that varied load only, varied both, or varied neither (Fonseca et al., 2014). It is one study, and not in powerlifters.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Keep the competition lifts in every block.** Change variations and accessories every block (about 3–6 weeks), so each has time to make progress you can measure (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith; Sheiko).

:::tip[In plain words]
Changing exercises can help, but only if you do it on a plan. Keep the three competition lifts all year. Swap the extra exercises every month or so, not every week, so you can see whether they're working.
:::

## What accessories are for

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 29 untrained young men, adding biceps curls and triceps extensions to a program of bench press and lat pulldowns gave no extra arm muscle or strength over 10 weeks (Gentil et al., 2013). Big compound lifts already train a lot of muscle, at least in beginners.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> For powerlifters, accessories earn their place when they: build muscle the lifts don't train enough (upper back, hamstrings, triceps); balance one side against the other; keep joints tolerant of heavy training; or add volume without the fatigue of more heavy barbell sets. How to program them is covered in Part 2, Chapter 13 (Programming accessories).

:::tip[In plain words]
Extra exercises are helpers, not the main event. Pick a few that build muscles your main lifts need, and don't add more just to feel busy. A beginner can get very strong with almost none.
:::

## How many exercises?

<Evidence level="practice" /> These are typical starting points, not research-derived limits (Rippetoe & Baker; Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith).

| Lifter | Exercises per session | Different exercises per week | Typical make-up |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Novice** | 2–4 | 5–8 | The three competition lifts plus 2–4 basic accessories |
| **Intermediate** | 3–5 | 8–12 | Competition lifts, 2–4 close variations, 3–5 accessories |
| **Advanced (off-season)** | 3–6 | 10–14 | More variations and accessories for weak points and muscle |
| **Any lifter, last 3–4 weeks before a meet** | 2–4 | 4–8 | Mostly competition lifts; few variations, minimal accessories |

**Two working rules:**

1. <Evidence level="practice" /> Most of each lift's weekly hard sets should come from the competition lift and its close variations (rungs 1–2), especially for bench press and squat.
2. <Evidence level="practice" /> Every exercise beyond the competition lifts should have a one-line reason written next to it ("long-pause bench: slow off the chest"). If you can't write the reason, drop the exercise.

:::tip[In plain words]
Two to five exercises in a session is plenty. Most of your hard work should go into the competition lifts and close versions of them. If you can't say why an exercise is in your program, take it out.
:::

## Sample programs

Intermediate lifter, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg, four days a week. Loads use the RPE chart from [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/); variations and accessories are given as RPE only, because their maxes differ from the competition lifts.

### Program 1: The same week for two bench weak points

Lifter A slows down **just off the chest**. Lifter B gets the bar off the chest fast but **stalls near lockout**. Squat and deadlift work is the same for both. Only the bench variations and accessories change; the competition bench stays.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load | Lifter A: weak off the chest | Lifter B: weak at lockout |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) | Same | Same |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) | Same | Same |
| 1 | Barbell row | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 | Same | Same |
| 2 | Deadlift | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~170 kg) | Same | Same |
| 2 | Bench variation | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 | **Long-pause bench (3 s)** | **Close-grip bench** |
| 2 | Accessory | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 | **Dumbbell bench press** (deep stretch at the bottom) | **Barbell triceps extension** |
| 3 | Squat | 4 × 3 | RPE 8 (~155 kg) | Same | Same |
| 3 | Bench press | 4 × 5 | RPE 7.5 (~95 kg) | Same | Same |
| 4 | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × 4 | RPE 6.5 | Same | Same |
| 4 | Bench variation | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 | **Spoto press** | **Pin press (top third)** |
| 4 | Accessory | 3 × 12 | RPE 8 | **Weighted dips** | **Overhead triceps extension** |
| 4 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 | Same | Same |

**Why it's built this way.** Both lifters keep 8 hard sets a week of the competition bench (rung 1). Each gets 7 sets of close variations (rung 2) aimed at their weak point, plus 6 sets of pressing accessories. Lifter B has only one partial-range slot (the pin press); the rest of the bench work stays full range, because partial-range training transfers less to the full lift (Martínez-Cava et al., 2022). After 4–6 weeks, compare the competition bench to the start of the block: if it hasn't moved, change the variation.

### Program 2: Minimal equipment (barbell, plates, rack, flat bench, pull-up bar)

Same lifter, training in a small gym or at home with no machines, cables or dumbbells. Three days a week.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load | Replaces |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) | — |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) | — |
| 1 | Barbell row | 4 × 8 | RPE 8 | Cable row |
| 1 | Chin-ups | 3 × max reps, stop 1–2 short of failure | Bodyweight | Lat pulldown |
| 2 | Deadlift | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~170 kg) | — |
| 2 | Close-grip bench | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 | — |
| 2 | Barbell split squat | 3 × 8 per leg | RPE 7 | Leg press |
| 2 | Push-ups (feet raised if easy) | 3 × 12–20 | RPE 8 | Dumbbell press, dips |
| 3 | Squat | 4 × 3 | RPE 8 (~155 kg) | — |
| 3 | Bench press | 4 × 5 | RPE 7.5 (~95 kg) | — |
| 3 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 | Leg curl, back extension |
| 3 | Barbell triceps extension (lying) | 3 × 12 | RPE 8 | Cable pushdown |

**Why it's built this way.** Every competition lift and close variation needs only a barbell, rack and bench, so the strength-building core is unchanged. Free-weight training improves free-weight strength at least as well as machines do (Haugen et al., 2023). Accessories are swapped by function: a split squat trains the legs like a leg press, chin-ups replace pulldowns.

### Program 3: Novice exercise menu (3 days)

A newer lifter (squat about 100 kg, bench press about 60 kg, deadlift about 120 kg). Fixed loads that rise each session, as in [Chapter 7: The novice lifter](/lets-git-it/foundations/07-novice-linear-progression/). The aim is practice, not variety.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 3 × 5 | Fixed, +2.5–5 kg per session |
| Mon | Bench press | 3 × 5 | Fixed, +1–2.5 kg per session |
| Mon | Barbell row | 3 × 8 | RPE 7–8 |
| Wed | Squat | 3 × 5 | Fixed |
| Wed | Bench press | 3 × 5 | Fixed |
| Wed | Deadlift | 2 × 5 | Fixed, +5 kg per session |
| Fri | Squat | 3 × 5 | Fixed |
| Fri | Bench press | 3 × 5 | Fixed |
| Fri | Romanian deadlift | 2 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| Fri | Chin-ups or pulldown | 3 × 8 | RPE 8 |

**Why it's built this way.** Six exercises in the whole week. The competition lifts get almost all the practice. Two hinge and two pulling accessories cover the back and hamstrings. Variations come later, when simple load increases stop working.

## Common mistakes

- **Replacing the competition lift with a variation for weeks at a time.** Variations support the competition lifts; they don't stand in for them.
- **Choosing variations because a famous lifter uses them.** Choose for this lifter's weak point, and test whether it worked.
- **Relying on partial-range lifts.** Board presses and rack pulls can help lockout, but strength in a short range carries over poorly to the full range.
- **Changing exercises every week.** Too much random change stops you seeing what works.
- **Too many accessories.** They add fatigue and time; a handful chosen for a reason is enough.
- **Training through pain instead of moving down the ladder.** Find the closest pain-free version and keep the pattern.

## Key takeaways

- Exercises fall into three groups: competition lifts, variations and accessories.
- Strength is specific to movement, range of motion and equipment, so the competition lifts at full range are the core of every block.
- Choose other exercises by five questions: transfer, weak point, pain, equipment and the lifter.
- Each lift has a specificity ladder; train higher on it as the meet gets closer.
- Keep competition lifts constant; rotate variations and accessories every block, on purpose.
- Two to five exercises per session is enough for most lifters; every extra exercise needs a reason.
