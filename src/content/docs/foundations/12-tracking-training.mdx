## In one minute

A training log turns "I think I'm getting stronger" into numbers a coach can act on. Record at least the **exercise, sets, reps, load and RPE** of every work set, plus short notes on sleep, pain and technique. From the log, an **estimated one-rep max (e1RM)** can be calculated for any hard set using Epley, Brzycki or the RPE chart from Chapter 5; these estimates are most accurate from sets of about 10 reps or fewer taken close to failure. Track three simple numbers each week: **top-set e1RM per lift**, **hard sets per lift**, and **how hard the planned work felt**. Look at trends over several weeks, not single sessions, and film top sets to check technique and RPE.

## Why keep a log

<Evidence level="practice" /> Coaches across every school of programming keep detailed records (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith; Tuchscherer). A log does four jobs:

1. **Shows progress** that is too slow to notice day to day.
2. **Spots fatigue early:** the same loads feeling harder for several sessions is a warning sign (see [Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/foundations/02-stress-recovery-adaptation/)).
3. **Sets the next session's loads**, especially in RPE-based programs.
4. **Shows what works for this lifter.** Individual responses differ, so the lifter's own history is the best guide to their next program.

:::tip[In plain words]
Write your training down. Memory is bad at spotting slow progress or slow decline. A notebook or spreadsheet shows you both, and tells you and your coach what to do next.
:::

## What to log

<Evidence level="practice" />

| Record | Essential? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Date and session (week/day of the program) | Essential | 2026-03-02, Week 2 Day 1 |
| Exercise (exact variation) | Essential | Pause squat (2 s), competition-grip bench |
| Sets × reps × load for every work set | Essential | 3 × 4 @ 145 kg |
| RPE of every work set, or at least the top set | Essential | 7, 7.5, 8 |
| Short notes: sleep, stress, pain, technique cues | Strongly recommended | "5 h sleep; left hip tight; knees out cue helped" |
| Bodyweight (morning, same conditions) | Recommended, essential before a meet | 82.6 kg |
| Video link of top sets | Recommended | Phone gallery or cloud link |
| Warm-up sets | Optional | Useful only if warm-ups are part of the problem |
| Rest times, bar speed | Optional | For lifters and coaches who use them |

**Be specific about variations.** "Squat 3 × 3 @ 150 kg" means something different with a belt or without, with a pause or without, high-bar or low-bar. Write the variation every time.

**Log what actually happened**, not what was planned. If the plan said 4 sets and the lifter stopped after 3, write 3.

:::tip[In plain words]
For every work set, write down the exercise, weight, reps and how hard it felt (RPE). Add a few words about sleep, pain or technique. Write what you actually did, not what the program said.
:::

## Paper, app, or spreadsheet?

<Evidence level="practice" /> All three work. The best log is the one the lifter actually fills in, every session.

| Format | Strengths | Weaknesses | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Paper notebook** | Fast, no battery, no distraction from the phone | No automatic calculations; hard to see trends; can be lost | Novices, lifters who dislike phones in the gym |
| **Training app** | Quick entry, automatic e1RM and charts | Fixed formats; data may be locked in the app; some apps calculate e1RM differently | Lifters who want convenience |
| **Spreadsheet** (Excel, Google Sheets) | Fully flexible; coach and lifter can share one file; easy custom charts | Takes setup; typing on a phone between sets is slower | Coaches, intermediate and advanced lifters |

A common compromise: log on paper or in a phone note during the session, then copy the work sets into a shared spreadsheet the same day.

:::tip[In plain words]
Paper, an app or a spreadsheet all work. Pick the one you'll actually use every time. Many lifters scribble in the gym and type it into a spreadsheet later.
:::

## Estimated one-rep max (e1RM)

Testing a true 1RM is tiring and can't be done often. An **estimated 1RM** uses a submaximal set to predict what the lifter could lift once.

### Three ways to calculate it

| Method | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Epley** | e1RM = load × (1 + reps ÷ 30) | Simple; popular in apps. A coaching formula, not from a peer-reviewed study |
| **Brzycki** | e1RM = load × 36 ÷ (37 − reps) | Published as a reps-to-fatigue formula (Brzycki, 1993) |
| **RPE chart** | e1RM = load ÷ chart percentage (reps, RPE) | Uses the chart in [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/); works for sets not taken to failure |

**Important:** Epley and Brzycki assume the set went **to failure**. For a set stopped short of failure, use **reps + reps in reserve** in place of reps (for example, 5 reps at RPE 8 = 5 + 2 = 7), or use the RPE chart.

### Worked examples

**Example 1: a set to failure.** Squat 150 kg × 5, RPE 10 (no reps left).

| Method | Calculation | e1RM |
|---|---|---|
| Epley | 150 × (1 + 5/30) = 150 × 1.167 | **175 kg** |
| Brzycki | 150 × 36 ÷ (37 − 5) = 150 × 1.125 | **168.75 kg** |
| RPE chart (5 reps, RPE 10 = 86.3%) | 150 ÷ 0.863 | **173.8 kg** |

The methods disagree by about 6 kg, around 4%. This is normal; they are different averages fitted to different data.

**Example 2: the same load, not to failure.** Squat 150 kg × 5 at **RPE 8** (2 reps left).

| Method | Calculation | e1RM |
|---|---|---|
| Epley with reps only (wrong) | 150 × (1 + 5/30) | 175 kg (too low) |
| Epley with reps + RIR (5 + 2 = 7) | 150 × (1 + 7/30) | **185 kg** |
| Brzycki with reps + RIR | 150 × 36 ÷ (37 − 7) | **180 kg** |
| RPE chart (5 reps, RPE 8 = 81.1%) | 150 ÷ 0.811 | **185 kg** |

Ignoring the reps in reserve underestimates the lifter's strength by about 10 kg here.

**Example 3: a heavy triple.** Bench press 100 kg × 3 at RPE 9: chart = 89.2%, so e1RM = 100 ÷ 0.892 ≈ **112 kg**.

:::tip[In plain words]
An estimated max uses a hard set to guess what you could lift once. If you stopped with reps left, count those reps too, or the formula will say you're weaker than you are. Different formulas give slightly different numbers, so pick one and stick with it.
:::

### How accurate are e1RMs?

- **Reps at a given percentage vary between people and lifts.** <Evidence level="strong" /> A large meta-regression found wide differences between individuals and between exercises in how many reps can be done at a given %1RM (Nuzzo et al., 2024). Every formula is an average, so it will be wrong for some lifters by several kilograms.
- **Fewer reps predict better.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 70 men and women aged 18–69, the 5RM predicted 1RM more accurately than the 10RM or 20RM on bench press and leg press, and the authors advised using no more than 10 reps in linear prediction equations (Reynolds et al., 2006). <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 103 young women, prediction equations for the bench press were more accurate when fewer than 10 reps to fatigue were used, both before and after 12 weeks of training (Mayhew et al., 2008).
- **RPE tracks percentage closely in powerlifters.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 15 powerlifters, RPE and %1RM were very strongly related on the squat, bench press and deadlift (Helms et al., 2017). This supports using the RPE chart to estimate 1RM in experienced lifters.
- **Experience may help at maximal loads.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 27 college-aged men, experienced benchers rated a true 1RM closer to RPE 10 than novices (about 9.9 vs 9.4), but ratings did not differ at lighter loads (Ormsbee et al., 2019). <Evidence level="moderate" /> Across studies, people predicted reps left more accurately closer to failure, and training status did not clearly change accuracy (Halperin et al., 2022). Any lifter's low-RPE set gives a shaky e1RM.
- **Bar-speed predictions aren't a shortcut yet.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 17 strength-trained men, predicting squat 1RM from the load–velocity relationship of warm-up sets was only moderately reliable and differed significantly from the actual 1RM, while the actual 1RM was very stable across three test days (Banyard et al., 2017). Velocity-based methods are covered in Part 3, Chapter 3.
- **A true 1RM is a reliable test.** <Evidence level="strong" /> A systematic review of 32 studies (pooled 1,595 participants) found 1RM testing had good to excellent test–retest reliability, with a median variation of about 4% between tests (Grgic et al., 2020). Use a real 1RM (or a meet) as the reference point; e1RMs fill the gaps between.

**Rules for useful e1RMs** <Evidence level="practice" />

1. Use sets of **1–5 reps** where possible, and no more than about 8–10.
2. Use sets at **RPE 7.5–10**. Low-RPE sets give the least accurate estimates.
3. Use the **same method, same lift and same variation** every time, so errors stay consistent.
4. Use the **competition lift** under competition-like conditions (same depth, same pause, same equipment).
5. Watch the **trend over 3+ weeks**. A single-session change of 1–2% is within normal day-to-day variation.

:::tip[In plain words]
Estimated maxes are good guesses, not facts. They're most accurate from heavy sets of five reps or fewer, done close to failure. The closer a set is to failure, the better anyone judges how many reps were left. Use them to see if you're trending up or down over weeks, not to celebrate or panic over one session.
:::

## Tracking volume

Volume is covered in [Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/foundations/04-training-variables/). For tracking, keep it simple:

- **Hard sets per lift per week.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Count each work set at roughly RPE 6 or higher. Count close variations with their parent lift (pause squat counts as squat; close-grip bench as bench). Warm-ups don't count.
- **Volume load (sets × reps × kg)** <Evidence level="practice" /> per lift per week is useful for comparing similar weeks on the same exercise, but misleading across different rep ranges (Chapter 4 explains why).

**Worked example:** a week with squat 1 × 1 + 3 × 4 (day 1) and 4 × 3 (day 3), plus pause squat 3 × 4 (day 4) = 1 + 3 + 4 + 3 = **11 hard squat sets**.

:::tip[In plain words]
Count your hard sets for each lift every week: every work set that was at least moderately hard. Pause squats count as squats; warm-ups don't count. It's the simplest way to see how much training you're really doing.
:::

## Simple progress metrics

<Evidence level="practice" /> A weekly summary with a few numbers beats a log full of data nobody reads.

| Metric | How to get it | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Best top-set e1RM per lift** | Highest e1RM from that week's sets of 1–5 reps at RPE ≥ 7.5 | Strength trend |
| **Load at a fixed rep–RPE target** (for example the "RPE 8 triple") | Read straight from the log | The same trend without formulas |
| **Hard sets per lift** | Count from the log | How much work was done |
| **RPE vs plan** | Did planned sets come in at, above or below target RPE? | Fatigue (above), or loads set too light (below) |
| **Bodyweight** (weekly average) | Average of morning weigh-ins | Weight-class planning; large unplanned changes need attention |
| **Notes flags** | Count of sessions with pain, poor sleep or high stress | Patterns behind good and bad weeks |

**How to read the trend:**

| Pattern over 2–3 weeks | Likely meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| e1RM up, RPE on target | Progressing | Keep going |
| e1RM flat, RPE on target | Normal within a block, or stalling | Keep going; review at the end of the block |
| e1RM down **and** RPE above target | Fatigue building | Reduce sets, or deload (see [Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/foundations/02-stress-recovery-adaptation/)) |
| e1RM down, notes show poor sleep or high stress | Life stress | Adjust for recovery first (see [Chapter 10](/lets-git-it/foundations/10-recovery-sleep-stress/)) |
| e1RM up, RPE well below target | Loads too light | Raise loads |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Remember the fitness–fatigue idea from [Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/foundations/02-stress-recovery-adaptation/): during a hard block, e1RM can stall or dip while the lifter is still building fitness. Judge a block by the trend **and** by performance after the deload or test.

:::tip[In plain words]
Each week, look at a few numbers: your best estimated max for each lift, how many hard sets you did, and whether the planned weights felt harder or easier than planned. If your max is falling and everything feels harder, you're tired. If it's rising, keep going.
:::

## Video

<Evidence level="practice" /> Video is the coach's second pair of eyes and the lifter's best RPE check. Film **every top set** of the competition lifts, and all sets when changing technique.

| Lift | Best angle | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Squat | Side-on at hip height, about 3–4 m away; a 45° front angle as a second view | Depth (hip crease below the top of the knee), bar path over mid-foot, knee and hip movement, bar speed |
| Bench press | Side-on at bench height; a view from behind the head for grip and bar path | Touch point, pause, bar path, elbow position, feet and hips staying down |
| Deadlift | Side-on at hip height | Bar staying close, hips and shoulders rising together, lockout |

**Uses:**

- **Check RPE honestly.** Compare the lifter's rating with how fast the bar moved. A slow, grinding rep called "RPE 7" is a calibration problem (see [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/)).
- **Compare over time.** The same lift filmed from the same angle at the same e1RM shows whether technique is improving.
- **Check meet standards.** Squat depth and the bench pause are easy to get wrong without video. Competition rules are covered in Part 4, Chapter 7.

Store videos by date and lift, and link them in the log, so they can be found later.

:::tip[In plain words]
Film your heaviest sets from the side. Watching them shows whether you hit depth, how the bar moved, and whether your rep was really as easy as you thought. Keep the clips labelled by date and lift.
:::

## Sample programs

These are log templates and a tracked block rather than training programs. Use them with any program in this manual.

### Program 1: A paper session log (novice, 3 days)

Lifter: novice, squat about 100 kg, bench press 60 kg, deadlift 120 kg, on a fixed-load program with RPE ratings (as in Chapter 5, Program 2). Copy the layout into a notebook; one page per session.

**Session header:** Date · Week/day · Bodyweight · Sleep (hours) · Stress (1–5) · Notes

| Exercise | Set | Load (kg) | Reps | RPE | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Squat | 1 | 85 | 5 | 6 | |
| Squat | 2 | 85 | 5 | 6.5 | |
| Squat | 3 | 85 | 5 | 7 | Knees caved on rep 5 |
| Bench press | 1 | 52.5 | 5 | 7 | |
| Bench press | 2 | 52.5 | 5 | 7 | |
| Bench press | 3 | 52.5 | 5 | 7.5 | |
| Barbell row | 1–3 | 50 | 8, 8, 8 | 7 | |

**End-of-session line:** *Next time: squat 90 kg (hardest set RPE 7 → add 5 kg); bench 52.5 kg (hardest set RPE 7.5 → keep the same load).*

**Why this layout:** one line per set makes it impossible to "forget" a bad set, and writing the next session's loads at the end turns the log into the plan. The progression rules come from [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/).

### Program 2: A spreadsheet log with automatic e1RM (intermediate)

One row per work set. Set the sheet up once, then just type the first six columns.

| A: Date | B: Week | C: Lift | D: Load (kg) | E: Reps | F: RPE | G: e1RM (Epley + RIR) | H: e1RM (RPE chart) | I: Hard set? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2026-03-02 | 1 | Squat | 155 | 3 | 7.5 | 183 | 182 | Yes |
| 2026-03-02 | 1 | Squat | 145 | 4 | 7 | 179 | 179 | Yes |
| 2026-03-02 | 1 | Bench press | 95 | 5 | 7.5 | 119 | 119 | Yes |

**Formulas** (row 2; copy down):

- **G (Epley with reps in reserve):** `=D2*(1+(E2+(10-F2))/30)`
  Reps in reserve is taken as 10 − RPE, so a half-point RPE such as 7.5 counts as 2.5 reps left.
- **H (RPE chart):** put the Chapter 5 chart on a second sheet named `Chart`, with RPE values 10, 9.5, 9 … 7 across `B1:H1` and the percentages for 1–10 reps in `B2:H11` (as decimals, for example 0.863). Then:
  `=D2/INDEX(Chart!$B$2:$H$11, E2, MATCH(F2, Chart!$B$1:$H$1, 0))`
  This works for 1–10 reps at RPE 7–10, the range the chart covers.
- **I (hard set):** `=IF(F2>=6,"Yes","No")`

**Weekly summary** (a second table or pivot table): for each week and lift, the **maximum** of column H for sets of 1–5 reps, and the **count** of "Yes" in column I.

**Check the arithmetic:** row 2 is 155 × (1 + (3 + 2.5)/30) = 155 × 1.183 ≈ 183 kg (Epley) and 155 ÷ 0.850 ≈ 182 kg (chart). Close agreement between the two columns is a good sign the data were entered correctly.

### Program 3: A tracked 4-week block, with interpretation (intermediate)

Lifter: squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg, 4 days per week. The weekly summary below comes from a full log. e1RM uses the RPE chart from [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to the nearest kilogram.

**Squat** (top set of 3)

| Week | Top set | e1RM | Hard sets | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 155 kg × 3 @ RPE 7.5 | 182 kg | 10 | |
| 2 | 157.5 kg × 3 @ RPE 7.5 | 185 kg | 10 | |
| 3 | 160 kg × 3 @ RPE 8 | 185 kg | 12 | Work deadline, ~6 h sleep |
| 4 | 162.5 kg × 3 @ RPE 8 | 188 kg | 12 | |

**Bench press** (top set of 5)

| Week | Top set | e1RM | Hard sets | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 95 kg × 5 @ RPE 7.5 | 119 kg | 14 | |
| 2 | 97.5 kg × 5 @ RPE 8 | 120 kg | 15 | |
| 3 | 97.5 kg × 5 @ RPE 8.5 | 118 kg | 16 | Work deadline, ~6 h sleep; front of shoulder achy |
| 4 | 97.5 kg × 5 @ RPE 9 | 116 kg | 16 | Shoulder still achy |

**Deadlift** (top set of 3)

| Week | Top set | e1RM | Hard sets | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 180 kg × 3 @ RPE 8 | 209 kg | 6 | |
| 2 | 182.5 kg × 3 @ RPE 7.5 | 215 kg | 6 | |
| 3 | 185 kg × 3 @ RPE 8 | 214 kg | 7 | |
| 4 | 187.5 kg × 3 @ RPE 8 | 217 kg | 7 | |

**Interpretation** <Evidence level="practice" />

- **Squat and deadlift are progressing.** e1RM rose about 6–8 kg over four weeks with RPE on target, despite a stressful week 3. Keep the plan.
- **Bench press is going backwards.** The same 97.5 kg went from RPE 8 to RPE 9 while sets rose from 14 to 16, and the notes flag an achy shoulder two weeks running. That is the "e1RM down, RPE above target" pattern: fatigue, possibly irritation from too much pressing.
- **Action:** deload as planned, then return bench volume to about 14 sets (the level that was working) and watch the shoulder. If the ache persists, see a qualified health professional.
- **Without the log**, week 4 would have looked like "bench felt heavy today". With it, the pattern is clear after two weeks.

## Common mistakes

- **Logging only the top set or only the plan.** Record every work set as it actually happened.
- **Calculating e1RM from sets not taken to failure without adding reps in reserve.** It underestimates strength.
- **Using high-rep or low-RPE sets for e1RM.** Sets above about 10 reps, or below about RPE 7.5, give the least accurate estimates.
- **Switching formulas or variations between weeks.** A change in method looks like a change in strength.
- **Reacting to one session.** Day-to-day variation is normal; act on trends over 2–3 weeks.
- **Never filming.** Without video, depth, pauses and RPE ratings go unchecked.
- **Collecting data nobody reads.** Review a short weekly summary; a log is only useful if it changes decisions.

## Key takeaways

- Log every work set: exercise, load, reps and RPE, plus brief notes on sleep, stress, pain and technique.
- Estimate 1RM with Epley, Brzycki or the RPE chart; add reps in reserve to reps for sets not taken to failure.
- e1RMs are most accurate from sets of 1–5 reps at RPE 7.5–10; they are estimates with errors of several kilograms.
- Track a few numbers weekly: best top-set e1RM, hard sets per lift, and RPE versus plan.
- Judge trends over weeks, and film top sets to check technique and RPE.
