## In one minute

Lifters adapt between sessions, so what happens outside the gym decides how much of the training actually turns into strength. **Sleep** is the biggest lever: losing sleep lowers performance on average, and a single night without sleep already reduces muscle-building activity in the lab. **Sleeping more**, at night or with naps, is the sleep intervention with the best evidence for improving performance. **High life stress** is linked to smaller strength gains and slower recovery after hard sessions, so training should flex when exams, work or family pile up. Popular passive recovery methods mostly make you *feel* less sore; none replaces sleep, food and sensible programming, and regular cold-water immersion after lifting may even blunt strength gains.

## Why recovery happens outside the gym

Training is the stimulus; adaptation happens in the hours and days after it (see [Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/foundations/02-stress-recovery-adaptation/)). Three things outside the program control how well that goes: **sleep**, **food** (covered in Chapter 11: Nutrition), and **total stress**, meaning training stress plus everything else in the lifter's life.

<Evidence level="practice" /> The body does not keep separate accounts for gym stress and life stress. A hard week at work, an exam period or a new baby draws on the same recovery capacity as a hard training block (Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith).

:::tip[In plain words]
Training breaks you down a little; sleep, food and a calm-enough life build you back up stronger. If one of those is missing, the same program gives you less.
:::

## Sleep and muscle

Short-term lab studies show that sleep loss works directly against the processes that build muscle:

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 13 healthy young adults (7 men, 6 women), **one night without sleep** reduced muscle protein synthesis by 18% the next day, raised cortisol by 21% and lowered testosterone by 24% compared with a normal night (Lamon et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 24 healthy young men, **five nights of 4 hours in bed** lowered the rate of muscle protein synthesis compared with 8 hours in bed. A group that did high-intensity interval exercise during the same sleep restriction kept synthesis at normal levels (Saner et al., 2020). This used interval exercise, not lifting, so it does not show that training "cancels out" bad sleep for powerlifters.

These are short studies of single markers, not long-term training studies. They explain *why* poor sleep could slow progress; they don't tell you exactly how much.

:::tip[In plain words]
Even one night without sleep makes your muscles worse at rebuilding the next day. Several short nights in a row do the same. Sleep is when a lot of the repair from training gets done.
:::

## Sleep loss and performance

- **Sleep loss lowers performance on average.** <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 69 publications (959 participants, 89% male) found that sleep loss before exercise reduced performance by about **7.6% on average**, with significant effects in every category tested, including strength. The type of sleep loss mattered: not every pattern of restricted sleep had a consistent effect (Craven et al., 2022).
- **For lifting, several short nights seem to matter more than one bad night.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A review of 17 studies (rated moderate or weak quality) found that one night of total sleep deprivation had little effect on strength during resistance exercise, but **consecutive nights of restricted sleep** could reduce force on multi-joint lifts (not on single-joint exercises). The authors suggested that training with others, caffeine, or training earlier in the day may help lifters perform when sleep has been poor (Knowles et al., 2018).
- **Real-world partial sleep loss is less clear.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> An expert consensus statement concluded that a night or more without sleep reduces athletic performance, but the effect of 1–3 nights of *partial* restriction (the common real-life case) remains unclear, and studies in female athletes are scarce (Walsh et al., 2021).

**What this means for coaches:** <Evidence level="practice" /> one rough night rarely ruins a session, especially if the lifter is motivated and warmed up. A run of short nights is the bigger problem, and it shows up first on squats and deadlifts. RPE-based loading ([Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/)) handles a single bad night well, because the loads adjust to the lifter's day automatically.

:::tip[In plain words]
One bad night usually doesn't wreck your training. You might feel worse, but you can still lift close to normal. Several short nights in a row are what really drag your squats and deadlifts down.
:::

## How much sleep?

- <Evidence level="practice" /> A US National Sleep Foundation expert panel recommends **7–9 hours** for young adults and adults, **8–10 hours** for teenagers and **7–8 hours** for older adults (Hirshkowitz et al., 2015). These are general-population recommendations from expert consensus.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> The athlete consensus statement warns that one range is unlikely to suit every athlete. It recommends an individual approach based on the athlete's own perceived sleep need, and notes that elite athletes are prone to habitual short sleep of under 7 hours a night (Walsh et al., 2021).

**A practical target:** at least 7 hours of actual sleep most nights, more during hard training blocks and before competition. Time in bed must be longer than the target, because nobody sleeps every minute they lie down.

:::tip[In plain words]
Aim for at least seven hours of real sleep a night, and more when training is hard. If you need an alarm to drag yourself out of bed every morning, you probably need more.
:::

## Sleeping more: sleep extension and naps

**Sleep extension** means deliberately spending more time asleep than usual.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 11 college basketball players who aimed for at least 10 hours in bed per night for 5–7 weeks, nightly sleep rose by about 111 minutes. Sprint times, shooting accuracy, reaction time, mood and daytime sleepiness all improved (Mah et al., 2011). There was no separate control group, and no strength measures.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 25 athlete studies found that **sleep extension and naps were the most effective interventions** for improving sleep and performance. Sleep hygiene education, removing electronic devices at night and cold-water immersion showed no effect on sleep or performance, but only a few studies tested them (Cunha et al., 2023).

**Naps** help lifters who can't get enough sleep at night.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 37 studies of moderate quality found napping may benefit physical and cognitive performance, fatigue and mood without harming night-time sleep. The authors suggested naps of **20–90 minutes** between about **13:00 and 16:00**, and **at least 30 minutes** between waking and training, to let grogginess pass (Lastella et al., 2021).

No study has tested sleep extension on powerlifting strength directly. The evidence comes from other sports.

:::tip[In plain words]
Getting more sleep, either by going to bed earlier or taking an afternoon nap, is the best-supported way to recover better. Keep naps to about 20 to 90 minutes in the early afternoon, and give yourself half an hour to wake up properly before you train.
:::

## Practical sleep habits

The table below lists habits coaches commonly use. Only the caffeine rule has a direct experimental test behind it; the rest are established sleep-hygiene practice. As noted above, sleep-hygiene *education alone* has not yet been shown to improve athletic performance (Cunha et al., 2023), so treat these as habits to build, not magic.

| Habit | Why | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed wake-up time,** even on weekends | Keeps the body clock steady | <Evidence level="practice" /> |
| **Enough time in bed** (target sleep + 30–60 min) | Nobody sleeps 100% of time in bed | <Evidence level="practice" /> |
| **No caffeine in the 6 hours before bed** | 400 mg of caffeine taken 0, 3 or 6 hours before bed all disturbed sleep compared with placebo (Drake et al., 2013) | <Evidence level="emerging" /> |
| **Dark, quiet, cool bedroom** (fan or air-conditioning in hot weather) | Light, noise and heat make sleep lighter and more broken | <Evidence level="practice" /> |
| **Wind down 30–60 min before bed** after evening training | Hard sessions and bright screens keep lifters alert | <Evidence level="practice" /> |
| **Pre-workout and energy drinks count as caffeine** | Many contain as much caffeine as one or more cups of coffee; check the label | <Evidence level="practice" /> |
| **See a doctor for loud snoring, gasping at night, or constant sleepiness despite enough time in bed** | These can be signs of a sleep disorder that habits won't fix | <Evidence level="practice" /> |

<Evidence level="practice" /> For lifters who train in the evening after work or school, the caffeine rule matters most: a pre-workout drink at 18:00 is still active at a 23:00 bedtime. Train without it, use a smaller dose, or move caffeine earlier.

:::tip[In plain words]
Get up at the same time every day, give yourself enough time in bed, and keep your room dark and cool. Stop coffee and pre-workout drinks at least six hours before bed. If you snore loudly or are always sleepy no matter how long you sleep, see a doctor.
:::

## Life stress and training

**Psychological stress** (exams, deadlines, money, relationships, grief) affects both how well lifters adapt and how quickly they recover.

- **Smaller strength gains.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 135 university students in 12-week weight-training classes, those reporting **low** life stress gained significantly more on the bench press and squat than those reporting **high** life stress (Bartholomew et al., 2008).
- **Slower recovery.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 31 university resistance-training students, higher life-event stress and higher perceived stress were linked to slower recovery of leg strength, energy, fatigue and soreness over the 4 days after a hard leg-press session (Stults-Kolehmainen et al., 2014).
- **Less training gets done.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review found that most prospective studies (76%) showed psychological stress predicting **less** physical activity or exercise, for example around final exams. Some people, though, exercise *more* to cope (Stults-Kolehmainen & Sinha, 2014).

Both training studies used students, not competitive powerlifters, and measured stress with questionnaires. The direction is consistent, but the size of the effect for a trained lifter is unknown.

**What to do about it:** <Evidence level="practice" /> plan around predictable stress (exam weeks, busy seasons at work, travel) by lowering training volume ahead of time. React to unpredictable stress with RPE caps and shorter sessions. Keep the competition lifts in every week so skill doesn't fade, and protect sleep first. Programming around long periods of high stress is covered in Part 3, Chapter 11: Programming around life stress and limited time.

:::tip[In plain words]
Stress from school, work or home uses up the same energy you need to recover from training. When life gets hard, train a bit less and a bit lighter for a while, and keep sleeping. You'll keep your strength and pick up again when things calm down.
:::

## Recovery methods: what helps and what doesn't

Massage guns, foam rollers, ice baths and compression clothing are popular. The evidence mostly shows that they reduce **soreness and the feeling of fatigue**, not that they make lifters stronger over time.

- **Massage, compression and immersion.** <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 99 studies found that active recovery, massage, compression garments, cold-water immersion, contrast water therapy and cryotherapy reduced muscle soreness. **Massage was the most effective** for soreness and perceived fatigue (Dupuy et al., 2018).
- **Foam rolling.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 21 studies found the effects of foam rolling on performance and recovery were **small and partly negligible**. Rolling after exercise slightly reduced the drop in strength and sprint performance and reduced muscle pain (Wiewelhove et al., 2019).
- **Cold-water immersion after lifting.** <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 8 studies found that **regular** cold-water immersion during a training program **reduced gains** in one-rep max and other strength measures, while it did not affect endurance gains (Malta et al., 2021). <Evidence level="emerging" /> In one of the key studies, 21 active men who used 10 minutes of cold-water immersion after each session for 12 weeks gained less strength and muscle than men who did an active cool-down (Roberts et al., 2015).

| Method | Use it? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Sleep, including naps | **Yes, first** | Best-supported recovery tool |
| Enough food and protein | **Yes** | See Chapter 11: Nutrition |
| Sensible programming (RPE caps, deloads) | **Yes** | See [Chapter 2](/lets-git-it/foundations/02-stress-recovery-adaptation/) |
| Massage | Optional | Reduces soreness and perceived fatigue; won't add strength |
| Foam rolling | Optional | Small effects; fine if the lifter likes it |
| Compression garments | Optional | May reduce perceived fatigue and soreness |
| Cold-water immersion after lifting | **Avoid as a routine** during training blocks | May blunt strength gains; occasional use (for example between competition days) is a different situation |

<Evidence level="practice" /> None of these fixes poor sleep, too little food or a program with too much volume. If a lifter is relying on recovery gadgets to get through the week, the training load or the lifestyle needs adjusting.

:::tip[In plain words]
Massages, foam rollers and compression clothes can make you feel less sore, and that's fine if you enjoy them. They won't make you stronger. Avoid regular ice baths after lifting: they may reduce the strength you gain. Sleep and food matter far more than any gadget.
:::

## Sample programs

### Program 1: Adjusting a session after a bad night (intermediate)

Lifter: squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. Loads from the RPE chart in [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg.

**The planned session**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 1 × 1, then 3 × 4 | RPE 8 (~165 kg), then RPE 7 (~145 kg) |
| Mon | Bench press | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| Mon | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| Mon | Leg curl | 3 × 12 | RPE 8 |

**Decision rules** <Evidence level="practice" />

| Sleep before the session | What to do |
|---|---|
| Normal | Train as planned. |
| One short night (roughly 4–6 hours) | Warm up fully and train as planned **by RPE, not by kilograms**. Don't chase the planned load. If RPE runs 1+ point high on the first work sets, drop one back-off set per lift. Train with a partner and have your usual caffeine early in the session if you use it. |
| Very little sleep (under ~4 hours), or 3+ short nights in a row | Replace the heavy single with technique work, cut sets by about half, cap everything at RPE 7, skip accessories. Nap if possible and get to bed early. |
| Bad sleep plus illness symptoms (fever, body aches) | Rest. |

**The adjusted session (one short night)**

The squat single at RPE 8 comes in at 157.5 kg instead of the usual ~165 kg. The back-off sets follow the day's single, using the same ratio as the plan (about 88% of the single): 157.5 × 0.88 ≈ **137.5 kg**.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 1 × 1, then 2–3 × 4 | RPE 8 (157.5 kg today), then ~137.5 kg |
| Mon | Bench press | 3–4 × 5 | RPE 7 (whatever load that is today) |
| Mon | Romanian deadlift | 2 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| Mon | Leg curl | Skip or 2 × 12 | RPE 8 |

**The adjusted session (very little sleep, or several short nights)**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 3 × 3 | RPE 6–7 (up to ~150 kg) |
| Mon | Bench press | 2 × 5 | RPE 7 |
| Mon | — | Accessories skipped | — |

**Why:** a single bad night has little effect on strength in most studies, so the session is kept and RPE absorbs the difference (Knowles et al., 2018). Consecutive short nights reduce multi-joint strength more, so volume and effort come down until sleep is back to normal. The week's later sessions stay as planned.

### Program 2: Reduced-stress week during exams or a work crunch (4 days → 3 days)

Lifter: a university student in the 63 kg class, squat 120 kg, bench press 65 kg, deadlift 150 kg. In the normal week she trains four days; during exam week she has time and energy for three short sessions.

**Normal week**

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~50 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 (~122.5 kg) |
| 2 | Bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 (~50 kg) |
| 3 | Squat | 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~102.5 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press | 4 × 3 | RPE 8 (~55 kg) |
| 4 | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × 4 | RPE 6.5 |
| 4 | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |
| 4 | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 |

**Exam week** (sessions of about 45 minutes)

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Squat | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| 1 | Bench press | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~50 kg) |
| 2 | Deadlift | 2 × 4 | RPE 7 (~122.5 kg) |
| 2 | Bench press | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 (~52.5 kg) |
| 3 | Squat | 2 × 3 | RPE 7 (~100 kg) |
| 3 | Bench press | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 (~55 kg) |

| Weekly hard sets | Normal week | Exam week |
|---|---|---|
| Squat (incl. pause squat) | 10 | 5 |
| Bench press (incl. close-grip) | 14 | 9 |
| Deadlift (incl. Romanian deadlift) | 6 | 2 |

**Why it works:** <Evidence level="practice" /> volume is roughly halved and nothing goes above RPE 7, so the training costs little recovery while life stress is high (Bartholomew et al., 2008; Stults-Kolehmainen et al., 2014). The loads barely change, so she keeps practising heavy-ish competition lifts and loses little strength. Accessories are dropped first because they matter least. **Minimum session** if a day goes badly: one squat or deadlift exercise and bench press, 2–3 sets each at RPE 7, in 30 minutes. After the exams, return to the normal week.

### Program 3: Daily recovery checklist

A simple daily routine and a 1-minute morning check. Use it with any program. <Evidence level="practice" />

**Daily habits**

| Item | Target | ✔ |
|---|---|---|
| Sleep | 7+ hours asleep; same wake-up time daily | |
| Nap (if night sleep was short) | 20–90 min, early afternoon, at least 30 min before training | |
| Caffeine cut-off | Nothing in the 6 hours before bed | |
| Meals | Regular meals with protein at each (see Chapter 11: Nutrition) | |
| Fluids | Drink regularly, more in hot and humid weather | |
| Wind-down | 30–60 min of calm, low-light time before bed | |
| Daily movement | An easy walk on rest days | |

**Morning check (score each 1–5; 5 = best)**

| Question | Score |
|---|---|
| How well did I sleep (and roughly how many hours)? | |
| How stressed am I today? (5 = not stressed) | |
| How sore or beaten-up do I feel? (5 = fresh) | |
| How motivated am I to train? | |

**How to use the total (4–20):**

| Total | Training today |
|---|---|
| 14–20 | Train as planned. |
| 10–13 | Train as planned by RPE; drop the last back-off set if RPE runs high. |
| 9 or less, or two days in a row of 10–13 | Use the "very little sleep" rules from Program 1, and look for the cause (sleep, stress, food, too much training). |

This is a simple coaching tool, not a validated test. Its main value is making the lifter notice patterns and write them down, which is covered in [Chapter 12: Tracking training](/lets-git-it/foundations/12-tracking-training/). More formal readiness monitoring is covered in Part 3, Chapter 8: Monitoring readiness, overreaching, and overtraining.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating sleep as optional.** Cutting sleep to fit in training, study or work costs more than the extra hour gains.
- **Panicking after one bad night.** One short night rarely hurts strength much; train by RPE and move on. Several short nights in a row need a lighter plan.
- **Forcing planned kilograms on a bad day.** Let RPE set the load so the session stays productive.
- **Ignoring life stress until performance crashes.** Plan lighter weeks around exams, deadlines and travel before they arrive.
- **Using pre-workout or coffee late in the day.** Caffeine 6 hours before bed still disturbs sleep.
- **Relying on ice baths and gadgets.** They reduce soreness, not fatigue from poor sleep or too much volume, and regular ice baths may blunt strength gains.

## Key takeaways

- Sleep loss lowers performance on average and reduces muscle-building activity; several short nights matter more for lifting than one.
- Aim for at least 7 hours of real sleep, more in hard blocks; sleeping longer and napping are the best-supported sleep interventions.
- High life stress is linked to smaller strength gains and slower recovery; cut volume and cap RPE when life is hard.
- Cut off caffeine at least 6 hours before bed.
- Massage, compression and foam rolling mainly reduce soreness; avoid regular cold-water immersion after lifting during training blocks.
