## In one minute

A **novice** is a lifter who can recover from one session and come back stronger by the next, so the simplest and fastest way to train them is **linear progression (LP)**: the same few lifts, the same sets and reps, and a little more weight every session. Research shows untrained people gain strength quickly and that early gains come from better coordination as well as from muscle growth, but the rate slows over months. The jump size should fit the lift and the lifter: bigger for squat and deadlift, smaller for bench press, and smaller again for lighter and many female lifters. When reps start to be missed, repeat the weight, then reset about 10% and build back. When resets stop working, the novice phase is over and the lifter moves to weekly progression.

## Who counts as a novice?

**Training age is defined by how fast a lifter adapts, not by how long they have been in a gym.**

- <Evidence level="practice" /> Rippetoe & Baker define a novice as someone whose recovery from a single session is enough to produce a measurable gain by the next session. Someone who has trained casually for years, but never on a progressive barbell program, may still progress like a novice.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> An expert position stand uses time instead: novices have no resistance-training experience (or none for several years), and intermediates have about 6 months of consistent training (American College of Sports Medicine, 2009). The two definitions usually agree for people starting from scratch.

:::tip[In plain words]
You're a novice if you can add weight to the bar almost every workout and still get your reps. It's about how quickly your body responds, not how many years you've had a gym membership.
:::

## Why novices progress so fast

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis found that untrained people gained the most strength at moderate loads (around 60% 1RM on average), training each muscle about 3 days per week. Trained people needed heavier loads to keep improving (Rhea et al., 2003). Beginners respond to less.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 7 young adults training the legs 3 times per week for 35 days, maximal force rose about 39% and muscle activation (EMG) about 35%; muscle size increases were already measurable after about 3 weeks (Seynnes et al., 2007). Early gains come from the nervous system **and** early muscle growth.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 20 untrained women training 3 days per week for 9 weeks, squat 1RM rose by about 35–41% and bench press 1RM by about 22–28%. Linear and undulating periodization worked equally well (Kok et al., 2009). For beginners, the program structure matters less than training consistently and progressively.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> The fast rate does not last. In training records of 14,690 adults on a minimal program, strength rose about 30–50% in the first year and had nearly levelled off after 1–2 years (Steele et al., 2023). In 7 young men who did the same bench press program without breaks for 24 weeks, the rate of strength and muscle gain slowed after the first 6 weeks (Ogasawara et al., 2013).

:::tip[In plain words]
In your first months, your body is learning the lifts and building muscle at the same time, so strength shoots up. That fast phase doesn't last forever. Use it fully while it lasts, with a simple plan that adds weight often.
:::

## How linear progression works

**The same lifts, the same sets and reps, a little more weight each session.** The lifter trains about three times per week with a rest day between sessions.

<Evidence level="practice" /> The classic novice template from Rippetoe & Baker:

- **Squat every session**, 3 sets of 5.
- **Bench press and overhead press alternate** between sessions, 3 sets of 5.
- **Deadlift**, 1 heavy set of 5 after warm-ups. Later in the novice phase, Rippetoe & Baker alternate the deadlift with another pull (the power clean); powerlifters often use a lighter deadlift or Romanian deadlift instead.
- **Few accessories.** Chin-ups and back work are added as the program matures.

**Why sets of 5?** <Evidence level="practice" /> Rippetoe & Baker argue that 5 reps are heavy enough to build strength and practise the lift under real load, and few enough that technique holds. Other reasonable approaches exist: an expert position stand recommends novices use loads they can lift 8–12 times (American College of Sports Medicine, 2009), and research suggests novices gain well at moderate loads (Rhea et al., 2003). For future powerlifters, sets of 3–6 on the competition lifts are a sensible middle ground: heavy enough to practise competition technique, light enough to recover between sessions.

**Why fixed loads, not RPE?** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 29 squatters, novices rated a true 1RM as less than maximal more often than experienced lifters did, meaning they underrated their hardest efforts (Zourdos et al., 2016). <Evidence level="practice" /> In LP the plan already adjusts the load every session, so RPE is not needed to set weights. Have novices rate their sets anyway, to learn the scale (see [Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/)).

:::tip[In plain words]
Do the same few lifts three days a week, three sets of five, and add a little weight each time. That's it. Simple plans work best at the start because your body improves faster than any fancy program can change.
:::

## Running it: starting weights and increments

**Start light.** <Evidence level="practice" /> The first session's working weight should move quickly for every rep with clean technique. A good rule is to work up in sets of 5 until bar speed first slows, then use that weight or a little less (Rippetoe & Baker). Starting too heavy wastes the easiest weeks of progress.

<Evidence level="emerging" /> In 10 untrained young men, much of the muscle-building response in the first week went to repairing muscle damage rather than to growth (Damas et al., 2016). New lifters get very sore at first; light starting weights and small early volume limit this.

**Increments by lift.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Typical jumps per session (Rippetoe & Baker):

| Lift | First 2–3 weeks | After that | When jumps start failing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Squat** | +5 kg | +2.5 kg | +1–1.25 kg, or reset |
| **Deadlift** | +5 kg (sometimes more for very light starters) | +2.5–5 kg | +2.5 kg, or reset |
| **Bench press** | +2.5 kg | +1–1.25 kg | +0.5–1 kg, or double progression |
| **Overhead press** | +1–2.5 kg | +0.5–1 kg | Double progression |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Upper-body lifts use smaller jumps because they are lighter: 2.5 kg is a much bigger percentage of a 50 kg bench press than of a 100 kg squat. Small change plates (0.25–1.25 kg) make this possible; if the gym has none, use double progression (for example 3 × 5 → 3 × 6 → 3 × 7, then add 2.5 kg).

:::tip[In plain words]
Start with weights that feel easy. Add about 5 kg to squats and deadlifts at first, then 2.5 kg. Add smaller amounts to the bench and overhead press, because they're lighter lifts. Once the weight starts getting hard, make the jumps smaller.
:::

## Female and lighter lifters

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis found that men and women gain **similar relative** muscle size and lower-body strength from the same program, and that women gained more relative upper-body strength in the studies analysed (Roberts et al., 2020). Most included studies were in untrained people.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In competition records of about 1,900 powerlifters, the rate of strength gain did not differ between men and women (Latella et al., 2020; Latella et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> What differs is the size of each jump compared with the weight on the bar. A 2.5 kg jump on a 40 kg squat is 6%; on a 100 kg squat it is 2.5%. Lighter lifters, including many women, many youth lifters and lifters in light weight classes, need **smaller jumps**, not a different kind of program. Rippetoe & Baker recommend small plates for exactly this reason.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> A useful check once the early easy weeks are over: keep jumps at roughly **1–3% of the working weight** per session. This is a coaching heuristic, not a research finding.

:::tip[In plain words]
Women and lighter lifters gain strength at the same pace as everyone else, compared with where they started. The difference is that a standard 2.5 kg jump is a bigger step for a lighter lift, so use smaller plates and smaller jumps.
:::

## When progress stalls

A **stall** is a missed rep on a working set, or technique breaking down before the reps are done.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Rippetoe & Baker's approach: first repeat the weight; if the stall persists, reduce the load (often by about 10%) and work back up, usually with smaller jumps. Check recovery before blaming the program: sleep, food and life stress are the usual causes of early stalls. A common version of the rules:

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Missed reps once | Repeat the same weight next session |
| Missed the same weight twice | Check sleep, food and stress; repeat once more |
| Missed the same weight three times | **Reset**: drop about 10%, build back with smaller jumps |
| Technique breaks down before reps are missed | Treat it as a miss; do not add load |
| Stalls on one lift only | Reset only that lift; keep progressing the others |

**After time off.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis found that stopping training reduces strength, with larger losses for longer breaks, for older people and for less-trained people (Bosquet et al., 2013). <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 14 young men, repeated 3-week breaks followed by 6 weeks of retraining gave the same 24-week gains as training straight through (Ogasawara et al., 2013); strength came back quickly. <Evidence level="practice" /> Practical rule: after a week off, resume at the last weights or slightly lighter; after 2–3 weeks, drop about 10%; after longer breaks, drop more and rebuild with normal novice jumps.

:::tip[In plain words]
If you miss reps, try the same weight again. If you miss it three times, take off about 10% and build back up with smaller jumps. Check your sleep and eating first: that's usually the problem. A short break won't erase your progress; it comes back fast.
:::

## When linear progression ends, and what comes next

<Evidence level="practice" /> Signs that the novice phase is over (Rippetoe & Baker):

- A lift has been **reset twice** and stalls again at about the same weight.
- Stalls come every week or two, even with good sleep and food.
- Single sessions no longer give enough recovery to beat the last one.

<Evidence level="practice" /> For most new lifters this happens after a few months, often on the bench press and overhead press first. Lifts can end at different times: keep squat and deadlift on LP while bench moves to weekly progression.

<Evidence level="emerging" /> In competitive powerlifters, the strongest men gained strength more slowly than the least strong (about 0.10 vs 0.21 kg per day of total), possibly a ceiling effect (Latella et al., 2020). The stronger the lifter, the slower the progress and the more structure training needs.

**What comes next:** **weekly progression**, where a week of varied sessions (heavy, light, medium) produces a new best. Program 4 below is one example. Planning beyond that, with blocks and periodization, is covered in Part 2, Chapter 2: When linear progression stops.

:::tip[In plain words]
When you keep failing the same weights even after taking weight off and building back up, the beginner phase is over. That's good news: it means you're stronger. Now you'll set new bests every week instead of every workout.
:::

## Sample programs

Loads are fixed kilograms, not RPE. Increments follow the table above. Warm-ups are not shown. The numbers show lifters who progress well; many will stall sooner, and the stall rules above handle that.

### Program 1: Classic 3-day novice LP (male novice, first 4 weeks)

**Lifter:** male, about 75 kg body weight, new to barbell training. Estimated maxes about squat 100 kg, bench press 60 kg, deadlift 120 kg. Starting weights are deliberately light.

**Workout A:** squat 3 × 5, bench press 3 × 5, deadlift 1 × 5.
**Workout B:** squat 3 × 5, overhead press 3 × 5, deadlift 1 × 5.
Alternate A and B on Monday, Wednesday and Friday (week 1: A–B–A; week 2: B–A–B).

| Week | Day | Workout | Squat 3 × 5 | Bench or press 3 × 5 | Deadlift 1 × 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mon | A | 60 kg | Bench 40 kg | 80 kg |
| 1 | Wed | B | 65 kg | Press 30 kg | 85 kg |
| 1 | Fri | A | 70 kg | Bench 42.5 kg | 90 kg |
| 2 | Mon | B | 75 kg | Press 32.5 kg | 95 kg |
| 2 | Wed | A | 77.5 kg | Bench 45 kg | 100 kg |
| 2 | Fri | B | 80 kg | Press 35 kg | 105 kg |
| 3 | Mon | A | 82.5 kg | Bench 47.5 kg | 107.5 kg |
| 3 | Wed | B | 85 kg | Press 36 kg | 110 kg |
| 3 | Fri | A | 87.5 kg | Bench 50 kg | 112.5 kg |
| 4 | Mon | B | 90 kg | Press 37 kg | 115 kg |
| 4 | Wed | A | 92.5 kg (5, 5, 4: missed) | Bench 51 kg | 117.5 kg |
| 4 | Fri | B | 92.5 kg (5, 5, 5: repeat made) | Press 38 kg | 120 kg |

**Why:** squat jumps fall from 5 kg to 2.5 kg after the first four sessions as the weight nears the lifter's real ability. Bench and press switch to 1 kg jumps once 2.5 kg becomes too big a step. In week 4, the missed squat is simply repeated, and the lifter makes it. Add chin-ups or rows (3 × 8–12) after week 2 if recovery is good.

### Program 2: Novice LP for a lighter lifter (female, 63 kg class, 6 weeks)

**Lifter:** female, about 62 kg body weight, new to barbell training. Estimated maxes about squat 70 kg, bench press 37.5 kg, deadlift 85 kg. Uses 0.5 kg change plates (1 kg jumps).

**Monday:** squat 3 × 5, bench press 3 × 5, lat pulldown 3 × 8–12.
**Wednesday:** squat 3 × 5, overhead press 3 × 5, deadlift 1 × 5.
**Friday:** squat 3 × 5, bench press 3 × 5, barbell row 3 × 8–12.

| Week | Squat 3 × 5 (Mon / Wed / Fri) | Bench 3 × 5 (Mon / Fri) | Deadlift 1 × 5 (Wed) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 40 / 42.5 / 45 kg | 25 / 26 kg | 55 kg | Easy start |
| 2 | 47.5 / 50 / 52.5 kg | 27 / 28 kg | 60 kg | |
| 3 | 55 / 57.5 / 60 kg | 29 / 30 kg | 65 kg | |
| 4 | 61 / 62 / 63 kg | 31 / 31 kg | 67.5 kg | Squat now +1 kg. Bench missed (5, 5, 4) Monday, repeated Friday (5, 4, 4) |
| 5 | 64 / 65 / 66 kg | 31 / 28 kg | 70 kg | Bench missed a third time Monday (5, 4, 3): **reset** ~10% on Friday |
| 6 | 67 / 68 / 69 kg | 29 / 30 kg | 72.5 kg | Bench builds back with 1 kg jumps |

Overhead press (Wednesday) starts at 20 kg and progresses by 0.5–1 kg per session.

**Why:** same structure as any novice program, but with smaller jumps. The bench press, the lightest competition lift, stalls first; only it is reset, while squat and deadlift keep going. Bench is trained twice a week because it is a competition lift; the overhead press is kept once a week for upper-body balance.

### Program 3: Novice LP with built-in variation (heavy–light–heavy, 4 weeks)

**Lifter:** the novice from Program 1, about two months in. Squat 3 × 5 at about 110 kg, bench 3 × 5 at 62.5 kg, deadlift 1 × 5 at 135 kg. Squatting heavy three times a week is getting hard to recover from, so Wednesday becomes a **light day** with variations.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load | How it progresses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 3 × 5 | Heavy | +2.5 kg per session |
| Mon | Bench press | 3 × 5 | Heavy | +1–1.25 kg per session |
| Mon | Deadlift | 1 × 5 | Heavy | +2.5 kg per week |
| Wed | Pause squat (2 s) | 3 × 3 | About 75% of Monday's squat | +2.5 kg per week |
| Wed | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 6–8 | Moderate | Double progression |
| Wed | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | Moderate | +2.5 kg when all reps are clean |
| Fri | Squat | 3 × 5 | Heavy | +2.5 kg per session |
| Fri | Bench press | 3 × 5 | Heavy | +1–1.25 kg per session |
| Fri | Chin-up or row | 3 × 8–12 | Body weight or moderate | Double progression |

| Week | Squat Mon / Fri | Pause squat Wed | Bench Mon / Fri | Deadlift Mon | Close-grip bench Wed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 110 / 112.5 kg | 85 kg | 62.5 / 63.75 kg | 135 kg | 50 kg × 6, 6, 6 |
| 2 | 115 / 117.5 kg | 87.5 kg | 65 / 66.25 kg | 137.5 kg | 50 kg × 8, 7, 6 |
| 3 | 120 / 122.5 kg | 90 kg | 67.5 / 68.75 kg | 140 kg | 50 kg × 8, 8, 8 |
| 4 | 125 kg (5, 5, 4) / 125 kg (5, 5, 5) | 92.5 kg | 70 kg (5, 5, 4) / 70 kg (5, 5, 5) | 142.5 kg | 52.5 kg × 6, 6, 6 |

**Why:** the light day keeps squat practice (and teaches control at the bottom) while giving the legs a break between the two heavy days. Rippetoe & Baker use a similar light squat day late in the novice phase. Variations add variety without slowing the main progression, and they progress by reps or small weekly jumps instead of every session.

### Program 4: The bridge to weekly progression (after LP ends)

**Lifter:** the same novice, a few weeks later. Squat 3 × 5 has been reset twice and stalled again at about 125 kg; bench at about 70 kg. Instead of a new best every session, the lifter now aims for a new best **once a week**. This is adapted from the "Texas Method" structure (Rippetoe & Baker): a **volume day**, a **light day** and an **intensity day**.

| Day | Squat | Bench press | Deadlift |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon (volume) | 5 × 5 at about 90% of Friday's target | 5 × 5 at about 90% of Friday's target | — |
| Wed (light) | 2 × 5 at about 80% of Monday's load | 3 × 5 light (or overhead press 3 × 5) | — |
| Fri (intensity) | 1 × 5, a new 5-rep best | 1 × 5, a new 5-rep best | 1 × 5, a new 5-rep best |

| Week | Squat Mon / Wed / Fri | Bench Mon / Fri | Deadlift Fri |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 × 5 @ 115 / 2 × 5 @ 92.5 / 1 × 5 @ 127.5 kg | 5 × 5 @ 64 / 1 × 5 @ 71 kg | 150 kg |
| 2 | 117.5 / 95 / 130 kg | 65 / 72 kg | 152.5 kg |
| 3 | 120 / 95 / 132.5 kg | 66 / 73 kg | 155 kg |
| 4 | 122.5 / 97.5 / 135 kg (4 reps: missed) | 66 / 74 kg | 157.5 kg |
| 5 | Repeat 122.5 / 97.5 / 135 kg; if missed again, cut Monday to 4 × 5 for a week | 66.5 / 75 kg | 160 kg |

**Why:** the Monday volume drives adaptation, Wednesday maintains practice while recovering, and Friday shows the result. Progress is now about 2.5 kg per week on squat and deadlift and about 1 kg per week on bench, much slower than LP but steady. Once this also stalls, the lifter is ready for the block-based programming in Part 2.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting too heavy.** It burns through the easiest weeks of progress and causes early stalls. Start light and let the jumps do the work.
- **Using the same jumps for every lift and every lifter.** Bench, overhead press and lighter lifters need smaller jumps.
- **Adding exercises and volume too early.** Novices progress on little volume. Extra work mostly adds soreness and fatigue.
- **Grinding through ugly reps to "make" the weight.** A rep with broken technique is a missed rep.
- **Resetting without checking recovery.** Most early stalls come from poor sleep, too little food or high life stress.
- **Staying on LP after it has ended.** Resetting again and again to the same stall point wastes months. Move to weekly progression.
- **Leaving LP too early.** A single missed session is not the end of the novice phase.

## Key takeaways

- A novice recovers between sessions and can add load every workout; that is the whole basis of linear progression.
- Untrained lifters gain strength fast, partly through skill and partly through early muscle growth, but the rate slows over months.
- Squat every session, alternate bench and overhead press, deadlift one heavy set; 3 days per week.
- Jump sizes depend on the lift and the lifter: about 2.5–5 kg for squat and deadlift, 0.5–2.5 kg for presses, and smaller for lighter and many female lifters.
- On a stall: repeat, then reset about 10% and rebuild. After two resets on a lift, move that lift to weekly progression.
- Men and women gain strength at similar relative rates; lighter lifters need smaller jumps, not a different program.
