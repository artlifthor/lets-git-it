## In one minute

The body moves in three planes: **sagittal** (forward and back), **frontal** (side to side) and **transverse** (rotation). The squat, bench press and deadlift move the bar almost entirely in the sagittal plane; apart from the bench's horizontal adduction at the shoulder, the muscles that work in the other two planes mostly just **hold position**, without moving through their full range. That leaves some qualities under-trained: hip abductors and adductors through range, anti-rotation and side-bending strength of the trunk, rotator cuff and shoulder-blade control, and single-leg stability. These gaps show up as **knee cave, hip shift, a tilting bar or an uneven bench lockout**, and a few low-fatigue accessories (lateral lunges, Copenhagen planks, Pallof presses, suitcase carries, single-leg Romanian deadlifts, band external rotations) can address them. The evidence is honest but limited: it supports these exercises for building the targeted strength and, in other sports, for fewer groin problems (one large trial) and mixed results for shoulder problems, but it does **not** show that they add kilos to the competition lifts on their own.

## The three planes, simply

Imagine three panes of glass cutting through a lifter standing upright. Each pane is a **plane**, and each one has its own family of movements.

| Plane | The pane of glass… | Movements in this plane | Everyday example | Gym examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sagittal** | Splits the body into left and right halves | Flexion and extension (bending and straightening hips, knees, elbows, spine; raising the arm forward) | Walking, climbing stairs, standing up from a chair | Squat, deadlift, bench press (bar path), row, lunge, Romanian deadlift, leg curl |
| **Frontal** | Splits the body into front and back halves | Abduction and adduction (moving a limb away from or toward the midline); side-bending of the trunk; tilting the pelvis up on one side | Stepping sideways, carrying a heavy bag in one hand | Lateral lunge, side-lying hip abduction, Copenhagen plank, side plank, lateral raise, suitcase carry |
| **Transverse** | Splits the body into top and bottom halves | Rotation (trunk, hip, shoulder); turning the arm out or in; horizontal movements of the arm across the chest | Turning to look behind you, throwing, swinging a bat | Landmine rotation, Pallof press (resisting rotation), band external rotation, cable chop, hip internal/external rotation drills |

### Sagittal plane: forward and back

Bending and straightening. In the squat and deadlift the hips and knees bend and straighten; in the bench press the elbows bend and straighten and the bar travels up and down. This is where nearly all of a powerlifter's training volume goes.

:::tip[In plain words]
This is bending and straightening, like sitting down and standing up. Squats, deadlifts and the up-and-down of the bench all happen here, so you already train it a lot.
:::

### Frontal plane: side to side

Moving a leg or arm out to the side and back in, and bending the trunk sideways. In the lifts, these muscles mostly **stop** sideways movement: the hip abductors keep the knees from caving and the pelvis level; the trunk keeps the shoulders level under the bar.

:::tip[In plain words]
This is moving sideways, like stepping to the side or leaning over to one side. In the big lifts these muscles mostly keep you from tipping or caving in, rather than moving you.
:::

### Transverse plane: rotation

Twisting the trunk, turning the hip or shoulder in or out. In the lifts, these muscles mostly **resist** rotation: the rotator cuff keeps the upper arm centred in the shoulder socket during the bench, the hip rotators help keep the knees out, and the trunk resists twisting when a load is slightly uneven.

:::tip[In plain words]
This is twisting, like turning to look behind you. In the big lifts these muscles mostly stop you from twisting, for example keeping your shoulders steady on the bench.
:::

### Real lifts mix planes

Real movements mix planes. A squat is mainly sagittal, but the hips also move slightly out to the side and rotate outward as the knees track over the toes. So "the squat and deadlift are sagittal lifts" means their main joint actions and bar path are sagittal. The bench press is a mix: the bar moves in the sagittal plane, but the shoulder's main action is horizontal adduction (transverse plane), trained hard and through full range. What the bench leaves out is shoulder **rotation** (turning the upper arm in and out), which the cuff only holds.

<Evidence level="practice" /> A detailed clinical review of hip function organises the 21 muscles that cross the hip by the three planes, and stresses that many of them act in more than one plane at once, depending on the hip's position (Neumann, 2010). The gluteus maximus, for example, extends the hip (sagittal) and also rotates it outward (transverse); the adductor magnus pulls the thigh inward (frontal) and is also a hip extensor, especially when the hip is deeply bent, as at the bottom of a squat.

:::tip[In plain words]
Your body can move forward and back, side to side, and twist. Every lift uses all three directions a little; "mostly forward and back" just means that's where the bar and the big joints move. The side-to-side and twisting muscles are working in those lifts, but mostly to hold you steady, not to move you.
:::

## What the squat, bench and deadlift leave under-trained

In the competition lifts, most frontal- and transverse-plane muscles work **isometrically** (the bench's horizontal adduction is the main exception): they hold the knees, pelvis, trunk and shoulder blades in place while the hips, knees and elbows do the moving. That is useful training, but it is not the same as moving those muscles through their range or challenging them one side at a time.

| Quality | What it does in SBD | Why SBD may not develop it fully |
|---|---|---|
| **Hip abductors and outward rotators** (gluteus medius and minimus, deep rotators) | Keep the knees tracking over the toes; keep the pelvis level; "spread the floor" in the squat and sumo pull | Work as holders at mid-range; rarely trained through range or on one leg |
| **Hip adductors** (adductor magnus, longus, brevis) | Help extend the hip out of the bottom of the squat and sumo deadlift | Deep and wide-stance squats already train them well as hip extensors; strength in **adduction itself**, and at long lengths with the leg out to the side, gets little direct work |
| **Obliques and anti-rotation strength** | Keep the trunk square when the bar, the stance or the grip is slightly uneven | Bilateral, symmetrical loading rarely tries to twist the trunk hard |
| **Lateral trunk** (quadratus lumborum, obliques) | Stop the trunk side-bending under a tilted bar or uneven push | Same: loads are symmetrical by design |
| **Rotator cuff and shoulder-blade control** | Hold the upper arm centred in the socket and the shoulder blades set during the bench press | The cuff works hard as a stabiliser but is rarely trained through rotation (horizontal adduction is already well trained by benching); pressing volume usually far exceeds pulling and rotation work |
| **Single-leg stability** | Keep each leg doing its share in a two-legged lift | A stronger side can quietly do more work without the lifter noticing |

<Evidence level="emerging" /> In 17 men, full-depth squats increased adductor and gluteus maximus volume more than half squats (Kubo et al., 2019). So "the adductors are under-trained" is only partly true for a lifter who squats deep: they get plenty of hip-extension work, but little frontal-plane work.

:::tip[In plain words]
The big lifts keep your side-to-side and twisting muscles busy holding position, but never really train them on their own. One-leg strength, hip muscles that push your knees out, your side-of-belly muscles (obliques) and the small muscles that hold your shoulder in place (rotator cuff) are the usual gaps.
:::

## What the evidence says

The honest summary first: most research on frontal-plane, rotational and single-leg training comes from **team-sport athletes, students and rehabilitation**, not powerlifters. It tells us what these exercises build and, in some sports, what they prevent. It does not tell us that they raise a powerlifting total.

### Does single-leg training build two-legged strength?

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 14 studies (392 participants aged 16–26) found that **two-legged training produced a small advantage for two-legged strength**, and one-legged training a large advantage for one-legged jumping. Differences in one-legged strength were not significant. The authors rated overall study quality as moderate (Liao et al., 2022). This is specificity at work: you get best at what you practise.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 33 developmental rugby players with about 5 years of training, 8 weeks of either back squats or step-ups (volume-matched) improved both exercises: each group improved most in its own exercise, with transfer to the other. The difference in squat gains between groups was unclear (Appleby et al., 2019).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 18 academy rugby players, 5 weeks of rear-foot-elevated split squats improved back squat 1RM similarly to back squat training (Speirs et al., 2016). In 38 untrained students, one-legged and two-legged training were equally effective for both kinds of leg strength over 8 weeks (McCurdy et al., 2005).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 14 resistance-trained men, unilateral squats used far lighter loads than bilateral squats. Quadriceps, biceps femoris and lower-back muscle activity was higher in the bilateral squat, though the authors suggested the unilateral version reached similar activity in several hip, hamstring, calf and abdominal muscles with less spinal load (Eliassen et al., 2018).

**What this means.** <Evidence level="practice" /> For a powerlifter, single-leg work does not replace the squat; the squat is the most specific way to build the squat. Single-leg work is a **low-spinal-load accessory** that trains each leg on its own, challenges the hip stabilisers, and still builds leg strength.

:::tip[In plain words]
One-leg exercises make your legs stronger and can even help your squat a little, but squatting is still the best way to get a better squat. Use one-leg work as an extra, not a replacement.
:::

### Do side-to-side differences matter?

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 18 studies found that differences in strength between the legs **may** hurt jumping, kicking and cycling, while findings for other qualities were inconsistent. All of the evidence was associative, with no randomised trials (Bishop et al., 2018).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 18 recreationally trained men and women, even symmetrical-looking squats had sizeable left-right differences in joint torques: on average about 12% at the hip, 13% at the knee and 17% at the ankle, with different patterns in different people. Few individuals matched the group average, and none was symmetrical at all three joints (Flanagan & Salem, 2007).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 28 trained lifters, those who stood with uneven weight distribution also squatted with more uneven force between the feet and **more tilting and rotation of the bar**, at both 60% and 75% of 1RM (Sato & Heise, 2012).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 31 recreationally trained men, 6 weeks of either bilateral or unilateral training increased isometric squat force, and **neither reduced left-right asymmetry** on average. In the subgroup with marked asymmetry, bilateral training tended to reduce it more (Lynch et al., 2023).

**What this means.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Some asymmetry is normal in every lifter and does not need fixing. It matters when it is **visible and getting bigger** (hip shift, bar tilt), when it changes with load, or when it comes with pain on one side. And single-leg work is not a guaranteed asymmetry fix: in the one trial that looked, it didn't reduce asymmetry better than bilateral training.

:::tip[In plain words]
Nobody is perfectly even, so a little difference between your sides is fine. Worry about it when you can see it, like your hips sliding to one side or the bar tipping, or when one side hurts.
:::

### Do strong hip abductors stop the knees caving in?

This is the most popular idea in this area, and the evidence is weaker than its popularity.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review in healthy women found **conflicting** results: weaker hips were linked with more knee valgus (knees moving inward) in eight studies, stronger hips in three, and no link in five. Pooled data showed a link in single-leg landing, but **not** in double-leg landing or single-leg squats (Dix et al., 2019). The link seems to depend on the task.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 17 studies found that people with dynamic knee valgus tended to have **less ankle dorsiflexion** (how far the knee can travel forward over the foot) (Lima et al., 2018). Restricted ankles are a possible contributor, not only weak hips.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 66 female recreational athletes, 9 weeks of strength training for the quads, hamstrings, gluteus medius and gluteus maximus made them stronger but **did not change** knee or hip mechanics during a jump-stop task (Herman et al., 2008). Getting stronger did not, by itself, change how they moved.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 10 exercise studies (423 participants) found that 7 programmes reduced dynamic knee valgus; these focused on muscles that cross the knee, such as the hamstrings and calves. The authors suggested the three that did not may have failed because they targeted trunk and back muscles instead (Sahabuddin et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 21 active adults, side-lying hip abduction produced the highest gluteus medius activity of 12 exercises, while single-leg squats and single-leg deadlifts produced the highest gluteus maximus activity along with high gluteus medius activity (Distefano et al., 2009). Muscle activity is a guide to which exercise works a muscle, not proof that it will make that muscle stronger.

**What this means.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Hip abductor work is reasonable for a lifter whose knees cave, but **strength gains alone do not reliably change a movement pattern** (one trial found no change, though most exercise programmes in a review did reduce valgus); pair hip work with practice of the pattern itself (cues, tempo and pause squats at moderate loads) and a check of ankle mobility. Expect better tolerance and control, not an automatic fix.

:::tip[In plain words]
Stronger hips can help with knees caving in, but they don't fix it on their own. You also need to practise squatting with your knees in the right place, and check that your ankles bend enough.
:::

### Does adductor and groin work prevent problems?

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 24 under-19 footballers, 8 weeks of the Copenhagen adduction exercise (the "Copenhagen plank") increased eccentric hip adduction strength by about 36% compared with no change in the control group, with little soreness (Ishøi et al., 2016).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In a cluster-randomised trial of 652 semi-professional male footballers, a programme built on that single exercise (three times a week in pre-season, once a week in season) cut the prevalence of groin problems from 21% to 14%, a **41% lower risk** (Harøy et al., 2019).

**What this means.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Footballers kick, sprint and change direction, so the injury result does not transfer directly to powerlifting. But the strength result is robust, the exercise is cheap, and lifters with wide squat stances or sumo deadlifts load their adductors at long lengths. That makes the Copenhagen plank a sensible joint-health accessory for those lifters, not a proven injury preventer in powerlifting.

:::tip[In plain words]
A short, simple exercise for the inner thighs made footballers much stronger there and cut their groin problems by about 40%. Powerlifting is different, but if you squat wide or pull sumo, it's an easy way to keep your groin strong.
:::

### Does rotator cuff and shoulder work prevent shoulder problems?

- <Evidence level="practice" /> The shoulder is one of the most commonly injured areas in powerlifters. In a survey of 104 Swedish powerlifters, the lower back and pelvis, shoulder and hip were the most commonly injured areas (Strömbäck et al., 2018); a systematic review of weightlifting and powerlifting injuries also named the spine, shoulder and knee (Aasa et al., 2017). Surveys describe how common injuries are; they don't tell us how to prevent them.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In a cluster-randomised trial in 660 elite handball players, a warm-up programme for shoulder rotation range, external rotation strength and shoulder-blade strength, done 3 times a week, gave a **28% lower risk** of shoulder problems over a season (Andersson et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> But a later cluster-randomised trial in 579 handball players found that a band-and-stretching programme **did not** reduce overuse shoulder injuries (Achenbach et al., 2022).

**What this means.** <Evidence level="practice" /> The evidence is **mixed**, and it comes from a throwing sport. There is no trial showing that rotator cuff work prevents shoulder pain in bench pressers. Coaches still use it because it is cheap, low-fatigue and balances a pressing-heavy week, and because it builds strength in a rotation that the bench press only holds. Treat it as sensible maintenance, not protection.

:::tip[In plain words]
Shoulder exercises with bands helped handball players in one big study and didn't help in another. For bench pressers, they're a cheap, easy extra that balances all your pressing, but they're not a guarantee against shoulder pain.
:::

### Does "core" and anti-rotation training add to performance?

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 24 studies concluded that targeted core stability training provides only **marginal** benefits to athletic performance; most studies used it alongside other training, and results were mixed (Reed et al., 2012).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> A laboratory study of strongman events (including the farmer's walk, suitcase carry and yoke walk) combined muscle-activity recordings with a model of the trunk. In the yoke walk, the hips could not produce enough side-to-side (abduction) torque on their own, so trunk muscles such as the quadratus lumborum made up the difference by stiffening the trunk and supporting the pelvis. The authors suggested that loaded carries challenge abilities that the traditional lifts do not, and could add to them (McGill et al., 2009).

**What this means.** <Evidence level="practice" /> The squat and deadlift already demand enormous trunk strength in the sagittal plane, which is why heavy compound lifting is the main "core training" for a powerlifter. Anti-rotation and side-bending exercises fill a smaller gap: resisting a trunk that wants to twist or tip under an uneven load. Keep them short and simple.

:::tip[In plain words]
Squats and deadlifts already train your trunk hard. Extra "core" exercises add only a little, so keep them simple: a few sets of exercises where you stop your body from twisting or leaning to one side.
:::

## Where frontal and transverse weaknesses show up in the lifts

Not every visible asymmetry is a frontal-plane strength problem. Before reaching for an accessory, rule out **technique and setup** (uneven grip, stance or bar position), **mobility** (one hip or ankle moves less), and **pain** (a lifter avoids loading a sore side). <Evidence level="practice" /> A useful test is the one from [Part 3 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/advanced/05-weak-point-analysis/): if the fault appears at light loads too, it is probably technique or mobility; if it appears only when heavy, strength is more likely.

**Film from the front or behind**, not only from the side. Most powerlifting video is shot side-on, which hides exactly the faults this chapter is about.

| Fault | Plane | What you see | Common causes to check | First fixes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Knee cave** (valgus) in the squat | Frontal / transverse | Knees move inward, usually just out of the bottom | Cueing and practice; ankle dorsiflexion; stance width and toe angle; adductors helping hip extension under heavy load; hip abductor strength | Cues ("knees over toes"), tempo and pause squats at moderate loads, ankle mobility, hip abduction and lateral lunges |
| **Hip shift** in the squat | Frontal | Hips slide toward one side on the way up; bar may tilt | Uneven stance or bar placement; one hip or ankle less mobile; one leg weaker; pain on one side | Check setup; mobility on the restricted side; single-leg work starting on the weaker side; tempo squats |
| **Bar tilt or twist** in the squat | Frontal / transverse | One end of the bar lower or further forward | Uneven hand position or bar placement; uneven weight on the feet; upper-back tightness on one side | Mark grip and bar position; check weight distribution; anti-rotation and carry work |
| **Hip shift or twist** in the deadlift | Frontal / transverse | Hips rotate toward one side as the bar leaves the floor; more common with a mixed grip | Mixed grip (one palm up, one down) pulling the bar unevenly; uneven stance; one-sided weakness | Check stance and grip; alternate which hand is supinated in training; suitcase carries, single-leg RDLs |
| **Uneven bench lockout** | Frontal / transverse | One arm locks out later; bar tilts | Uneven grip width; shoulder blade not set on one side; one-sided triceps or shoulder weakness; shoulder pain | Mark grip, re-check setup; single-arm dumbbell pressing; rotator cuff and shoulder-blade work |
| **Elbow flares unevenly** in the bench | Transverse | One elbow drifts out more on the way down | Shoulder rotation control; habit | Pause bench, single-arm dumbbell press, band external rotation |
| **Trunk leans to one side** | Frontal | Shoulders not level under the bar | Uneven grip or stance; lateral trunk weakness; one-sided pain | Suitcase carries, side planks; check setup |

<Evidence level="practice" /> The causes listed are coaching hypotheses. The only well-supported links from this chapter's studies are that **uneven weight distribution goes with more bar tilt and rotation** in the squat (Sato & Heise, 2012) and that **ankle dorsiflexion is associated with knee valgus** in general movement tasks (Lima et al., 2018).

### A closer look at knee cave

- <Evidence level="practice" /> A small, brief inward movement of the knees as a heavy squat leaves the bottom, which then returns outward, is common among strong lifters and does not by itself mean danger or weakness. Persistent or worsening cave, cave that is larger on one side, cave at light loads, or cave with knee pain are the patterns worth working on.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> In the deep squat the adductors help extend the hip (Neumann, 2010). A lifter who caves out of the hole may simply be using the adductors hard. That is one reason why adductor strength work, not just abductor work, belongs in the plan.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Check ankle dorsiflexion before blaming the hips. If one or both ankles are restricted, raised-heel squat shoes or ankle mobility work may change the pattern more than any hip exercise. Mobility tests are covered in [Chapter 8: Mobility and movement preparation](/lets-git-it/technique-injury/08-mobility-movement-prep/).

### A closer look at hip shift

- <Evidence level="practice" /> Work through the checklist in order: (1) **setup**: feet even, toes at the same angle, bar centred; (2) **mobility**: compare hip flexion, hip rotation and ankle dorsiflexion side to side; (3) **strength**: compare split-squat or single-leg RDL performance side to side; (4) **pain**: ask directly. Only (3) is a strength problem.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Pain that makes a lifter shift away from one side is a reason to see a qualified health professional, not to add accessories. Programming around pain is covered in Part 5, Chapter 10 (Programming around injury).

:::tip[In plain words]
Film your lifts from the front and back, not just the side. If your knees cave or your hips slide, check your setup and your ankle and hip movement first. Only then decide it's a strength problem and add exercises for it.
:::

### How much, and where it goes

- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Dose:** most lifters need **2–4 of these exercises per session, on two to four days a week**, 2–3 sets each, about 10–15 minutes per session. Add more only when a specific fault has been diagnosed.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Placement:** light drills (band external rotations, side-lying abduction, Pallof presses) fit in the **warm-up** if they don't tire the muscles needed for the main lift. Heavier ones (split squats, single-leg RDLs, heavy carries, Copenhagen planks) go **after** the main work.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Scheduling:** don't put hard single-leg work or Copenhagen planks the day before a heavy squat or deadlift. Single-leg work can leave the legs sore for a day or two when it is new; start Copenhagen planks on the short lever and progress gradually, as in the trials, where soreness stayed low.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Progression:** use double progression (more reps, then more load) for loaded exercises, and longer holds or a longer lever for planks and carries. For single-leg exercises, **train the weaker side first** and match the reps on the stronger side, rather than letting the strong side run ahead.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **How to judge it:** after 6–8 weeks, compare side-to-side reps on a single-leg test, holds on the Copenhagen and side plank, and front-on video of the competition lifts. Expect the accessory numbers to improve first; changes in the lifts, if they come, are slower.

:::tip[In plain words]
You need only a few of these exercises: two to four per session, a few sets each. Do the light ones in your warm-up and the harder ones after your main lifts. On one-leg exercises, start with your weaker side.
:::

## Sample programs

### Program 1: Add-on accessory block for a 4-day powerlifting week

**Lifter:** intermediate, male, 83 kg, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. No specific fault; the goal is general balance and joint health in a 6-week hypertrophy-oriented block. The main lifts stay as programmed; only the add-ons are shown.

**Weekly layout:** Mon squat + bench · Tue bench (light) + upper back · Thu deadlift + bench · Sat squat + bench. Heavy squat (Mon), heavy deadlift (Thu) and heavy squat (Sat) are 72 h and 48 h apart.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load | Placement and job |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Band external rotation | 2 × 15 per side | RPE 6–7 | Bench warm-up; rotator cuff |
| Mon | Pallof press | 2 × 10 per side, 2 s hold | RPE 7–8 | After main work; anti-rotation |
| Tue | Single-arm dumbbell row | 3 × 10 per side | RPE 7–8 | After bench; shoulder-blade control |
| Tue | Face pull with external rotation | 2 × 15 | RPE 8 | After rows; rear shoulder, cuff |
| Tue | Rear-foot-elevated split squat | 3 × 8 per side | RPE 7–8 | End of session; single-leg strength (48 h before Thu) |
| Thu | Suitcase carry | 3 × 25 m per side | Heavy, upright | After deadlifts; lateral trunk |
| Thu | Side-lying hip abduction | 2 × 15 per side | RPE 7–8 | End of session; hip abductors |
| Sat | Copenhagen plank (short lever) | 2 × 20 s per side | Hard but controlled | After squats; adductors (Sat → Mon is 48 h) |
| Sat | Band external rotation | 2 × 15 per side | RPE 6–7 | Bench warm-up |

**Progression:** add reps or load each week on rows, split squats and carries; add 5 s to holds; move the Copenhagen plank to the long lever when 2 × 30 s is easy. **Week 6 deload:** halve the sets (21 add-on sets → about 11; for example 3 sets → 1–2, 2 sets → 1) and keep loads the same.

**Why it's built this way.** Each plane gets one or two exercises across the week, nothing is done to failure, and the heaviest add-ons (split squats, carries, Copenhagen planks) sit at least 48 h before the next heavy squat or deadlift. Total extra time is about 10 minutes per session.

### Program 2: Lifter with a squat hip shift

**Lifter:** female, 63 kg class, squat 120 kg, bench press 65 kg, deadlift 150 kg. Front-on video shows her hips sliding to the **right** out of the hole on sets above RPE 8; the bar tilts slightly lower on the left. At RPE 6 the shift is small.

**Assessment (in order):**

| Check | Finding |
|---|---|
| Setup | Feet even, bar centred (checked with tape marks) |
| Mobility | Knee-to-wall test: left ankle clearly less range than the right; hip rotation similar |
| Strength | Rear-foot-elevated split squat, 8RM: right 22.5 kg dumbbells total, left 17.5 kg |
| Pain | None |

**One-sentence diagnosis:** the shift moves weight toward the stronger, more mobile right side; the left leg is weaker and the left ankle restricted. (A shift *toward* the right often means the right side is doing more work, but check: it can also be a way of avoiding a sore or stiff side.)

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Ankle mobility (knee-to-wall rocks), left side extra | 2 × 10 | — | Before squatting |
| Mon | Squat | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) | Front-on video on the last set |
| Mon | Tempo squat (3-1-0) | 2 × 4 | RPE 6–7 | Practise an even descent and drive |
| Mon | Rear-foot-elevated split squat | 3 × 8 per side | RPE 8 (left side) | Left side first; right matches left's reps and load |
| Wed | Single-leg Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 per side | RPE 7–8 | Left side first |
| Wed | Copenhagen plank (short lever) | 2 × 20 s per side | — | Adductors |
| Wed | Pallof press | 2 × 10 per side | RPE 7–8 | Anti-rotation |
| Fri | Ankle mobility, left side extra | 2 × 10 | — | Before squatting |
| Fri | Squat | 4 × 3 | RPE 7.5 (~102.5 kg) | Front-on video |
| Fri | Side-lying hip abduction | 2 × 15 per side | RPE 7–8 | Left side first |

Deadlifts are trained on Wednesday before the accessories (not shown), 48 h from both squat days. Bench work is not shown.

**Progression:** split squats and single-leg RDLs by double progression, always led by the left side. **Re-test after 6 weeks:** split-squat 8RM each side, ankle test, and front-on video of a set at RPE 8–8.5. **Success:** smaller side-to-side split-squat gap, more left-ankle range, and visibly less shift at the same RPE. **If the shift is unchanged** after a full block, re-check setup and mobility and consider a qualified professional's assessment; some shifts are structural (for example hip shape) and are best managed, not "fixed".

**Why it's built this way.** The plan treats the two likely causes (restricted left ankle, weaker left leg) directly, keeps the competition squat as the main work, and adds tempo squats to practise the even pattern. Leading with the weaker side stops the strong side from doing extra work in the accessories too.

### Program 3: The minimal 15-minutes-a-week version

For a busy lifter, or during a strength block when there's no time for much else. Two short circuits a week, done after the main lifts on any two non-consecutive days that are not the day before a heavy squat or deadlift. Rest about 30–60 s between exercises.

| Circuit | Exercise | Sets × reps | Plane |
|---|---|---|---|
| A (about 7 min) | Single-leg Romanian deadlift | 2 × 8 per side | Single-leg |
| A | Pallof press | 2 × 10 per side | Transverse |
| A | Band external rotation | 2 × 15 per side | Transverse (shoulder) |
| B (about 8 min) | Suitcase carry | 2 × 25 m per side | Frontal |
| B | Copenhagen plank (short lever) | 2 × 20 s per side | Frontal |
| B | Side-lying hip abduction | 2 × 15 per side | Frontal |

Do circuit A in one session and circuit B in the other, so each session has three exercises and takes about 7–8 minutes (about 15 minutes a week). **Per session:** 6 sets (12 per week), mostly low-fatigue. If you have time for both circuits in the same session, that is fine too, but it is not needed. In the last 2–3 weeks before a meet, cut to circuit A once a week or drop it entirely.

**Why it's built this way.** One exercise for each main gap: single-leg stability, anti-rotation, rotator cuff, lateral trunk, adductors and abductors. The circuit format saves time because the exercises use different muscles.

## Common mistakes

- **Filming only from the side.** Knee cave, hip shift and bar tilt are frontal-plane faults; film from the front or behind.
- **Treating every asymmetry as a problem.** Some left-right difference is normal; act on faults that are visible, growing, or painful.
- **Blaming weak glutes for every knee cave.** Check cues, stance and ankle range too; hip strength alone often doesn't change the pattern.
- **Expecting accessories to add kilos to the total.** Their job is control, balance and tissue tolerance; there is no evidence that they raise the total directly.
- **Replacing the squat with single-leg work.** Two-legged training builds two-legged strength best.
- **Letting the strong side lead.** On single-leg exercises, start with the weaker side and match it.
- **Turning "balance work" into a second workout.** Two to four exercises per session is enough for most lifters.
- **Using accessories to treat pain.** One-sided pain that changes how a lifter moves needs a qualified health professional.

## Key takeaways

- The body moves in three planes; the competition lifts are mostly sagittal and, apart from the bench's horizontal adduction, train the frontal and transverse planes mainly as stabilisers.
- The usual gaps are hip abductors and adductors through range, anti-rotation and lateral trunk strength, rotator cuff and shoulder-blade control, and single-leg stability.
- Single-leg training builds strength but does not beat bilateral training for bilateral strength, and it has not been shown to fix asymmetry better.
- The link between hip strength and knee cave is inconsistent; combine hip work with movement practice and an ankle check.
- Adductor (Copenhagen) and shoulder programmes reduced problems in some team-sport trials; evidence in powerlifting is lacking and the shoulder evidence is mixed.
- Film from the front, rule out setup, mobility and pain first, then choose two to four accessories per session, 2–3 sets each, on two to four days a week.
