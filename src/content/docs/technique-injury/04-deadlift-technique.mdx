## In one minute

The deadlift is the simplest competition lift to describe, but position is easy to lose: the bar starts on the floor and there is no lowering phase to set up the pull. Lifters compete with one of two styles: **conventional** (feet about hip width, hands outside the legs), which uses a more inclined trunk and moves the bar further, or **sumo** (wide stance, hands inside the legs), which uses a more upright trunk, more knee and hip-rotation demand, and a shorter bar path. Research shows clear mechanical differences between the styles but no evidence that either one is stronger for everyone, so the best way to choose is to test both. Good technique for either style means a consistent setup, the bar close to the body, a back position that stays the same from floor to lockout, and a finish that meets the IPF rules.

## What the deadlift asks of the lifter

The deadlift is mainly a **hip extension** lift, with knee extension doing most of its work early, off the floor.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 25 lifters filmed at a regional powerlifting championship, the squat and the conventional deadlift differed clearly in hip, knee and ankle angles at their sticking points. The authors described the squat as a **simultaneous** movement (hips and knees extend together) and the deadlift as a **sequential** one (knees extend first, then hips finish) (Hales et al., 2009).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Because there is no eccentric phase before the pull, the lifter cannot use a stretch at the bottom to help, and the setup decides much of the outcome. Most failures happen off the floor or around the knees (see [Part 3 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/advanced/05-weak-point-analysis/)).

:::tip[In plain words]
In a deadlift your legs push the bar off the floor and then your hips finish the job. You start from a dead stop, so how you set up before the bar moves matters more than in any other lift.
:::

## Conventional vs. sumo: what the research shows

### The mechanics

| Feature | Conventional | Sumo | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stance width | ~32 cm | ~70 cm | Escamilla et al., 2000 |
| Feet turned out | ~14° | ~42° | Escamilla et al., 2000 |
| Grip width | ~55 cm | ~47 cm (hands inside legs) | Escamilla et al., 2000 |
| Trunk at lift-off | More inclined | 5–10° more upright | Escamilla et al., 2000 |
| Vertical bar distance and work | ~25–40% greater | Less | Escamilla et al., 2000 |
| Muscles emphasised | Hamstrings, calves, upper back | Quadriceps (vastus medialis/lateralis); more sideways and rotational hip and knee moments | See below |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Coaches also expect more adductor demand in sumo; the EMG studies above did not show a style difference for the adductors.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 24 national-championship powerlifters (12 sumo, 12 conventional), the sumo lifters used a more upright trunk and thigh, and the conventional lifters moved the bar further, did about 25–40% more mechanical work, and produced different knee and ankle moments. **Hip moments did not differ significantly** between styles (Escamilla et al., 2000).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 57 lifters at a national championship, a computer model estimated that the sumo style produced about **10% less moment and 8% less shear force** at the lower back (L4/L5) than the conventional style (Cholewicki et al., 1991). This is a model estimate, not a measured injury difference.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 30 experienced men lifting 85% of 1RM, the conventional deadlift produced higher hip extension moments and more hamstring (biceps femoris) activity. The sumo deadlift involved more sideways and rotational movement and moment at the hip and knee, and more vastus lateralis activity early in the lift (Hanen et al., 2025). This finding on hip moments differs from the competition data above, so treat the hip question as **unsettled**.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 13 collegiate football players lifting a 12-rep-max load, the sumo deadlift produced more quadriceps (vastus medialis and lateralis) and tibialis anterior activity, and the conventional deadlift more calf (medial gastrocnemius) activity (Escamilla et al., 2002).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 11 trained women, 3-rep-max loads were almost identical for conventional and sumo (about 103 and 102 kg), and both styles produced larger hip moments near lockout than the hex-bar deadlift (Gundersen et al., 2025).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 24 men and women, the bar travelled a shorter distance in the sumo deadlift at every load. Bar speed at 1RM was the same for both styles, but differed at several submaximal loads (conventional was faster) (Kasovic et al., 2019). If you use velocity-based training, build a **separate load-velocity profile for each style**.

**What the evidence does not show:** no controlled trial has shown that one style produces a bigger deadlift for lifters in general, or that one style is safer. The differences are real but they are trade-offs, not a winner.

:::tip[In plain words]
Sumo shortens how far the bar travels and keeps your chest more upright, but it asks more of your quads and hips. Conventional moves the bar further and leans you over more, so it works your hamstrings and back harder. Neither is "cheating" and neither is best for everyone.
:::

### Who suits which style

<Evidence level="emerging" /> The popular belief that body proportions (arm, torso and leg lengths) decide the best style has little direct support. In 11 trained men, arm, torso and shank lengths were not clearly related to conventional deadlift mechanics (Keith et al., 2023). No study we could verify shows that measuring a lifter's limbs predicts which style will be stronger.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Coaches use these signs as **hints, not rules**:

| Lean towards sumo if… | Lean towards conventional if… |
|---|---|
| Hips abduct and rotate outward comfortably in a wide stance | Wide stances cause hip or groin discomfort |
| Strong quads and adductors; squat is relatively strong | Strong back and hamstrings; squat is relatively weak |
| Trunk tends to fold over badly off the floor in conventional | Sumo setup feels cramped and the lifter loses tightness |
| Short arms relative to torso (conventional forces a very low hip or very bent-over start) | Long arms (conventional start is naturally more upright) |
| Lower back gets irritated by heavy conventional pulling | Adductors or hips get irritated by heavy sumo pulling |

The reliable way to decide is to **train both for a block and compare** estimated 1RMs and how the body tolerates each (Program 1 below).

:::tip[In plain words]
There is no body-shape formula that tells you which style to use. Try both properly for several weeks, compare the numbers, and notice which one your hips and back put up with better.
:::

## Setup

<Evidence level="practice" /> A repeatable setup is the most coachable part of the deadlift. Use the same order every rep.

**Conventional**

1. Feet about hip width, toes slightly out, bar over the middle of the foot.
2. Hinge and grip the bar just outside the legs; arms vertical.
3. Bring the shins to the bar and the hips down only until the shins touch; do not squat the bar.
4. Brace: big breath into the belly, tighten the trunk (and belt, if worn).
5. Pull the slack out: pull up on the bar until it "clicks" against the plates, with the back tight and the lats engaged.
6. Push the floor away; the bar leaves the floor without a jerk.

**Sumo**

1. Stance wide enough that the shins are close to vertical at the start; toes turned out so knees track over feet.
2. Bar over the middle of the foot (often closer to the shins than in conventional).
3. Grip with hands roughly shoulder width, arms hanging straight down inside the knees.
4. Push the knees out over the toes and bring the hips towards the bar; chest up.
5. Brace and pull the slack out as above, then "spread the floor" with the feet as the bar breaks.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 10 experienced lifters doing isometric pulls, setting up with the bar over the middle of the foot (versus over the ball of the foot) produced more hamstring and upper-lower-back muscle activity and less quadriceps activity and knee moment. **Lower-back compression and shear forces did not differ** between the two setups (Edington et al., 2018).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 16 studies found that heavy deadlifts produce some of the highest intra-abdominal pressures recorded in resistance exercise, behind only the squat (Blazek et al., 2019). Bracing with a held breath (the Valsalva manoeuvre) is how lifters create this pressure. Lifters with high blood pressure or heart conditions should get medical advice on heavy breath-holding.

:::tip[In plain words]
Set up the same way every time: feet, grip, shins, breath, pull the slack out, then push. Start with the bar over the middle of your foot. Take a big breath and hold it to make your trunk rigid before the bar moves.
:::

## Bar path

<Evidence level="practice" /> The bar should move in a near-vertical line over the middle of the foot, staying in contact with or very close to the legs all the way up.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 40 lifters at the 1999 Special Olympics World Games (athletes with intellectual disabilities; skill and load were confounded), the more skilled lifters kept the bar closer to the body, with smaller hip and ankle moment arms and 15% less vertical bar distance (Escamilla et al., 2001). The authors suggested this improves performance and may reduce injury risk.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Every centimetre the bar drifts forward adds leverage against the hips and back. Common causes: the bar starting over the toes, the lats not engaged, or the hips rising first so the shoulders move forward of the bar.

**Filming tip:** film from the side, camera at hip height, level with the bar's end. Track the plate's centre. A good pull rises nearly straight; a forward hook off the floor or around the knees points to the faults covered in [Part 5 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/technique-injury/05-technical-faults/).

:::tip[In plain words]
Drag the bar up your legs in a straight line. If it swings away from you, it gets much heavier, even though the plates haven't changed.
:::

## Back position: neutral, flexion, and what the evidence really says

This topic attracts strong opinions. Here is what the research supports.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> Heavy deadlifts usually involve **some** spinal flexion. In 4 national-class powerlifters filmed with X-ray video while pulling 184–211 kg, the spine flexed during the lifts but, in all but one of the lifts, the lumbar spine stayed 1.5–13° short of the lifter's full flexion range. The ligaments were not stretched enough to carry much load, so the back muscles did the work (Cholewicki & McGill, 1992).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 13 participants lifting 60–90% of 1RM, flexion in the lower thoracic and upper lumbar spine increased as load increased, along with faster backward tilting of the pelvis (Shoji et al., 2025). The authors suggested keeping the lower back arched and the trunk rigid at high loads. This is a lab study of motion, not of injury.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review with meta-analysis found **low-quality evidence** that greater lumbar flexion during lifting was **not** a risk factor for developing or keeping low back pain (Saraceni et al., 2020). Most included studies involved everyday lifting, not maximal barbell deadlifts, so it cannot prove heavy flexed pulls are safe.

**What this means in practice** <Evidence level="practice" />:

- Some rounding of the upper back (thoracic spine) is common and accepted in competitive deadlifters, especially conventional pullers.
- The real problem is **position change under load**: a back that starts in one shape and rounds further as the bar leaves the floor, often with the hips shooting up. That means the lifter cannot hold the position at that load.
- Aim for a lower back that is braced and roughly neutral, and keep it the **same** from floor to lockout.
- Do not frighten lifters with claims that any flexion will injure them. Build the back's tolerance gradually through progressive training, and reduce load when position changes a lot rep to rep.
- Persistent or severe back pain, or pain with leg symptoms, needs assessment by a qualified health professional (see Part 5 · Chapter 9).

:::tip[In plain words]
A little rounding in a heavy deadlift is normal and not a disaster. What matters is that your back holds the shape it started in. If your back keeps rounding more as the bar comes up, the weight is too heavy for your current technique.
:::

## Lockout and the IPF rules

<Evidence level="practice" /> Under the IPF Technical Rules (check the current edition before every meet; [Part 4 · Chapter 7](/lets-git-it/peaking/07-competition-rules/) covers the rules in detail):

- The bar is gripped with any grip in both hands and lifted until the lifter stands **erect, knees locked straight, shoulders back**. Under the 2026 rulebook, "shoulders back" means the front of the shoulder is behind the line of the bar.
- The head referee gives the **"Down"** signal once the bar is motionless in the finished position. Lowering before the signal is a fault.
- **Faults include:** any downward movement of the bar before the finish (a slight settle as the shoulders come back is not a fault); not standing erect with shoulders back; knees not locked; **supporting the bar on the thighs** (hitching); stepping forward, backward or sideways; and letting go of the bar on the way down instead of returning it to the platform under control with both hands.
- Any deliberate attempt to lift the bar counts as an attempt.

**Hitching vs. a slow lockout.** A hitch is resting the bar on the thighs and using the legs to "ramp" it up in stages. A bar that slides up the thighs slowly but keeps rising, without being supported, is a grind, not a hitch. Referees decide; train lockouts so the question never arises.

**Lockout cues** (practice): drive the hips forward into the bar, squeeze the glutes, stand tall. **Do not lean back** further than needed to get the shoulders behind the bar: it is not required and loads the lower back for no gain.

:::tip[In plain words]
Finish standing straight with your knees locked and shoulders back, then wait for "Down". Never rest the bar on your thighs to get it up, and don't drop it on the way down. Leaning way back isn't needed.
:::

## Grip

| Grip | How | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Double overhand** | Both palms facing you | Symmetrical; trains grip | Usually fails first at heavy loads |
| **Hook** | Overhand, thumb trapped under the fingers | Symmetrical; very secure once adapted | Painful at first; thumb needs weeks to adapt |
| **Mixed** | One palm facing you, one away | Easiest way to hold heavy loads | Asymmetrical; small risk to the biceps of the palm-up arm |
| **Straps** | — | Removes grip as a limit | **Not allowed** in competition; training only |

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 29 trained men and women lifting 50–90% of 1RM, the mixed grip needed the least forearm muscle activity and was rated the easiest grip. Grip type did not change peak bar speed (Pratt et al., 2020).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 35 distal biceps tendon ruptures found on YouTube videos, 25 happened during deadlifts. In every mixed-grip deadlift rupture, it was the **palm-up (supinated) arm** that ruptured, with the elbow straight (Kapicioglu et al., 2021). This describes how the injury happens; it cannot tell us how often it happens.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> If using a mixed grip, keep the palm-up elbow straight and never try to "curl" the bar; a bent elbow under load is the classic biceps-rupture mechanism. But the ruptures in the video study happened with the elbow already straight (Kapicioglu et al., 2021), so a straight arm does not remove the risk. Hook or double overhand removes the palm-up arm altogether; consider them for lighter sets and alternate which hand is palm-up.

:::tip[In plain words]
Pick a grip you can hold at a max. Mixed grip is easiest to hold, but the palm-up arm carries a small risk of tearing the biceps even when it's straight. Never bend that elbow. Hook grip hurts at first but is even on both sides and avoids that risk. Straps are fine in training but not on the platform.
:::

## Common variations

| Variation | Main use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Paused deadlift** (1–2 s just off the floor or below the knee) | Owning the start position; positional strength | Same stance as competition |
| **Deficit deadlift** (2.5–5 cm platform) | Strength off the floor; longer range | Only if the lifter can hold position in the deficit |
| **Block or rack pull** (bar at or below the knee) | Lockout strength; heavier overload | Less specific; can tire the back a lot |
| **Romanian / stiff-leg deadlift** | Hamstrings, hip hinge, back strength | Usually with lighter loads, 6–10 reps |
| **Tempo deadlift** (slow 3–5 s rise to the knee) | Bar path and position awareness | Light loads only |
| **Opposite-stance deadlift** | General strength, variety, managing irritation | Lower specificity |
| **Hex (trap) bar deadlift** | General pulling strength; more knee extension | Different mechanics from the barbell pull |

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 19 male powerlifters, the hex-bar 1RM was heavier than the straight-bar 1RM (265 vs 245 kg), with lower peak moments at the lower back, hip and ankle and higher moments at the knee (Swinton et al., 2011). In 11 trained women, the hex bar produced faster bar speed and larger knee moments than conventional or sumo (Gundersen et al., 2025). Useful for general strength or managing back fatigue, but it is not a substitute for the competition pull.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 30 participants lifting 70% of 1RM, wearing shoes increased bar displacement and work compared with lifting barefoot, in both styles; peak force and bar speed did not differ (Valenzuela et al., 2021). Footwear is covered in Part 5 · Chapter 11.

How to choose and score variations against the competition lift is covered in [Part 3 · Chapter 6: Special exercises](/lets-git-it/advanced/06-special-exercises/).

:::tip[In plain words]
Variations are tools. Paused and deficit pulls build the start, blocks build the finish, and Romanian deadlifts build the muscles behind. Pick them to fix a specific problem, and keep doing your competition deadlift too.
:::

## Sample programs

Default lifter: intermediate, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, **conventional** deadlift 210 kg. Loads from the RTS chart in [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg. Squat and bench work continue as normal and are not shown in full; heavy squat is on day 1 (Monday), so heavy pulling is on day 4 (Thursday), at least 72 h later, and no heavy squat follows the next day.

### Program 1: Testing sumo vs. conventional over a 6-week block

Goal: give sumo a fair trial without losing the conventional deadlift. Sumo will feel awkward at first, so the first two weeks are pure practice.

| Week | Thursday (heavy pull) | Sets × reps | Load | Saturday (moderate pull) | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Conventional | 1 × 3, then 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~180 kg), then RPE 7 (~175 kg) | Sumo | 5 × 3 | Start at 130 kg; add weight only while RPE ≤ 7 |
| 2 | Conventional | 1 × 3, then 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~180 kg), then RPE 7 (~175 kg) | Sumo | 5 × 3 | RPE 7 (by feel) |
| 3 | Sumo | 1 × 3, then 3 × 3 | RPE 8, then RPE 7 (by feel) | Conventional | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 (~175 kg) |
| 4 | Sumo | 1 × 3, then 3 × 3 | RPE 8, then RPE 7 (by feel) | Conventional | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 (~175 kg) |
| 5 | Conventional | 1 × 1 @ RPE 8.5, then 2 × 3 | ~197.5 kg, then RPE 7 (~175 kg) | Sumo | 4 × 2 | RPE 7 (by feel) |
| 6 | Sumo | 1 × 1 @ RPE 8.5, then 2 × 3 | By feel, then RPE 7 | Conventional | 3 × 2 | RPE 7 (~180 kg) |

**Decision rule** <Evidence level="practice" />: estimate each style's 1RM from the RPE 8.5 single (load ÷ 0.939). Film every top set.

- If sumo's estimate is **at least equal** to conventional's after only six weeks, sumo is likely to pass it with more practice: switch, or run another block to confirm.
- If sumo is **within about 5%** and felt better on the hips and back, run a second 6-week block before deciding.
- If sumo is clearly weaker, or irritates the hips or adductors, stay conventional and keep sumo as an occasional variation.

**Why it's built this way:** both styles are trained every week so neither skill fades; the heavy slot alternates so each style gets its own heavy exposures; the comparison uses top sets at the same RPE, which are more reliable for estimating 1RM than light sets. Don't switch styles within 8–12 weeks of a meet.

### Program 2: Technique block for a lifter losing position off the floor

Problem: on heavy conventional pulls the hips rise before the bar moves, the lower back rounds further, and the bar drifts forward. Lighter sets look fine. Plan: four weeks of capped effort on the competition lift, plus paused pulls to own the start.

| Week | Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Thu | Deadlift | 4 × 3 | RPE 7 (~175 kg) |
| 1 | Thu | Front squat | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 |
| 1 | Sat | Paused deadlift (2 s, 3–5 cm off the floor) | 4 × 3 | RPE 7 (by feel; start ~140 kg) |
| 1 | Sat | Back extension | 3 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 2 | Thu | Deadlift | 1 × 3, then 3 × 3 | RPE 7.5 (~177.5 kg), then RPE 7 (~175 kg) |
| 2 | Sat | Paused deadlift | 4 × 3 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Thu | Deadlift | 1 × 3, then 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~180 kg), then RPE 7 (~175 kg) |
| 3 | Sat | Paused deadlift | 4 × 3 | RPE 7.5 |
| 4 (deload) | Thu | Deadlift | 2 × 3 | ~165 kg (about 6% lighter, RPE ≤ 7) |
| 4 (deload) | Sat | Paused deadlift | 2 × 3 | Same load as week 1 |

Front squat and back extension continue in weeks 2–3 at the same sets; in week 4 they drop to 2 sets each.

**Rules for this block** <Evidence level="practice" />:

- A rep only counts if the setup position holds as the bar breaks the floor. If two reps in a row show the fault (hips rising early), drop 5–10 kg for the next set.
- Film the top set every session from the side; compare hip and shoulder height at lift-off with the setup position.
- Cue: "push the floor away", "chest and hips rise together".

**Why it's built this way:** the fault appears at high effort, so the competition lift is capped at RPE 8 while the pattern is rebuilt. The paused deadlift forces the lifter to hold the exact position that breaks down. Front squats and back extensions build the quadriceps and back strength that keep the hips from shooting up. Sets fall from 8 per week (weeks 1–3) to 4 in the deload.

### Program 3: Adopting a hook grip (female lifter)

Lifter: 63 kg class, conventional deadlift 150 kg, currently using a mixed grip; wants a symmetrical grip. Pulls on Tuesday (heavy) and Friday (light); her heavy squat is on Saturday.

| Week | Tuesday | Load | Friday | Load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hook grip for all warm-ups up to ~100 kg; mixed grip for 4 × 3 work sets | RPE 7 (~125 kg) | Hook grip 4 × 5 | Start at 90 kg; stay at RPE ≤ 7 |
| 2 | Hook up to ~110 kg; mixed for 4 × 3 | RPE 7 (~125 kg) | Hook grip 4 × 3 | Start at 90 kg; build to ~105–110 kg, RPE ≤ 6 |
| 3 | Hook for first 2 work sets, mixed for last 2 (4 × 3) | RPE 7 (~125 kg) | Hook grip 4 × 3 | Start at 90 kg; build to ~105–110 kg, RPE ≤ 6 |
| 4 | Hook for all 4 × 3 work sets | RPE 7 (~125 kg) | Hook grip 3 × 3 | RPE 7 |
| 5 | Hook: 1 × 1, then 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~137.5 kg), then RPE 7 (~125 kg) | Hook grip 4 × 3 | RPE 7 |

**Why it's built this way:** the thumb needs time to tolerate the hook, so exposure grows from warm-ups to all working sets over four weeks while the work-set load stays the same. If the bar slips or thumb pain stays sharp, hold the previous week's step for another week. Thumb tape is common for hook grip; the IPF limits how much tape is allowed on the thumbs (see [Part 4 · Chapter 7](/lets-git-it/peaking/07-competition-rules/)). If the hook never feels secure near maximal loads, mixed grip remains a valid choice.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing a style from a body-shape formula.** Test both styles for a block instead.
- **Squatting the bar up in the conventional deadlift.** Hips set too low, bar drifts forward as the hips rise to their real start height.
- **Yanking the bar off the floor.** Pull the slack out first; a jerk loses tightness and position.
- **Letting the bar drift away from the legs.** Engage the lats and keep the bar over the middle of the foot.
- **Fearmongering about any spinal flexion**, or the opposite, ignoring a back that rounds more and more as the bar rises.
- **Bending the palm-up elbow in a mixed grip.** Keep it straight, and remember a straight arm lowers, not removes, the risk.
- **Leaning back at lockout** or lowering before "Down".
- **Testing sumo for two sessions and giving up.** A new style needs weeks of practice before a fair comparison.

## Key takeaways

- Conventional and sumo differ mechanically: sumo is more upright, has a shorter bar path and more quadriceps and hip-rotation demand; conventional has more trunk lean, hamstring and back demand, and bar travel.
- No study shows that one style is stronger or safer for all lifters, and body proportions predict little. Test both.
- Set up the same way every rep: bar over midfoot, brace, pull the slack out, then push.
- Keep the bar close to the legs; skilled lifters do.
- Some spinal flexion is normal in heavy pulls; the warning sign is position changing under load.
- Finish erect with knees locked and shoulders back, wait for "Down", and never support the bar on the thighs.
- Mixed grip is easiest to hold but puts the palm-up biceps at some risk; hook grip is symmetrical but needs adaptation.
