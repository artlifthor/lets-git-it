## In one minute

A technical fault is a repeatable change from the lifter's intended technique that costs performance, risks a red light, or loads a tissue the lifter can't yet tolerate. The same visible fault can have different causes (poor skill, a weak muscle, limited mobility, fatigue, or simply too much weight), and the fix depends on the cause, not the symptom. Diagnose first: film, note when the fault appears (every rep, only heavy reps, or only late in sets), then choose cues, variations, accessories, mobility work, or load changes. Most of the fixes in this chapter are established coaching practice rather than tested in trials; where research exists, it is cited. Feedback helps: visual feedback and external cues have the best support.

## How to diagnose a fault

### When does it appear?

The first question is not "what is wrong?" but "**when** does it go wrong?"

| The fault appears… | Most likely cause | First fix to try |
|---|---|---|
| On every rep, even warm-ups | Skill, setup, or mobility | Cues, setup changes, lighter practice, mobility check |
| Only above ~85% or RPE 8+ | Strength limit at a specific position | Variations and accessories targeting that position; cap RPE while it's fixed |
| Only on the last reps of a set | Fatigue | Fewer reps per set, lower RPE, more rest |
| Only on some days | Fatigue, sleep, stress, or pain | Autoregulate the day; check recovery |
| Suddenly, with pain | Possible injury | Stop loading the painful pattern; see a qualified health professional |

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 22 studies (293 experienced lifters) found that higher intensity and fatigue predictably slow the bar and **increase variability in joint movements**, especially in the acceleration and sticking regions. Different lifters adopted different mechanical strategies as loads rose. Only two included studies examined the deadlift (Bakhshinejad et al., 2025).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 18 recreational lifters doing bench press sets to failure at 75% 1RM, the bar path changed as the set went on and the time to lift the bar more than doubled from the first to the last rep (Duffey & Challis, 2007). The authors suggested that less-skilled lifters should focus on a good bar path rather than training to failure.

:::tip[In plain words]
Before you fix a mistake, find out when it happens. A fault on every rep is a skill problem. A fault only on heavy singles is a strength problem. A fault only on the last reps is a tiredness problem. Each one needs a different fix.
:::

### The five fix categories

<Evidence level="practice" /> Each table below lists fixes from these five categories. Use the cheapest fix first.

1. **Cues and setup**: change what the lifter thinks about or where they put their feet, hands or the bar.
2. **Variations**: a version of the lift that forces the correct position (pauses, tempo, pins).
3. **Accessories**: build a muscle that is losing the battle at the faulty position.
4. **Mobility**: only if the lifter cannot reach the position even unloaded.
5. **Load management**: cap RPE, reduce reps, or drop load while the pattern is rebuilt.

:::tip[In plain words]
Try the simple fixes first: a better cue or a small setup change. If that doesn't work, add a variation or extra exercise. Lower the weight while you fix it.
:::

## Feedback and cues: what works

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 20 studies found that feedback during resistance training improved acute performance; bar velocity rose by about 8.4% on average. **Visual feedback** (for example, seeing a velocity number or video) beat verbal feedback, and feedback after every rep worked best for acute performance. Studies that gave feedback over a training cycle reported better improvements in strength and technical competency (Weakley et al., 2023).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 10 studies found that an **external focus** (attention on the bar or the floor) gave a small acute strength increase compared with an internal focus (attention on a body part). Over long-term training there was no significant difference overall, but in a subgroup of lower-body exercises, external focus produced larger strength gains (Grgic et al., 2021).

| Internal cue (body) | External cue (bar / floor / environment) |
|---|---|
| "Push your knees out" | "Spread the floor apart with your feet" |
| "Squeeze your glutes at the top" | "Push your hips into the bar" |
| "Tuck your elbows" | "Bend the bar" |
| "Extend your back" | "Push the floor away" |
| "Keep your chest up" | "Push your back into the bar" (squat) |

<Evidence level="practice" /> One cue per set. Give it before the set, not during it. Review video between sets, briefly. Keep cues that work and drop those that don't.

:::tip[In plain words]
Show lifters their video and tell them how each rep went; it helps. Cues about the bar or the floor ("push the floor away") tend to work a bit better than cues about body parts ("straighten your knees"). Use one cue at a time.
:::

## Video-review checklists

<Evidence level="practice" /> Film the top set (and a back-off set) of each competition lift every week. Phone at hip height, about 3–4 m away, held still, with the whole body and bar in frame.

**Squat**
- [ ] Side view: depth (hip crease below the top of the knee), bar over midfoot, trunk angle change out of the hole
- [ ] Front or rear view: knees track over toes, hips level, no shift to one side
- [ ] Bar position the same on every rep; brace held through the rep

**Bench press**
- [ ] Side view: bar touch point, bar path (down, then up and back), pause motionless
- [ ] Head-end or foot-end view: bar level, both arms locking out together, elbows symmetrical
- [ ] Buttocks, head and shoulders on the bench, feet flat, from "Start" to "Rack"

**Deadlift**
- [ ] Side view: bar over midfoot at start, bar stays close to the legs, hips and shoulders rise together off the floor
- [ ] Back shape from setup to lockout: the same or changing?
- [ ] Lockout: knees locked, shoulders back, no hitch, waits for "Down", controlled descent

:::tip[In plain words]
Film your heavy sets from the side every week and check them against a short list. A few seconds of video shows more than how a lift felt.
:::

## Squat faults

| Fault | Likely causes | Fixes |
|---|---|---|
| **Not reaching depth** | Misjudging depth; stance too narrow or too wide for the lifter's hips; ankle mobility; fear of the bottom; load too heavy | Film every set from the side; pause squats at competition depth; adjust stance; heeled shoes; cap RPE until depth is consistent |
| **Knee cave (valgus)** | Habit; weak hip abductors or adductors; stance and foot angle mismatch; fatigue | External cue ("spread the floor"); adjust foot angle to match knee path; pause or tempo squats; hip abductor and adductor work; reduce load if it gets worse with fatigue |
| **"Good-morning" squat** (hips rise faster than the bar) | Quadriceps weaker than hips and back; bar too high or too low for the lifter; lost brace | Cue "push your back into the bar"; pause squats; front squats or high-bar squats; leg press; brace practice |
| **Butt wink** (pelvis tucks under at the bottom) | Squatting deeper than hip structure allows; narrow stance; brace lost at the bottom | Squat only to competition depth; widen stance or turn toes out; brace harder before descending; heeled shoes |
| **Shifting** (hips move to one side) | Bar or feet off-centre; mobility or strength difference between sides; old injury; pain | Check bar and foot placement with a front video; tempo and pause squats; single-leg work (split squats); see a professional if painful |

- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Depth rule:** the IPF requires the top surface of the legs at the hip joint to go below the top of the knees. Lifters often misjudge their own depth, so check on video rather than by feel.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> A narrative review of biomechanical and cadaver research concluded that, with good technique and progressive loading, deep squats do not increase injury risk to passive tissues of the knee and spine compared with shallower squats (Hartmann et al., 2013). This was a literature review, mostly of modelled forces, not a trial of injuries.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Butt wink:** in 19 participants, the lumbar spine flexed from standing to the bottom of the squat. The lower lumbar spine flexed less when the knees were allowed to travel forward past the toes (Hebling Campos et al., 2017). In 14 trained men squatting light loads, a 2.5 cm heel raise reduced forward trunk lean but did **not** change the angle between the trunk and pelvis (Charlton et al., 2017). So heels may help a forward-leaning squat, but don't expect them to remove butt wink.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Knee bands:** a popular knee-cave drill is squatting with a mini-band around the knees. In 26 participants squatting 40% and 80% 1RM, the band increased gluteus maximus activity but also **increased** knee valgus and tibial rotation, more so with a stronger band (Reece et al., 2020). The study did not measure injuries; the point is that the band does not train the knees out during the lift.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Some brief inward knee movement as a heavy squat leaves the bottom is common even in strong lifters. Concern rises when the cave is large, gets worse rep to rep, differs between sides, or is painful.

:::tip[In plain words]
Check depth on video, not by feel. A little knee wobble out of the bottom on a heavy squat is common; a big or worsening cave is not. If your hips shoot up first, your legs are probably the weak link, so train them with pause and front squats.
:::

## Bench press faults

| Fault | Likely causes | Fixes |
|---|---|---|
| **Elbows flare early** | Weak triceps or lats; grip too wide or too narrow; habit | Cue "bend the bar"; close-grip bench; paused bench; rows and pulldowns; test grip width |
| **Bar drifts towards the feet or face** | Inconsistent touch point; elbows not under the bar at the bottom; flaring too early | Mark the touch point on video; paused bench; Spoto press (pause 1–2 cm above the chest); tempo bench |
| **Butt lifts off the bench** (a fault under IPF rules) | Leg drive pushing up instead of back; feet too far back; overly aggressive arch | Cue "drive back along the bench"; move feet forward slightly; practise with competition commands; reduce arch |
| **Losing the arch / upper back position** | Weak setup; shoulder blades not set; unracking pulls shoulders forward | Set shoulder blades before unrack; get a hand-off; rows and rear-shoulder work; pause bench |
| **Uneven press** (one arm locks out first, bar tilts) | Uneven grip; strength or mobility difference; old shoulder or elbow injury | Check grip against the bar's rings; dumbbell bench; single-arm accessories; film from the head end; refer if painful |

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 powerlifters and 22 recreational lifters, the powerlifters used a different bar path, with shorter joint ranges of motion and lower peak elbow moments (Mausehund & Krosshaug, 2023). Bar path is a skill that improves with practice.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 14 trained men, 1RM was similar with wide and medium grips (about 110 and 109 kg) and lower with a narrow grip (about 104 kg) (Larsen et al., 2021). Grip changes are a real lever for flaring and touch-point problems.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 20 experienced men, the powerlifting-style arched bench increased latissimus dorsi activity and produced about 8% lower integrated shoulder moments than a standard flat-back bench at 25–75% 1RM (Cudlip et al., 2022). In 15 highly trained lifters, 1RM was about 4 kg higher with an arched back, while the flat-back bench had a longer bar path and faster bar speed (Bartolomei et al., 2024). The arch is a legitimate technique, but losing it mid-rep changes the lift.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> The IPF requires the head, shoulders and buttocks to stay on the bench and the feet to stay flat on the floor from "Start" to "Rack". Current IPF rules also require the elbows to come down to at least shoulder level at the bottom, which limits very high arches; see [Part 4 · Chapter 7: Competition rules](/lets-git-it/peaking/07-competition-rules/).

:::tip[In plain words]
Most bench faults come from an inconsistent touch point or a setup that falls apart. Set your shoulder blades first, touch the same spot every rep, and push your legs back along the bench, not up into the air.
:::

## Deadlift faults

| Fault | Likely causes | Fixes |
|---|---|---|
| **Hips shoot up** off the floor | Hips set lower than the real start position; weak quads; slack not pulled out | Set hips where they will be when the bar breaks; "push the floor away"; paused deadlift; front squats; cap RPE |
| **Back rounds progressively** (shape changes under load) | Load too heavy; brace lost; weak back extensors; lats not engaged | Reduce load to where shape holds; brace drills; paused deadlift; RDLs and back extensions |
| **Bar drifts away** from the legs | Bar starts over the toes; lats loose; shoulders too far in front | Bar over midfoot; cue "drag the bar up your legs"; paused deadlift below the knee; lat work |
| **Lockout hitch** (bar rests on the thighs) | Weak hips at lockout; leaning back early; fatigue | Block pulls above the knee; RDLs; hip thrusts; cue "hips into the bar"; practise "Down" commands |
| **Slow off the floor** | Weak quads (conventional) or adductors and quads (sumo); poor start position; wrong style | Paused and deficit deadlifts; front squats or pause squats; test the other style |

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 40 lifters at the 1999 Special Olympics World Games, the more skilled lifters kept the bar closer to the body (Escamilla et al., 2001).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Back rounding: avoid fearmongering.** A systematic review found low-quality evidence that greater lumbar flexion during lifting was not a risk factor for low back pain (Saraceni et al., 2020), though most studies examined everyday lifting, not maximal deadlifts. <Evidence level="practice" /> The coaching target is a back shape that **holds** from setup to lockout, not a perfectly flat back. See [Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/technique-injury/04-deadlift-technique/).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> **Hitching** (supporting the bar on the thighs) is a red light under IPF rules. A slow lockout that keeps rising is not a hitch. Train the lockout so the bar never stalls.

:::tip[In plain words]
If your hips shoot up, set them a bit higher at the start and train your legs. If your back keeps rounding more as the bar comes up, the weight is too heavy for now. Keep the bar against your legs the whole way.
:::

## Load management during a fix

<Evidence level="practice" /> Technique changes need repetitions with the new pattern. Heavy, high-RPE sets reinforce the old one.

- **Cap RPE at about 8** on the competition lift while the fault is being fixed; most reps at RPE 7.
- **Shorter sets:** 2–4 reps per set rather than 6–10, so fatigue doesn't break technique.
- **Drop load mid-session** if two reps in a row show the fault.
- **Don't change technique close to a meet.** Within about 4 weeks of competition, only small cues; save big changes for the off-season.
- **Expect a dip.** A new pattern can feel weaker for a few weeks before it is stronger.

:::tip[In plain words]
While you fix a mistake, lift weights you can do well, with a couple of reps left in the tank. Lots of good reps teach the new pattern; grinding heavy reps teaches the old one.
:::

## Sample programs

Default lifter: intermediate, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. Loads from the RTS chart in [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg. Variations are loaded by RPE because their 1RMs are unknown. Week layout: Monday heavy squat and bench, Wednesday bench, Thursday heavy deadlift, Saturday light squat and bench. Heavy squat and heavy deadlift are never on consecutive days.

### Program 1: Fixing a "good-morning" squat (4 weeks)

Problem: out of the hole, the hips rise before the bar and the chest drops; worst above RPE 8. Diagnosis: quadriceps are the weak link.

| Week | Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mon | Squat | 4 × 3 | RPE 7 (~150 kg) |
| 1 | Mon | Front squat | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 |
| 1 | Sat | Pause squat (2 s at the bottom) | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| 2 | Mon | Squat | 1 × 3, then 3 × 3 | RPE 7.5 (~152.5 kg), then RPE 7 (~150 kg) |
| 2 | Mon | Front squat | 3 × 5 | RPE 7.5 |
| 2 | Sat | Pause squat | 4 × 4 | RPE 7.5 |
| 3 | Mon | Squat | 1 × 3, then 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~155 kg), then RPE 7 (~150 kg) |
| 3 | Mon | Front squat | 3 × 5 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Sat | Pause squat | 4 × 4 | RPE 8 |
| 4 (deload) | Mon | Squat; front squat | 2 × 3; 2 × 5 | ~140 kg (about 7% lighter); week 1 load |
| 4 (deload) | Sat | Pause squat | 2 × 4 | Week 1 load |

Leg press 3 × 10 at RPE 8 on Saturday in weeks 1–3 (2 sets in week 4).

**Cue:** "push your back into the bar" as you leave the bottom. Film the top set from the side and compare trunk angle at the bottom and 10 cm above it.

**Why it's built this way:** front squats and pause squats force an upright trunk and load the quadriceps, the muscle group that fails. The competition squat stays in at RPE ≤ 8 so the lifter keeps practising, but not grinding the old pattern. Squat-pattern sets fall from 11 per week (weeks 1–3) to 6 in the deload.

### Program 2: Fixing bar drift and early elbow flare on the bench (4 weeks)

Problem: the touch point varies by 3–5 cm, the elbows flare as soon as the bar leaves the chest, and the bar drifts towards the face early. Lockout is fine.

| Week | Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mon | Bench press (competition pause) | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~97.5 kg) |
| 1 | Wed | Spoto press (pause 1–2 cm above the chest) | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| 1 | Wed | Chest-supported row | 4 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 1 | Sat | Tempo bench (3 s down, 1 s pause) | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 |
| 1 | Sat | Close-grip bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 2 | Mon | Bench press | 1 × 3, then 3 × 4 | RPE 7.5 (~102.5 kg), then RPE 7 (~97.5 kg) |
| 2 | Wed / Sat | As week 1 | Same sets | RPE +0.5 |
| 3 | Mon | Bench press | 1 × 1, then 3 × 4 | RPE 8 (~110 kg), then RPE 7 (~97.5 kg) |
| 3 | Wed / Sat | As week 1 | Same sets | RPE 8 |
| 4 (deload) | All | Same exercises | About half the sets (2 per exercise) | Week 1 loads |

**Cues:** "bend the bar" and "touch the same spot". Put a chalk mark on the shirt at the target touch point and check it on video every set.

**Why it's built this way:** the Spoto press and tempo bench slow the bottom of the lift down so the lifter learns where the bar should be and keeps the elbows under it. Rows support the upper-back setup, and close-grip bench builds the triceps that keep the elbows from flaring. Bench sets fall from 14 per week in weeks 1–3 (plus 4 row sets) to 8 in the deload (plus 2 row sets).

### Program 3: Fixing a slow lockout and hitching risk on the deadlift (4 weeks)

Problem: heavy conventional pulls pass the knee and then stall; at max the lifter leans back early and the bar starts to rest on the thighs.

| Week | Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Thu | Deadlift | 1 × 2, then 3 × 3 | RPE 7 (~180 kg), then RPE 7 (~175 kg) |
| 1 | Thu | Block pull (bar just above the knee) | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 |
| 1 | Sat | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 1 | Sat | Hip thrust | 3 × 8 | RPE 8 |
| 2 | Thu | Deadlift | 1 × 2, then 3 × 3 | RPE 7.5 (~185 kg), then RPE 7 (~175 kg) |
| 2 | Thu / Sat | Block pull, RDL, hip thrust | As week 1 | RPE +0.5 |
| 3 | Thu | Deadlift | 1 × 2, then 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~187.5 kg), then RPE 7 (~175 kg) |
| 3 | Thu / Sat | Block pull, RDL, hip thrust | As week 1 | RPE 8 (hip thrust RPE 8.5) |
| 4 (deload) | Thu | Deadlift; block pull | 2 × 3; 1 × 3 | ~165 kg (about 6% lighter); week 1 load |
| 4 (deload) | Sat | RDL; hip thrust | 2 × 6; 2 × 8 | Week 1 loads |

**Rules:** a training partner calls "Down" on every top set; the lifter holds the lockout until the call. Any set where the bar rests on the thighs ends there, and the next set drops 10 kg.

**Cue:** "hips into the bar", stand tall, no leaning back.

**Why it's built this way:** block pulls overload the exact range where the lift stalls, while RDLs and hip thrusts build the hip extensors that finish the lift. The competition deadlift stays at RPE ≤ 8 so the lockout is practised fast, not ground out. Deadlift-pattern sets fall from 10 per week (weeks 1–3) to 5 in the deload; Saturday's work is light, and Monday's heavy squat comes 4 days after Thursday's pull.

## Common mistakes

- **Fixing the symptom, not the cause.** A knee cave from fatigue needs fewer reps, not a band around the knees.
- **Giving several cues at once.** One cue per set.
- **Coaching by feel instead of video.** Lifters (and coaches) misjudge depth, bar path and back shape in real time.
- **Grinding heavy reps through the fault.** It practises the fault. Cap RPE while fixing it.
- **Labelling normal variation as a fault.** Small changes at near-maximal loads are expected; look for large, repeatable, or worsening ones.
- **Making big technique changes close to a meet.**
- **Ignoring pain.** A fault that appears suddenly with pain needs assessment by a qualified health professional, not a new cue.

## Key takeaways

- Diagnose before fixing: note whether a fault appears on every rep, only at heavy loads, only late in sets, or only on some days.
- Fixes come in five categories: cues and setup, variations, accessories, mobility, and load management. Start with the cheapest.
- Visual feedback and external-focus cues have the best research support.
- Movement becomes more variable as load and fatigue rise; not every change is a fault.
- Most specific fault fixes are coaching practice; use video to test whether a fix works for your lifter.
- Fix technique at RPE ≤ 8 with short sets, and keep major changes for the off-season.
