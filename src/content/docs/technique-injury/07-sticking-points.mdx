## In one minute

Each competition lift has three broad regions where a heavy rep can stall: the **start** (out of the hole, off the chest, off the floor), the **middle** (the classic sticking region) and the **lockout**. Research shows that a slow middle region is normal in the bench press and common in the squat, and that **which muscle limits the lift differs between lifters**, so the region alone doesn't name the cause. Read the region together with what the body did just before the slowdown: a position change points to one muscle group, a clean grind to another. Then pick one variation that loads that region and one or two accessories that build the muscle behind it, and set a lift-specific test before you start. Deadlift sticking-region research is thin, so deadlift choices lean more on coaching practice.

## Reading a sticking point: three questions

For any heavy rep, ask three questions in order.

1. **Where** did the bar slow down? Use the landmarks for each lift below.
2. **What changed just before it slowed?** Hips rose, chest dropped, bar drifted, elbows flared, knees caved, or nothing (a clean grind).
3. **Is it the same on most heavy sets?** One rep proves little; a repeated pattern is a diagnosis.

**Why question 2 matters most.** When the position changes first, the slowdown is often the *result* of a muscle elsewhere giving way. A squat that slows at parallel after the hips shot up is usually not a "parallel" problem; it is a problem with whatever let the hips rise.

**What the research says about reading heavy reps:**

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 22 studies (293 experienced lifters) found that as loads got heavier, bar speed dropped, the upward phase took longer, and **joint movement became more variable, especially in the acceleration and sticking regions**. Different lifters adopted different strategies near the sticking point. Only two of the studies looked at the deadlift (Bakhshinejad et al., 2025).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 bench-trained men, the last rep of a 3RM and 6RM set looked like a 1RM in bar movement and muscle activity; only the 10RM differed, with higher bar speed through the sticking region (Larsen et al., 2022). The final reps of heavy sets of 3–6 are reasonable stand-ins for maximal attempts when diagnosing.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 21 college-aged lifters, physical measures (including knee, hip and back extensor strength tested at the sticking position, and body size) were more closely related to squat 1RM than psychological ones, but in the combined model only **lean (fat-free) mass relative to height** predicted it (Vigotsky et al., 2019). Muscle size matters to every region; a "weak point" plan should not crowd out general hypertrophy.

:::tip[In plain words]
When a heavy rep slows down, note where it slowed, what your body did right before, and whether it happens on most heavy sets. The thing that went wrong just before the slowdown is usually the real clue. Your last hard reps of a heavy triple show this almost as well as a max does.
:::

## The squat

### What the research shows

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Not everyone has one.** In 15 trained men squatting a 6RM, only 10 showed a sticking region on the last rep, and it was short compared with the bench press (van den Tillaar et al., 2014).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **It goes with a hip-dominant, forward-leaning position.** In 25 recreationally trained lifters squatting 3RMs, the sticking region coincided with more forward lean and a larger hip share of the work (Larsen, Kristiansen & van den Tillaar, 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **In elite lifters, the hips do most of the work through the sticking region.** In 8 elite national-level powerlifters lifting singles at 90%, 95% and 100% of 1RM, hip torque was **more than three times** knee torque in the sticking region. With heavier loads, the lifters pushed harder with the knees out of the bottom (about 8% more knee torque from 90% to 95%) and leaned further forward towards the end of the sticking region (Andersen et al., 2026).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 29 elite Austrian powerlifters squatting at 70–90% of 1RM, musculoskeletal modelling estimated that **gluteus maximus and hamstring (semitendinosus) forces rose most** as the load increased, while the quadriceps (vasti) produced the largest forces overall. Quadriceps, hamstring and calf forces barely increased through the sticking region as the load got heavier (Pürzel et al., 2025).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Depth and load stress muscles differently.** In 10 strength-trained women, knee extensor effort rose with squat **depth** but not with load, while hip extensor effort rose with both depth and load (Bryanton et al., 2012).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **The limiting joint differs between people.** In a case series of 5 lifters who failed a rep at their 3RM, the hip produced less energy on the failed rep at group level, but for some individuals the **knee** was the limiting joint (Flanagan et al., 2015).

**What this means.** <Evidence level="practice" /> At heavy loads the squat leans on the hips more and more, so most lifters' sticking region is a hip-and-position problem. But the quads drive the bottom of the squat and can still be the weak link for some lifters, which shows up as the hips rising and the chest falling. Video tells you which.

:::tip[In plain words]
In a heavy squat, your hips do more and more of the work, and the slow spot usually comes just above the bottom as you lean forward. Some lifters run out of hip strength there; others run out of leg (quad) strength lower down, and their hips shoot up first. Your video shows which one you are.
:::

### Squat regions and what to do

| Region | What you see | Likely cause | Variation (pick one) | Accessories (pick 1–2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Out of the hole**, chest drops / hips shoot up first | Bar slows just above the bottom after the torso tips forward | Quads limited; position not held | Pause squat (2–3 s), tempo squat (3-1-0), front squat | Leg press (deep), hack squat, split squat, leg extension |
| **Out of the hole**, position holds but bar barely moves | Slow, upright grind from the very bottom | Strength from the bottom without the bounce; quads and adductors | Pause squat (3 s), pin squat from the bottom | Leg press, adductor machine, Copenhagen plank |
| **Mid-range** (around or just above parallel), position holds | Bar decelerates and grinds through the middle; torso angle stays roughly the same | Hip extensors limited (glutes, hamstrings, adductors) | Pause squat at the sticking height (pause on the way **up**), pin squat from the sticking height | Romanian deadlift, good morning, back extension, adductor work |
| **Mid-range**, hips rise into a "good morning" | Torso tips sharply as the bar slows | Quads giving way, then back extensors holding the lift | Tempo squat, front or safety-bar squat | Leg press, back extension |
| **Lockout** | Bar slows in the last third | Rare; usually bracing or fatigue, sometimes the lifter starts to rack early | Fix bracing; heavier singles; occasionally pin squat in the top half | Usually none; check belt and breathing |
| **Knees cave** as it slows | Knees move in, then the bar slows | Pattern, ankle range, hip and adductor strength | Tempo or pause squat with a "knees out" cue | See [Chapter 6: Planes of motion](/lets-git-it/technique-injury/06-planes-of-motion/) |

<Evidence level="practice" /> The causes in this table are coaching interpretations consistent with the studies above, not tested diagnoses. Note also that the "bounce" out of the hole is real: a pause squat removes most of the stored elastic help, so a lifter who depends on the bounce will find pause squats disproportionately hard (the bench press study on pauses is described below).

## The bench press

### What the research shows

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **A sticking region is normal.** In 12 recreational lifters, every 1RM bench press showed a sticking period starting about 0.2 s into the press and lasting about 0.9 s (van den Tillaar & Ettema, 2010). In 12 experienced powerlifters, a clear sticking region appeared with narrow, medium and wide grips, but at different elbow and shoulder angles for each grip (Gomo & van den Tillaar, 2016).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **It is not simply a dead spot of muscle activity.** In 10 elite powerlifters, the chest, front shoulder and triceps were close to maximally active from the start of the press and stayed that way through the sticking region. The authors proposed that the sticking region is a **transition** between an early phase helped by stored elastic energy and a later phase where leverage improves (Elliott et al., 1989).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **A pause removes the elastic help quickly.** In 12 experienced lifters pressing 95% of 1RM, the boost from the stretch at the bottom faded with pause length, with a half-life of about 0.85 s (Wilson et al., 1991). A 1-second competition pause removes more than half of it; longer pauses remove most of the rest.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> **Grip changes the job.** In 14 bench-trained men, 1RMs were about 110 kg wide, 109 kg medium and 104 kg narrow; triceps activity was higher with medium and narrow grips (Larsen, Gomo & van den Tillaar, 2021). In 12 bench-trained men, triceps activity rose and biceps activity fell from before to after the sticking region (Larsen et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> A computer model of the bench press predicted that the sticking region appears when shoulder torque falls below a critical level, that the elbow extensors (triceps) contribute little early but dominate near lockout, and that grip width shapes the sticking region more than muscle properties do (Evangelista et al., 2025). Modelling studies generate hypotheses; they don't confirm what happens in a given lifter.

**What this means.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Off the chest, the chest and front shoulders carry most of the load, and the pause has removed most of the stretch help. Mid-range is the normal sticking region. Near lockout, the triceps take over. Grip width moves these regions around, so diagnose with the lifter's **competition grip**.

:::tip[In plain words]
Almost everyone's bench slows down somewhere in the middle. That's normal. Off the chest, your chest and shoulders do the most; near the top, your triceps take over. The pause at the bottom takes away the "bounce", so if you're weak off the chest, practise pausing.
:::

### Bench press regions and what to do

| Region | What you see | Likely cause | Variation (pick one) | Accessories (pick 1–2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Off the chest** (first few cm) | Bar barely leaves the chest after the pause, or sinks on the pause | Chest and front shoulder strength from a dead stop; position on the chest | Long-pause bench (2–3 s), Spoto press (pause just above the chest), Larsen press | Dumbbell bench press (deep stretch), dips, cable or dumbbell fly |
| **Off the chest**, bar drifts toward the feet first | Bar moves forward off the chest, then stalls | Bar path, touch point, elbow position; often technique | Pause bench at moderate load with a "drive back" cue; tempo bench (3-1-0) | Lat and upper-back work (chest-supported row, pulldown) |
| **Mid-range** (the classic sticking region) | Bar slows about a third to halfway up; path stays clean | Shoulder and chest strength at that angle; transition to triceps | Pause on the way up at the sticking height; dead-stop pin press at the sticking height | Dumbbell bench press, incline press, weighted dips |
| **Mid-range**, elbows flare early | Elbows move out sharply as the bar slows | Lifter shifting load to the chest and shoulders; triceps or position | Pause bench with "elbows under the bar" cue; close-grip bench | Triceps extensions, rows |
| **Lockout** (last third) | Bar slows near the top, path clean | Triceps | Close-grip bench, pin or board press (top third), floor press | Overhead and lying triceps extensions, pushdowns, dips |
| **Uneven lockout** | One arm finishes later; bar tilts | Grip or setup asymmetry; one-sided weakness | Check grip marks; single-arm dumbbell press | See [Chapter 6](/lets-git-it/technique-injury/06-planes-of-motion/) |

<Evidence level="practice" /> If a lifter misses at lockout because the bar drifted toward the feet, the fault is bar path, not triceps. Always look at the path before the region.

## The deadlift

### What the research shows

Research on the deadlift's sticking region is the thinnest of the three lifts: a recent systematic review found only **two** studies on how the deadlift changes with load or fatigue in experienced lifters (Bakhshinejad et al., 2025).

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 25 lifters at a regional powerlifting competition, the **conventional** deadlift's sticking point was measured at thigh and trunk angles of about 57° and 58°, very different from the squat's. The squat was a **simultaneous** movement (hips and knees extend together), while the deadlift was **sequential** (knees first, then hips). The authors concluded the two lifts are mechanically very different (Hales et al., 2009).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> Sumo and conventional deadlifts differ in trunk and thigh angles and in joint loading; the conventional lift moved the bar further and did more mechanical work (Escamilla et al., 2000).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> A review comparing sticking points across the three lifts emphasised that each lift has its own pattern, so findings from one should not be applied to another (Kompf & Arandjelović, 2017).

**What this means.** <Evidence level="practice" /> For the deadlift, rely more on careful video and the variation-ratio checks from [Part 3 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/advanced/05-weak-point-analysis/), and keep sumo and conventional fixes separate. Because the conventional lift is sequential, a "slow off the floor" deadlift is mostly a knee-extension and position problem, while a slow lockout is mostly a hip-extension problem.

:::tip[In plain words]
There's very little research on where deadlifts slow down, so coaches rely more on video. Off the floor, your legs push; near the top, your hips finish the lift. Sumo and conventional pulls are different lifts, so fix them in their own style.
:::

### Deadlift regions and what to do

| Region | What you see | Likely cause | Variation (pick one) | Accessories (pick 1–2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Off the floor**, position holds | Bar breaks slowly; back angle stays | Quads and strength from the lowest position | Deficit deadlift (2–5 cm), paused deadlift just off the floor | Leg press, front squat |
| **Off the floor**, hips rise first | Hips shoot up before the bar leaves the floor | Quads limited or setup too low/too high | Paused deadlift just off the floor at moderate load; tempo deadlift | Leg press, front squat, split squat |
| **At or just below the knee** (conventional) | Bar slows or drifts forward around the knees | Bar path; lats; transition from knees to hips | Paused deadlift at the knee | Lat pulldown, rows, Romanian deadlift |
| **Above the knee to lockout** | Bar passes the knee and grinds; hips don't come through | Hip extensors (glutes, hamstrings, adductors in sumo) | Paused deadlift just above the knee; block pull just below the knee (one slot only) | Hip thrust, Romanian deadlift, back extension |
| **Upper back rounds** as it slows | Shoulders roll forward under load | Upper-back strength and position | Paused deadlift; tempo deadlift at moderate load | Chest-supported row, back extension (rounded upper back allowed, light) |
| **Sumo: hips stuck low, bar won't break** | Lifter pushes but the bar won't leave the floor; knees may cave | Adductors and quads in the wide stance; hip position | Paused sumo deadlift just off the floor; sumo deficit (small) | Adductor machine, Copenhagen plank, wide-stance leg press |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Use partial-range pulls (blocks, rack pulls) for one slot at most. Strength built in a short range carries over poorly to the full lift (see the range-of-motion evidence in [Part 3 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/advanced/05-weak-point-analysis/)).

## Testing whether the fix worked

Part 3, Chapter 5 sets out the general evaluation: did the competition lift improve, did the weak region change, did the variation improve. Here are **lift-specific** ways to measure the middle question, because "it looks better" is easy to fool yourself with.

<Evidence level="practice" /> These are coaching methods. They are simple and repeatable, but none has been validated as a research measure.

| Lift and region | Simple test at a fixed load (about RPE 8 at the start of the block) | Success looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Squat, out of the hole | Frame-count the time from the bottom to thighs parallel on side-on video | Fewer frames at the same load; or the same frames at more load |
| Squat, mid-range | Frame-count from thighs parallel to halfway up; check torso angle at the slowest point | Shorter slow phase; torso angle at the sticking point the same or more upright |
| Bench, off the chest | Frame-count from the end of the pause to 5 cm off the chest | Faster first 5 cm; no sinking on the pause |
| Bench, mid-range | Frame-count the slow phase; check bar path | Shorter slow phase with the same path |
| Bench, lockout | Time from mid-range to lockout; close-grip bench at the same RPE | Faster finish; close-grip bench load up |
| Deadlift, off the floor | Frame-count from the first pull to the bar passing mid-shin | Faster break from the floor at the same load |
| Deadlift, lockout | Frame-count from the knee to lockout | Hips finish faster; no hitching |

**How to frame-count.** Film from the same spot, at the same frame rate (30 or 60 frames per second), with the same load. Step through the video frame by frame and count frames between the two landmarks. At 30 fps, 3 frames is 0.1 s. A bar-speed app or velocity device measures the same thing more directly, if the lifter has one.

**Watch the noise.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Count frames on two or three reps, not one, and compare with the lifter's normal day-to-day variation (see [Part 3 · Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/advanced/04-individual-response/)). A one-frame difference is noise.

:::tip[In plain words]
Before you start, film one heavy set and count the video frames through your slow spot. After the block, film the same weight from the same spot and count again. Fewer frames, or the same frames with more weight, means the fix worked.
:::

## Sample programs

Each program shows a **4-week block** for one lift: weeks 1–3 build, week 4 is a deload with about half the sets, and the test comes at the start of the next week. The other lifts follow a normal plan (not shown). These diagnoses are deliberately different from the three examples in [Part 3 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/advanced/05-weak-point-analysis/). Competition-lift loads use the RTS chart from [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/); variations are given as RPE only.

### Program 1: Squat — mid-range grind with the position held

**Lifter:** intermediate, male, 83 kg, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg.

**Diagnosis:** on side-on video of heavy triples, the bar leaves the bottom well, then slows from about parallel to a third of the way up. Torso angle barely changes; knees stay out. Pause squat (3 s at the bottom) is 160 kg (0.89, normal for him). Hip-extension accessories have been minimal for a year. **One sentence:** a mid-range force deficit with good position, most likely hip-extensor limited.

**Weekly layout:** Mon squat · Tue bench · Wed deadlift · Fri squat · Sat bench. Squat and deadlift days are 48 h apart.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Weeks 1–3 | Week 4 (deload) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 1 × 2, then 3 × 4 | Top RPE 8 (~160 kg); back-offs RPE 7 (~145 kg) | 2 × 4 at ~145 kg |
| Mon | Squat with 2 s pause on the way **up**, just above parallel | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 → 7.5 → 8 | 2 × 3, RPE 6 |
| Wed | Romanian deadlift (after deadlifts) | 3 × 8 | RPE 7–8 | 2 × 8, RPE 6 |
| Fri | Squat | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~145 kg), +2.5 kg per week | 2 × 4 at ~145 kg |
| Fri | Pin squat from the sticking height (dead stop) | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 → 7.5 → 8 | 1 × 3, RPE 6 |
| Fri | Back extension | 3 × 12 | RPE 8 | 2 × 12, RPE 7 |

**Weekly squat-pattern hard sets:** weeks 1–3: competition squat 8, paused-on-the-way-up 3, pin squat 3 = **14**, with 57% on the competition lift. Week 4: 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 = **7** (half).

**Test (week 5):** a triple at 155 kg (about RPE 8 in week 1), filmed side-on. **Success:** fewer frames from parallel to a third of the way up, and the e1RM from a top double above week 1's beyond normal noise.

**Why it's built this way.** The pause on the way up makes him produce force from a standstill in exactly the weak region while still doing the full lift. The pin squat is the single partial-range slot and starts at the sticking height. Romanian deadlifts and back extensions build the hip extensors that the elite-lifter data suggest dominate this region (Andersen et al., 2026; Pürzel et al., 2025). Romanian deadlifts sit on Wednesday, 48 h before Friday's squats.

### Program 2: Bench press — weak off the chest

**Lifter:** female, 63 kg class, squat 120 kg, bench press 65 kg, deadlift 150 kg; benches three times a week.

**Diagnosis:** at RPE 8+, the bar sinks slightly during the pause and is slow for the first few centimetres; once moving, it locks out well. Bar path is clean. Long-pause (3 s) bench is 57.5 kg (0.88, low for her); close-grip bench is 62.5 kg (0.96, normal). **One sentence:** a force deficit off the chest, likely chest and front shoulder strength from a dead stop.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Weeks 1–3 | Week 4 (deload) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Bench press | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~50 kg), +1–2.5 kg per week | 2 × 5 at ~50 kg |
| Mon | Long-pause bench (3 s) | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 → 7.5 → 8 | 2 × 3, RPE 6 |
| Mon | Dumbbell bench press (deep stretch) | 3 × 8–10 | RPE 8 | 2 × 8, RPE 7 |
| Wed | Spoto press (pause 2–3 cm above the chest) | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 → 7.5 → 8 | 1 × 4, RPE 6 |
| Wed | Dips (assisted if needed) | 3 × 6–10 | RPE 8 | 2 × 6, RPE 7 |
| Fri | Bench press | 1 × 1, then 3 × 3 | Top RPE 8 (~60 kg); back-offs RPE 7.5 (~55 kg) | 2 × 3 at ~52.5 kg |
| Fri | Chest-supported row | 3 × 10 | RPE 8–9 | 2 × 10, RPE 7 |

**Weekly bench-pattern hard sets:** weeks 1–3: competition bench 8, long-pause 3, Spoto 3 = **14**, with 57% on the competition lift. Week 4: 2 + 2 + 1 + 2 = **7** (half).

**Test (week 5):** a single at RPE 8 with a 1–2 s pause and a long-pause triple, compared with week 1. **Success:** no sinking on the pause, fewer frames over the first 5 cm, long-pause ratio back toward 0.92, competition e1RM up beyond noise (about 2.5 kg for this lifter).

**Why it's built this way.** The long pause removes the stretch help almost completely (Wilson et al., 1991), training the exact weakness. The Spoto press adds control just above the chest. Dumbbell pressing and dips build the chest and front shoulders through a long range. Small jumps (1–2.5 kg) match her bench size.

### Program 3: Sumo deadlift — slow lockout

**Lifter:** intermediate, male, 83 kg, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg, pulls **sumo**.

**Diagnosis:** the bar breaks the floor quickly, passes the knees, then grinds; his hips stay back and come through late, sometimes with a slight hitch. Block pull just below the knee is 220 kg (1.05, low for him; it used to be about 1.12). Hip-extension accessories have been missing. **One sentence:** a lockout force deficit, most likely hip-extensor limited.

**Weekly layout:** Mon squat + paused sumo deadlift · Tue bench · Wed sumo deadlift · Fri squat · Sat bench. Mon → Wed and Wed → Fri are each 48 h.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Weeks 1–3 | Week 4 (deload) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Paused sumo deadlift (2 s just above the knee), after squats | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 → 7.5 → 8 | 1 × 3, RPE 6 |
| Mon | Hip thrust | 3 × 8–10 | RPE 8 | 2 × 8, RPE 7 |
| Wed | Sumo deadlift | 1 × 2, then 4 × 3 | Top RPE 8 (~187.5 kg); back-offs RPE 7 (~175 kg) | 3 × 3 at ~170 kg |
| Wed | Sumo block pull (just below the knee) | 2 × 2 | RPE 7.5 → 8 → 8.5 | 1 × 2, RPE 6 |
| Wed | Back extension | 3 × 12 | RPE 8 | 2 × 12, RPE 7 |

**Weekly deadlift-pattern hard sets:** weeks 1–3: competition sumo 5, paused 3, block pull 2 = **10**, with 50% on the competition lift. Week 4: 3 + 1 + 1 = **5** (half).

**Test (week 5):** a double at 187.5 kg filmed from the side. **Success:** fewer frames from knee to lockout, no hitch, block-pull ratio back toward 1.10, and competition e1RM up.

**Why it's built this way.** The pause just above the knee makes him hold and then drive from the weak region within a full-range sumo pull. The block pull overloads the top for one short slot. Hip thrusts and back extensions build the hip extensors without much extra spinal fatigue. All pulling variations are sumo, because sumo and conventional pulls are mechanically different (Escamilla et al., 2000). Monday's paused pulls are moderate (RPE 7–8) and sit 48 h before Wednesday's heavy pull.

## Common mistakes

- **Naming the region but not the cause.** "Weak at parallel" isn't a diagnosis; what happened just before the bar slowed is.
- **Diagnosing from a single max.** The last reps of heavy triples show the same pattern and give you more examples.
- **Treating the normal bench sticking region as a weakness.** Nearly every heavy bench slows somewhere in the middle.
- **Using a conventional fix for a sumo puller** (or the reverse).
- **Fixing a bar-path problem with an accessory.** A bar that drifts toward the feet needs technique work, not more triceps.
- **Diagnosing with a different grip or stance** from the one used in competition.
- **Stacking several partial-range variations.** Use one partial slot at most.
- **Judging success by eye.** Count frames or measure bar speed at a fixed load, before and after.

## Key takeaways

- Every lift has start, middle and lockout regions; what happens just before the slowdown tells you more than where it happens.
- In heavy squats the hips do most of the work through the sticking region, but some lifters are quad-limited; video decides.
- The bench sticking region is normal; off the chest is chest and front shoulders (with the pause removing the stretch help), lockout is triceps.
- Deadlift sticking-region research is thin; rely on careful video and keep sumo and conventional fixes separate.
- Match each region to one close variation plus one or two accessories for the muscle behind it.
- Set a lift-specific, measurable test (frame counts or bar speed at a fixed load) before the block starts.
