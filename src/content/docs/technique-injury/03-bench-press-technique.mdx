## In one minute

The competition bench press is a paused press from a stable, legal setup: head, shoulders and buttocks on the bench, feet flat on the floor, thumbs around the bar, and a motionless pause on the chest until "Press". A tight upper back with the shoulder blades pulled together, a moderate-to-large arch and a medium-to-wide grip shorten the bar's path and put the shoulders in a stronger position; research suggests narrow grips lower the load lifted, while very wide grips may raise shoulder joint loads. Skilled lifters lower the bar under control, touch on the lower chest and press back towards the shoulders. The pause removes most of the rebound, so strength off the chest must be trained, not borrowed. Variations each change one thing (pause length, grip, range, leg drive) and should be matched to the lifter's fault.

## The competition standard

Summarised from the **IPF Technical Rulebook (2026 edition, effective 1 March 2026)**. These are rules, not evidence claims; the full text is the final word.

| Item | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Body position | Head, shoulders and buttocks in contact with the bench; position kept throughout the lift |
| Feet | **Flat on the floor** (as flat as the shoe allows); feet may move but must stay flat; no feet on the bench during setup; blocks or plates up to 30 cm may build up the floor |
| Grip | Thumbs around the bar (no thumbless or reverse grip); **no more than 81 cm** between the forefingers |
| Start | Bar at arms' length, elbows locked, motionless; then **"Start"** (with a downward arm signal) |
| Lowering | Bar to the chest or abdominal area, not touching the belt; the **underside of both elbows level with or below the top of each shoulder joint** |
| Pause | Bar **motionless** on the chest until **"Press"** |
| Press | No heaving or sinking, no upper-body thrust, no downward movement of the whole bar, no raising of head, shoulders or buttocks, no hands sliding |
| Finish | Arms straight, elbows locked, motionless; then **"Rack"** |

<Evidence level="practice" /> The 2026 change notes describe the rule against heaving and torso-driven thrust as a clarification, and add that lowering the rib cage after "Press" to gain momentum is not allowed. Very high-arch lifters should film from the side to confirm elbow depth. Part 4, Chapter 7 covers commands and red-light checklists.

:::tip[In plain words]
Lie with your head, shoulders and butt on the bench and your feet flat on the floor. Hold the bar with your thumbs around it, wait for "Start", lower it to your chest, keep it completely still until "Press", press it up, and wait for "Rack". No bouncing, no lifting your butt, and your elbows must come down at least to shoulder level.
:::

## Setup, step by step

<Evidence level="practice" /> The sequence below is common powerlifting practice; the evidence on its parts follows in the sections after.

| Step | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Eyes under the bar | Lie down with the eyes roughly under the bar | Short, safe unrack; the bar doesn't hit the hooks on the way up |
| 2. Grip | Set the grip width; bar low in the palm, over the wrist and forearm | Straight line of force; less wrist bending |
| 3. Upper back | Pull the shoulder blades together and down; set the upper back on the bench | Stable base; shorter bar path |
| 4. Feet | Place the feet flat, often a little behind the knees or wide, as comfort and rules allow | Base for leg drive; must stay flat |
| 5. Arch | Lift the chest towards the bar while keeping the buttocks on the bench | Shorter range; stronger shoulder position |
| 6. Unrack | Pull the bar out over the shoulders (or take a hand-off to arms' length) | Keeps the shoulder blades set |
| 7. Breathe and brace | Big breath, chest high; wait for "Start" | Trunk stiffness and a stable chest |

:::tip[In plain words]
Get the same setup every time: eyes under the bar, hands set, shoulder blades squeezed together and down, feet planted flat, chest up. Then unrack without losing that tightness. The setup is half of a good bench.
:::

## Shoulder blades and upper back

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In a computer-model study of 10 experienced strength athletes doing 21 bench press variations, **retracting the shoulder blades** reduced estimated backward shear force at the shoulder joint and rotator cuff activity compared with other scapular positions (Noteboom et al., 2024).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Retracted, depressed shoulder blades also lift the chest, shorten the press and give the upper back a firm base to press from. The cost is upper-back fatigue in long sets; keep the position on every rep, including warm-ups.

:::tip[In plain words]
Squeeze your shoulder blades together and pull them down before you unrack, and keep them there. It gives you a solid base, makes the press a little shorter, and may be easier on your shoulders.
:::

## The arch

An arch raises the chest and lowers the bar's travel distance. It is legal as long as the head, shoulders and buttocks stay on the bench and the elbows reach shoulder level.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 20 experienced male lifters pressing 25–75% of 1RM, the powerlifting (arched) technique increased latissimus dorsi activity, and the standard (flat-back) technique produced about 8% larger integrated shoulder moments (Cudlip et al., 2022). The authors suggested arching does not increase overall shoulder loading.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 50 men over 10 weeks, full-range bench press training improved full-range and partial-range strength more than partial-range training did (Martínez-Cava et al., 2022). An arch shortens the competition range; it is still worth keeping some full, flatter-back pressing (for example, dumbbell or feet-up work) for muscle and joint tolerance.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Build the arch from the upper back (chest up, shoulder blades down), not only from the lower back. Lifters with back pain should use a smaller arch and get it assessed.

:::tip[In plain words]
Arching your back so your chest sits higher makes the bar travel less, and research suggests it doesn't load your shoulders more. Keep your butt and shoulders on the bench and make sure your elbows still drop to shoulder level. Do some flatter-back pressing too.
:::

## Leg drive

<Evidence level="practice" /> Leg drive means pushing the feet into the floor so the body is pressed towards the head end of the bench, keeping the upper back pinned and the arch tight. It does not lift the bar directly. It stabilises the body and helps hold position through the press. There is little direct research on it, so treat it as coaching practice.

- Set the feet so they can stay **flat** under maximal effort. If the heels lift at heavy loads, move the feet forward a little, use lower or flatter shoes, or use blocks (shorter lifters).
- Push "back towards the head", not straight up, so the buttocks stay on the bench.
- Drive should be on **before** "Start" and held through "Press"; a sudden drive after "Press" that lifts the hips or drops the rib cage risks a red light under the 2026 rules.

:::tip[In plain words]
Leg drive means pushing your feet hard into the floor to keep your whole body tight and pinned in position. Your feet must stay flat and your butt must stay down. Set it before "Start" and keep it steady.
:::

## Grip width

Grip width changes range of motion, which joints do more work, and possibly shoulder stress.

- **Load lifted.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> Several studies agree that a narrow grip lowers the load lifted, while medium and wide grips are similar:
  - <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 national- and international-level bench press athletes, 6RM loads were 5.8–11.1% greater with medium and wide grips than with a narrow grip (Saeterbakken et al., 2017).
  - <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 28 novice and resistance-trained men, both groups lifted less for 6RM with a narrow grip than with medium or wide grips (Saeterbakken et al., 2021).
  - <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 14 bench-trained men, 1RM was about 110 kg wide, 109 kg medium and 104 kg narrow (Larsen et al., 2021).
  - <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 27 resistance-trained people, a close-grip bench (about 95% of shoulder width) lifted less than the preferred grip but produced higher peak power and velocity (Lockie et al., 2017).
- **Muscle activity.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> Narrower grips increased triceps activity (Lehman, 2005; Saeterbakken et al., 2021), but the changes were small, and the authors of one study advised choosing grip by what the sport requires rather than to target muscles (Lehman, 2005).
- **Sticking region.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 experienced male powerlifters, all grips showed a sticking region, but at different elbow and shoulder angles (Gomo & van den Tillaar, 2016).
- **Shoulder stress.** <Evidence level="emerging" /> In the computer-model study of 10 athletes, grips narrower than 1.5 times shoulder (biacromial) width reduced estimated compression at the acromioclavicular joint and backward shear at the shoulder, which the authors linked to lower potential risk of some shoulder injuries (Noteboom et al., 2024). This is a modelling study, not injury data.

**Decision rule:**

| Lifter | Starting grip |
|---|---|
| Healthy shoulders, wants maximal load | Medium to wide, up to the 81 cm limit; test both |
| Long arms | Wider end of the range to shorten bar travel |
| Shoulder or AC-joint discomfort | Move the grip in a little (towards about 1.5 times shoulder width or less) and see a qualified health professional if pain persists |
| Weak lockout | Keep the competition grip; add close-grip bench as a variation |

<Evidence level="practice" /> A useful start is a grip where the forearms are close to vertical at the bottom when seen from the front. Mark grip width by the rings on the bar so it is identical every set.

:::tip[In plain words]
A grip that's too narrow costs you weight. Medium and wide grips lift about the same, and a wide grip shortens the press, but very wide grips may stress your shoulders more. Start with your forearms roughly vertical at the bottom, use the bar's rings to set the same grip every time, and move in a little if your shoulders complain.
:::

## Elbows, touch point and bar path

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 36 lifters (19 experts, 17 novices), experts lowered the bar more slowly, used a bar path **closer to the shoulders**, and moved the bar in a different sequence; the required shoulder torque did not differ (Madsen & McLaughlin, 1984).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 34 adults, powerlifters used a different bar path, had lower normalized peak elbow moments and a shorter range of motion than recreational lifters, and were more shoulder dominant (Mausehund & Krosshaug, 2023).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 18 recreational lifters doing a set to failure at 75% of 1RM, the bar path changed across the set, with lifters keeping the bar more directly over the shoulder on later reps (Duffey & Challis, 2007).

<Evidence level="practice" /> Most powerlifters use a slightly curved path: the bar is lowered to the **lower chest or upper abdomen** (around the bottom of the sternum, depending on arch and arm length) with the elbows tucked somewhat, then pressed **up and back** towards a lockout over the shoulders. Keep the forearms close to vertical under the bar; if the bar drifts towards the face early or the elbows flare and lead, the moment arms at the elbow and shoulder grow (see [Chapter 1](/lets-git-it/technique-injury/01-biomechanics-basics/)).

| Element | Typical target | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Touch point | Lower chest, consistent every rep | Chalk mark on the shirt or side video |
| Elbows at the bottom | Under the bar, angled down from the shoulders (not fully flared, not pinned to the sides) | Front view: forearms near vertical |
| Bar path | Down to the lower chest, then up and back to over the shoulders | Side view or bar-path app |
| Lockout | Over the shoulders, elbows locked | Wait for "Rack" |

:::tip[In plain words]
Lower the bar under control to the same spot on your lower chest every time, with your elbows under the bar. Then press it up and slightly back so it finishes over your shoulders. Experienced lifters keep the bar closer to their shoulders than beginners do.
:::

## The pause

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 experienced male lifters pressing 95% of 1RM, the boost from the stretch on the way down decayed with pause length, with a half-life of about 0.85 s (Wilson et al., 1991). A competition pause, often 1 s or more, removes most of it.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 17 resistance-trained athletes, adding a 2 s pause between lowering and pressing made bench press and squat load–velocity testing more consistent (Pallarés et al., 2014). Paused reps are more repeatable, which also helps when tracking bar speed.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Pause length depends on the head referee; train variable pauses so the lifter can stay tight for 1–2 s. Sinking the bar into the chest during the pause, or heaving it off the chest, is a red light.

:::tip[In plain words]
When you pause the bar on your chest, the spring from lowering it fades within about a second. So in a meet you're pressing from almost a dead stop. Practise paused reps often, with pauses of different lengths, and keep the bar still without sinking it.
:::

## Bracing and breathing

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review found that the bench press produced the lowest intra-abdominal pressure of the exercises studied (Blazek et al., 2019). Bracing still matters for keeping the chest high and the body stable.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Take the breath before "Start" (or before each rep in training), hold it through the press, and breathe at the top. Very long sets may need a small top-up breath at lockout.

:::tip[In plain words]
Take a big breath before you lower the bar and hold it until the bar is back up. It keeps your chest high and your body tight.
:::

## Variations and what they train

Selection and the specificity ladder are covered in [Part 1 · Chapter 8](/lets-git-it/foundations/08-exercise-selection/). This table focuses on the technique job of each.

| Variation | What changes | Technique job | Evidence and notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Long-pause bench** (2–3 s) | Longer pause | Tightness and strength off the chest; no sinking | <Evidence level="practice" /> |
| **Spoto press** (pause 2–3 cm above the chest) | Stops before touching | Control and bar path just off the chest | <Evidence level="practice" /> |
| **Close-grip bench** (about shoulder width) | Narrower grip | Triceps, lockout; shoulder-friendly | <Evidence level="emerging" /> Lifts less load but moves faster (Lockie et al., 2017) |
| **Wide-grip bench** (at the 81 cm limit) | Wider grip | Shorter range; pec and shoulder emphasis | <Evidence level="emerging" /> Similar load to medium grip (Larsen et al., 2021) |
| **Larsen press** (feet up, legs straight) | No leg drive | Upper-body stability and position without leg drive | <Evidence level="practice" /> Training only; the feet must stay flat in competition |
| **Touch-and-go bench** | No pause | Volume at heavier loads | <Evidence level="practice" /> Less specific |
| **Pin press, board press** | Partial range | Top-half strength, overload | <Evidence level="emerging" /> Partial-range training transferred less to full range (Martínez-Cava et al., 2022); use as a supplement |
| **Incline bench** | Bench angle | Upper chest and shoulders | <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 bench athletes, incline 6RM was about 19–22% lower than flat (Saeterbakken et al., 2017) |
| **Dumbbell bench press** | Free arms, deeper range | Muscle, side-to-side balance | <Evidence level="practice" /> |
| **Tempo bench** (3–4 s down) | Slower lowering | Bar path and touch-point consistency | <Evidence level="practice" /> |

:::tip[In plain words]
Each bench variation changes one thing to fix one problem: a longer pause for weakness off the chest, a close grip for lockout, feet-up for stability, slow lowering for a messy bar path. Pick the one that matches where your bench goes wrong.
:::

## Cues that work

<Evidence level="practice" /> Coaching practice; one or two cues per set. Faults and fixes are covered in Part 5, Chapter 5 (Common technical faults).

| Problem | Cue | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Shoulders roll forward at the bottom | "Shoulder blades in your back pockets" | Keeps the scapulae set |
| Bar drifts towards the face | "Touch low, press back" | Consistent touch point and path |
| Elbows flare early | "Bend the bar" or "elbows under the bar" | Keeps forearms vertical |
| Sinks the bar on the pause | "Touch the shirt, hold your chest up" | Legal, tight pause |
| Butt lifts | "Push yourself up the bench" | Leg drive horizontal, hips down |
| Presses before the command | "Wait for the word" | Command discipline |

## Sample programs

Loads use the RPE chart from [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/). Variations are given as RPE only, because their maxes differ from the competition bench.

### Program 1: Learning the competition bench (novice, 6 weeks)

A novice male lifter (bench about 60 kg), three full-body sessions a week (Monday, Wednesday, Friday). The aim is a repeatable setup and a legal pause before chasing load.

| Weeks | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Setup drill (empty bar): set up, unrack, hold 5 s, rack | 3 × 3 | Empty bar | Steps 1–7 of the setup every time |
| 1–2 | Bench press, 1 s pause on each rep | 3 × 5 | Start at 40 kg, RPE ≤6 | Touch point; feet flat |
| 3–4 | Bench press, 1 s pause on each rep | 3 × 5 | Add 2.5 kg per session while RPE ≤7 | Film side view weekly |
| 3–4 | Dumbbell bench press | 3 × 10 | RPE 7–8 | Muscle; full range |
| 5–6 | Bench press, first rep of each set with commands ("Start", "Press", "Rack") | 3 × 5 | Keep adding 2.5 kg per session while RPE ≤7 | Command discipline |
| 5–6 | Close-grip bench (Fridays) | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 | Triceps |

**Why it is built this way:** the setup is practised separately with an empty bar until it is automatic. Every rep is paused from day one so the lifter never learns to rely on a bounce. Commands are added once the setup is stable.

### Program 2: Moving to a wider grip (intermediate, 6 weeks)

Intermediate lifter (bench 120 kg) with a medium grip, long arms and healthy shoulders, testing a wider grip near the 81 cm limit. Bench on Monday, Wednesday and Friday.

| Week | Monday | Wednesday | Friday | Load notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Current grip 4 × 5; new grip 3 × 5 | New grip 4 × 6 | Close-grip bench 3 × 6 | Current grip RPE 7 (~79%, 95 kg). New grip: start at 80 kg, add 2.5–5 kg per session while RPE ≤7. Close grip RPE 7 |
| 3–4 | New grip 4 × 4 | Current grip 4 × 5 | Long-pause bench (new grip, 3 s) 3 × 4 | New grip by RPE 7.5. Current grip RPE 7 (95 kg). Long pause RPE 7 |
| 5 | New grip 3 × 3 | Current grip 3 × 4 | Close-grip bench 3 × 5 | New grip by RPE 8. Current grip RPE 7 (~81%, 97.5 kg) |
| 6 | New grip: work up to 1 × 1 at RPE 8, then 2 × 3 at ~90% of that single | Light day: new grip 3 × 3 at RPE 6 | Current grip 1 × 1 at RPE 8 (~92%, 110 kg), then 2 × 3 at 100 kg | Compare the two singles |

**How to decide:** estimate each 1RM from the RPE 8 single (≈ 92% of 1RM). Keep the wider grip only if it is equal or stronger **and** the shoulders feel fine. <Evidence level="emerging" /> Studies show medium and wide grips lift similar loads (Saeterbakken et al., 2017; Larsen et al., 2021), so a big gain is unlikely; the main reason to switch is a shorter range for a long-armed lifter. If the front of the shoulder or the top of the shoulder (AC joint) becomes sore, move the grip back in.

### Program 3: Pause and commands discipline (female intermediate, 4 weeks)

Female lifter in the 63 kg class (bench 65 kg) who has been red-lighted for sinking the bar and pressing early. Bench on Monday, Wednesday and Friday. Every competition-bench set is filmed from the side to check the pause, elbow depth and feet.

| Week | Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mon | Bench press, every rep with commands | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~81%, 52.5 kg) |
| 1 | Wed | Long-pause bench (3 s) | 4 × 3 | RPE 7 |
| 1 | Fri | Larsen press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 2 | Mon | Bench press, every rep with commands | 4 × 4 | RPE 7.5 (~82%, 52.5 kg) |
| 2 | Wed | Spoto press | 4 × 3 | RPE 7 |
| 2 | Fri | Bench press, random pause 1–3 s (partner calls "Press") | 5 × 2 | RPE 7 (~86%, 55 kg) |
| 3 | Mon | Bench press, commands; single then back-off sets | 1 × 1, then 3 × 3 | Single RPE 8 (~92%, 60 kg); triples RPE 7.5 (~85%, 55 kg) |
| 3 | Wed | Long-pause bench (3 s) | 4 × 3 | RPE 7.5 |
| 3 | Fri | Bench press, random pause 1–3 s | 5 × 2 | RPE 7.5 (~88%, 57.5 kg) |
| 4 | Mon | Bench press, commands | 2 × 4 | 50 kg (volume deload) |
| 4 | Wed | Long-pause bench (3 s) | 2 × 3 | RPE 6 |
| 4 | Fri | Bench press, random pause 1–3 s | 2 × 2 | 52.5 kg |

**Why it is built this way:** long pauses and Spoto presses teach the lifter to stay tight and keep the bar still; random-length pauses with a partner's "Press" command remove the habit of pressing on a fixed rhythm; the Larsen press builds upper-body stability without relying on leg drive. Week 4 cuts the bench sets from 13 in week 3 to 6 at similar or slightly lighter loads.

## Common mistakes

- **A different setup every set.** Set grip by the rings and run the same setup steps every time, warm-ups included.
- **Training touch-and-go only.** The competition pause removes the rebound; most heavy work should be paused.
- **Sinking or heaving.** Both are red lights; train the pause with commands.
- **Lifting the buttocks or heels.** Set the feet where they stay flat under maximal effort.
- **Extreme arch without checking elbow depth.** The 2026 rules require the elbows to reach shoulder level.
- **Choosing a grip by fashion.** Narrow grips cost load; very wide grips may load the shoulders more. Test medium and wide.
- **Ignoring shoulder pain.** Narrow the grip, reduce volume, and see a qualified health professional if pain persists; see Part 5, Chapter 9 (Pain and common powerlifting injuries).

## Key takeaways

- Competition bench: head, shoulders and buttocks on the bench, feet flat, thumbs around the bar, grip ≤81 cm, motionless pause until "Press", elbows to shoulder level (IPF 2026).
- Retracted shoulder blades and a legal arch give a stable base and a shorter press; arching did not increase overall shoulder loading in one study.
- Narrow grips lower the load lifted; medium and wide grips are similar; wider grips may raise shoulder joint loads.
- Skilled benchers lower under control, touch the lower chest and press back towards the shoulders.
- The stretch from lowering fades within about a second; the paused bench must be trained as a pressing skill from a near dead stop.
- Choose variations by the fault they fix, and test whether they worked.
