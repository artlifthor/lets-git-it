## In one minute

A powerlifter does not need to be very flexible; they need **enough range to reach each lift's positions, and strength in those positions**. Full-range strength training improves range of motion about as much as stretching does, so for most lifters squatting, benching and pulling through a full range *is* the main mobility work. Stretching and foam rolling also increase range over weeks, but short-term gains mostly come from a higher **tolerance** to the stretch, and neither one makes you stronger in any meaningful way. Mobility work earns its place when a specific restriction stops a lifter reaching a position: ankle dorsiflexion and hip flexion for squat depth, shoulder and upper-back extension for the bench, hip range for the deadlift setup. Test the restriction, treat it with a short targeted routine, and retest after 4–6 weeks.

## Flexibility, mobility and strength in range

These three words get used as if they meant the same thing. They don't.

| Term | What it means | Example | How you train it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Flexibility** | How far a joint can be moved **passively** (by gravity, a partner, a strap) | A coach can push your knee forward over your toes | Stretching, foam rolling, loaded full-range lifting |
| **Mobility** | How far you can move a joint **actively**, under your own control | You can squat to depth with your heels down and your back in position | Active drills, loaded full-range lifting, practising the position |
| **Strength in range** | How much force you can produce **in** the position the lift needs | You can stand up out of the bottom of a heavy squat without losing position | Heavy and paused lifts through the full range |

<Evidence level="practice" /> For powerlifting, flexibility is only useful when it turns into mobility, and mobility only matters when it supports strength in the positions of the lifts. A lifter with average flexibility who can hold a solid bottom position under load does not need more range. A lifter who can't reach depth without the lower back rounding or the heels lifting does.

:::tip[In plain words]
Being bendy isn't the goal. You need enough movement to get into a good squat, bench and deadlift position, and the strength to hold that position under a heavy bar. Anything beyond that is a bonus, not a requirement.
:::

## Why range of motion improves

- <Evidence level="practice" /> A review of the mechanisms concluded that the range gained after a single stretching session, and after programmes of 3–8 weeks, mostly comes from **changed sensation** (a higher tolerance to the stretch), not from the muscle actually getting longer. Muscle length does increase during a stretch, but only briefly (Weppler & Magnusson, 2010). Longer-term effects were not well studied at the time.

**What this means.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Range improves partly because the nervous system learns that the end of range is safe. That is one reason why **loading a position** (pause squats, deep goblet squats, paused bench) works as mobility training: it teaches the lifter that the position is safe *and* builds strength there.

:::tip[In plain words]
When you stretch and get more range, it's mostly your body learning to tolerate the stretch, not your muscles getting longer. Practising the actual position under a weight teaches the same thing and makes you strong there too.
:::

## What the evidence says

### Stretching before lifting

- <Evidence level="strong" /> Static stretching of 60 seconds or more per muscle can reduce strength for a while; shorter stretches have little effect, especially when followed by dynamic activity (Behm et al., 2016; Simic et al., 2013). The details are in [Part 1 · Chapter 9](/lets-git-it/foundations/09-session-structure-warm-ups/).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A review of dynamic stretching (controlled, active movements through range) found substantial evidence that it increases range of motion and can improve subsequent force, power, sprint and jump performance, though some studies found no change or a loss, depending on duration, amplitude and speed. Controlled dynamic stretching appeared more beneficial than fast, bouncing (ballistic) stretching (Opplert & Babault, 2018).

**What this means.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Before lifting, use **dynamic drills and short static holds (under about 30–60 seconds per muscle)** only where they help the lifter reach a position, then do the specific warm-up sets. Save long holds for after training or another time.

:::tip[In plain words]
Before lifting, use moving stretches and short holds only for the spots that stop you getting into position. Long, slow stretching is fine, just after training or on another day.
:::

### Stretching over weeks: does it make you stronger?

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 42 studies (1,318 participants) found that weeks of static stretching produced **small** increases in maximal strength (effect size about 0.30) and very small increases in muscle size (about 0.20), with larger effects from longer stretching durations and longer programmes. The authors described stretching as of "minor effectiveness" compared with resistance training (Warneke et al., 2024).

**What this means.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Regular stretching won't hurt a powerlifter's strength, and it may help slightly, but it is not a strength tool. Its role is range of motion, when range is the limiter.

:::tip[In plain words]
Stretching regularly won't make you weaker, and it might make you a tiny bit stronger, but lifting does that far better. Stretch to get range when you need it, not to build strength.
:::

### Foam rolling

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Before training:** rolling slightly improved flexibility and had a negligible effect on strength (Wiewelhove et al., 2019).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> **Over weeks:** a meta-analysis of 11 studies (290 participants) found that foam rolling programmes produced a moderate increase in range of motion, but programmes needed to last **more than 4 weeks**. Rolling the hamstrings and quadriceps increased range; **rolling the calves did not improve ankle dorsiflexion** (Konrad et al., 2022).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A larger meta-analysis (72 stretching and 13 foam rolling studies) found that both increased range of motion with moderate effects and did not differ overall; for programmes of 4 weeks or less, static stretching worked better (Konrad et al., 2024).

**What this means.** <Evidence level="practice" /> Foam rolling is optional. It is a reasonable, low-cost tool if a lifter likes it, but for the ankle (the most common squat restriction) the evidence favours other methods, and for short-term range gains stretching does more.

:::tip[In plain words]
Foam rolling won't make you stronger. Over a couple of months it can loosen your thighs a bit, but it didn't help tight ankles in the research. Use it if you like it; skip it if you don't.
:::

### Does strength training improve flexibility?

- <Evidence level="strong" /> A meta-analysis of 55 studies found that resistance training **increased range of motion** (effect size about 0.73), with no significant difference from stretching, and no extra benefit from adding stretching to resistance training. Training with external loads worked; bodyweight-only training did not significantly improve range. Untrained people improved more than trained people, but trained people still improved (Alizadeh et al., 2023).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> An earlier meta-analysis of 11 randomised trials (452 participants) also found no difference between strength training and stretching for improving range of motion, though the studies were very varied (Afonso et al., 2021).

**What this means.** <Evidence level="practice" /> The myth that lifting makes people "muscle-bound" and stiff is not supported. **Full-range lifting is mobility training.** Deep squats, paused bench presses, Romanian deadlifts and deficit pulls train range and strength at the same time. Separate stretching is needed only when a specific restriction remains.

:::tip[In plain words]
Lifting weights through a full range makes you more flexible, about as much as stretching does. Deep squats and full-range presses are already mobility work. Add stretching only for a spot that still holds you back.
:::

## Mobility needs for each lift

Each lift has a few positions that demand range. If a lifter can reach them under load with good position, mobility is not their problem.

| Lift | Joint and range needed | Signs of a restriction | Common fixes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Squat** | **Ankle dorsiflexion** (knees travel forward over the toes) | Heels lift; lifter can't reach depth without the torso tipping far forward; knees cave as the ankles run out of range | Ankle mobility drills; raised-heel squat shoes; slightly wider stance with toes turned out more; loaded deep squats |
| Squat | **Hip flexion** (thighs come up toward the chest) | Pelvis tucks under at the bottom ("butt wink") before depth; depth only with a very wide stance | Find the stance width and toe angle that fit the lifter's hips; hip flexion drills; deep goblet squats |
| Squat (low-bar) | **Shoulder external rotation and upper-back extension** (to hold the bar) | Elbow or wrist pain; can't get hands in; bar sits unstably | Wider grip; thumbs over the bar; shoulder drills; a higher bar position if needed |
| **Bench press** | **Upper-back (thoracic) extension** and **shoulder extension** | Can't set the shoulder blades and arch; shoulders roll forward at the bottom | Thoracic extension drills; shoulder-blade setup practice; paused bench |
| Bench press | **Hip extension** (for leg drive with the feet back) | Can't get the feet back without lifting the hips | Hip-flexor stretch; adjust foot position |
| **Deadlift (conventional)** | **Hip flexion with a straight back** (hamstrings) | Back rounds before the hands reach the bar | Romanian deadlifts through range; hamstring drills; setup practice; slightly higher start (if allowed by the lifter's proportions) |
| **Deadlift (sumo)** | **Hip abduction and external rotation** (adductors) | Can't get the hips close to the bar or the knees out | Adductor drills; Copenhagen planks; narrower stance until range improves |

### The ankle: the most common squat restriction

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 101 healthy adults, ankle dorsiflexion (measured with the knee bent) and hip flexion range were linked with how deep men could squat; in women, ankle dorsiflexion (knee straight) and the strength of the muscles that lift the foot were linked (Kim et al., 2015).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 30 active adults, restricting ankle dorsiflexion with a wedge under the forefoot during a double-leg squat **reduced knee bend and increased knee valgus** (knees moving in), with less quadriceps activity (Macrum et al., 2012).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 17 studies found that people with dynamic knee valgus tended to have less ankle dorsiflexion (Lima et al., 2018). See [Chapter 6](/lets-git-it/technique-injury/06-planes-of-motion/) for knee cave.

**Squat shoes.** Raised-heel shoes reduce the ankle range a squat needs.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 32 participants, weightlifting shoes reduced ankle bend and increased knee bend compared with athletic shoes, and gave a more upright trunk in unloaded squats; experienced squatters used the effect more than novices (Legg et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> But in 14 recreational lifters squatting 80% of 1RM, raised-heel postures (shoes or a platform) **did not change** trunk angles or muscle activity (Lee et al., 2019).

<Evidence level="practice" /> So shoes are a reasonable, immediate fix for a lifter whose ankles limit depth, but don't expect them to change the trunk angle of an experienced lifter's heavy squat. Choosing shoes and other equipment is covered in Part 5, Chapter 11 (Equipment).

:::tip[In plain words]
If your heels lift or you can't squat deep without folding forward, your ankles are often the reason. Squat shoes with a raised heel help straight away; ankle drills help over weeks. Check your ankles before blaming your hips.
:::

### Hips: fit the stance to the lifter

<Evidence level="practice" /> Hip structure differs between people (the depth and angle of the hip socket, the angle of the thigh bone), and these differences can't be stretched away. A lifter whose pelvis tucks under at the bottom of a narrow squat may reach depth comfortably with a wider stance and more toe-out. Experiment with stance before prescribing weeks of hip stretching. Horschig's *The Squat Bible* takes a similar approach to squat mobility: assess the squat, find the setup that suits the lifter's body, then work on the specific joint that still limits the position.

:::tip[In plain words]
Everyone's hips are built a bit differently, and stretching can't change bone shape. Try a wider or narrower stance and different toe angles first. Often the right stance fixes what looked like tight hips.
:::

## Screening tests (and how much to trust them)

The point of a mobility test is narrow: **does a restriction stop this lifter reaching a lift position, and is it changing?** It is not to predict injury.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 24 studies found that the Functional Movement Screen composite score had only a small association with later injury in military personnel, and recommended **against** using it to predict injury in football; evidence in other groups was limited or conflicting (Moran et al., 2017). Movement screens are not injury crystal balls.
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 12 studies found the **weight-bearing lunge test** (knee-to-wall) for ankle dorsiflexion to be reliable, both for the same tester and between testers. A change of about **1.6–1.9 cm** (or 4.6–4.7°) is needed to be sure it is a real change rather than measurement error (Powden et al., 2015).

| Test | What it checks | How to do it | Evidence for the test | How to use it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Knee-to-wall (weight-bearing lunge)** | Ankle dorsiflexion | Half-kneeling-style lunge facing a wall, heel down; slide the foot back until the knee just touches the wall; measure toe-to-wall distance | Reliable; real change ≈ 2 cm (Powden et al., 2015) | Compare left and right; retest every 4–6 weeks |
| **Bodyweight deep squat** (arms forward) | Combined ankle, hip and trunk range | Squat as deep as possible with heels down; film from the side and front | Coaching practice; no validated scoring for powerlifters | Watch for heels lifting, pelvis tucking, knees caving; compare with and without a heel raise |
| **Heel-raise comparison** | Whether the ankle is the limiter | Repeat the bodyweight squat with heels on a 2–3 cm plate | Coaching practice | If depth and position improve a lot, the ankle is the main limiter |
| **Knee-to-chest (supine)** | Hip flexion | Lying on the back, pull one knee toward the chest; note when the pelvis starts to tilt | Coaching practice | Compare sides; relevant if the pelvis tucks at squat depth |
| **90/90 hip rotation** | Hip internal and external rotation | Sit with both knees bent at 90°, one leg in front and one to the side; switch without hands | Coaching practice | Large side-to-side differences can go with a hip shift (see [Chapter 6](/lets-git-it/technique-injury/06-planes-of-motion/)) |
| **Back-to-wall arm raise** | Shoulder flexion and upper-back extension | Stand with back and head against a wall; raise straight arms overhead | Coaching practice | Relevant for low-bar position and the bench arch |
| **Active straight-leg raise** | Hamstring range | Lying on the back, raise one straight leg as high as possible | Coaching practice | Relevant if the back rounds before reaching the bar in the deadlift |

**The rule:** <Evidence level="practice" /> a test result matters only if the lift shows the matching problem. A lifter with "poor" ankle range who squats to depth with a solid position under 200 kg doesn't need ankle work.

:::tip[In plain words]
Mobility tests can't tell you whether you'll get hurt. Use them to answer one question: is a stiff joint stopping you getting into a lift position? If your lifts look fine, a "bad" test score doesn't matter.
:::

## A decision process

1. **Watch the lift.** Is there a position problem (heels lift, pelvis tucks, can't set the shoulders, back rounds at the setup)?
2. **Rule out setup.** Try stance, grip, toe angle and bar position changes first.
3. **Test the likely joint.** Use the tests above; compare left and right.
4. **Treat for 4–6 weeks:** a short daily or pre-session drill for the restricted joint, plus **loaded full-range work** in that position (goblet squats, pause squats, paused bench, Romanian deadlifts), plus equipment if it helps (squat shoes).
5. **Retest** the test and the lift. If the lift position is fine, stop the extra drills and let normal training maintain it.

<Evidence level="practice" /> If a restriction comes with pain, has appeared suddenly, or follows an injury, see a qualified health professional rather than stretching into it. Pain is covered in Part 5, Chapter 9 (Pain and common powerlifting injuries).

:::tip[In plain words]
Look at your lift first, try a different setup, then test the stiff spot. Work on it for four to six weeks with a short drill and some full-range lifting, then check again. If the position looks good, you can drop the extra drill.
:::

## Sample routines

Three routines for an intermediate lifter (default lifter: male, 83 kg, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg). Each one goes **after** a few minutes of general warm-up (bike, rower or brisk walk) and **before** the specific warm-up sets. Holds before lifting stay under about 30–60 seconds per muscle.

### Routine 1: 10-minute pre-squat routine

**For:** a lifter whose knee-to-wall test is 7 cm on the right and 4 cm on the left, whose heels rise slightly at depth, and whose pelvis tucks under just before depth.

| Order | Drill | Dose | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Knee-to-wall ankle rocks | 2 × 10 per side (extra set on the left) | Ankle dorsiflexion, actively |
| 2 | Kneeling hip-flexor stretch with reach | 2 × 30 s per side | Hip extension; helps a neutral pelvis |
| 3 | Bodyweight squat with a 3–5 s pause at the bottom, holding a light plate in front | 2 × 5 | Hip and ankle range in the actual position |
| 4 | Goblet squat, 2 s pause | 2 × 5 at a light-moderate weight | Load the bottom position; practise knees out and heels down |
| 5 | Banded lateral walk | 1 × 10 steps each way | Wake up the hip abductors |
| 6 | Empty-bar squats in squat shoes | 1 × 5–8 | Rehearse the lift |

Then begin squat warm-up sets as usual (for example 60 kg × 5, 100 kg × 3, 130 kg × 2, 150 kg × 1 before work sets). **Total time:** about 10 minutes.

**Why it's built this way.** The left ankle gets an extra set because it is more restricted. Static holds are short. The paused goblet squat does what stretching can't: it practises and loads the exact position, which is how range carries over to the lift. **Retest** knee-to-wall after 4–6 weeks: a gain of about 2 cm or more is a real change (Powden et al., 2015).

### Routine 2: Bench press shoulder prep (8 minutes)

**For:** a lifter who struggles to set the shoulder blades and hold an arch, and whose front shoulders feel "pinchy" at the bottom of the bench.

| Order | Drill | Dose | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Thoracic extension over a foam roller or bench edge | 2 × 8 slow reps | Upper-back extension for the arch |
| 2 | Open-book rotation (side-lying) | 1 × 8 per side | Upper-back rotation and chest opening |
| 3 | Band pull-apart | 2 × 15 | Shoulder-blade retraction; upper back |
| 4 | Band external rotation, elbow at side | 2 × 15 per side | Rotator cuff |
| 5 | Scapular push-up | 1 × 10 | Shoulder-blade control |
| 6 | Doorway or bench pec stretch | 2 × 20–30 s per side | Chest and front shoulder range |
| 7 | Empty-bar bench with a 2 s pause, full setup each rep | 1 × 8 | Rehearse the arch and shoulder-blade set |

Then bench warm-up sets as usual. **Total time:** about 8 minutes.

**Why it's built this way.** The drills move from upper-back range to shoulder-blade control to rehearsal of the setup. The pec stretch is short so it won't blunt pressing strength. If the shoulder pain persists or worsens, the lifter should see a qualified health professional; prep work is not a treatment.

### Routine 3: Off-day mobility (20–25 minutes)

**For:** the same lifter on one or two rest days a week. Off days are where longer holds belong, because there's no heavy lifting to blunt. Stretching programmes need several weeks to show results (Konrad et al., 2024).

| Order | Drill | Dose | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Easy cardio | 5 min | Warm the tissues |
| 2 | Half-kneeling ankle stretch (knee over toes, weight on the front foot) | 3 × 45–60 s per side, extra set on the restricted side | Ankle dorsiflexion |
| 3 | Deep squat hold (holding a support), shifting side to side | 3 × 30–45 s | Hip and ankle range in the squat position |
| 4 | 90/90 hip switches | 2 × 8 per side | Hip rotation, actively |
| 5 | Couch stretch (rear foot against a wall) | 2 × 45–60 s per side | Hip flexors and quadriceps |
| 6 | Seated or lying hamstring stretch | 2 × 45–60 s per side | Hamstring range for the deadlift setup |
| 7 | Thoracic extension over a foam roller | 2 × 10 | Upper-back extension |
| 8 | Goblet squat with a long pause (5 s) | 2 × 5 | Loaded range in the squat position |

**Progression:** add 15 s to each hold every two weeks, up to about 90 s. **Retest** after 6 weeks; if the squat position is solid and the tests have improved, drop to one session a week or stop.

**Why it's built this way.** Longer holds over several weeks are what the stretching research supports for range gains (Konrad et al., 2024; Warneke et al., 2024). The session ends with a loaded position, so the range is used, not just gained.

## Common mistakes

- **Doing long static stretches right before heavy lifting.** Keep pre-lifting holds short; save long holds for after training or off days.
- **Stretching for strength.** Stretching may add a little strength over months, but lifting does far more.
- **Mobility work with no target.** If you can't name the position it fixes, it is probably not needed.
- **Trusting screens to predict injury.** Movement-screen scores are weak predictors; use tests to track a specific restriction.
- **Stretching what is really a setup problem.** Try stance, toe angle, grip and shoes first.
- **Foam rolling calves to fix the ankle.** The research found it didn't improve dorsiflexion; use loaded and active ankle work instead.
- **Never retesting.** Without a before-and-after test you can't tell whether the routine worked or can be dropped.
- **Stretching into pain.** Persistent or sudden restriction with pain needs a qualified health professional.

## Key takeaways

- Powerlifters need enough range to reach each lift's positions, plus strength in those positions, not maximal flexibility.
- Short-term range gains mostly come from increased stretch tolerance; loading the position teaches tolerance and builds strength together.
- Full-range resistance training improves range of motion about as much as stretching; stretching adds little strength.
- Foam rolling is optional; it increases range over more than 4 weeks but did not improve ankle dorsiflexion.
- The ankle is the most common squat restriction; test it with knee-to-wall (a change of about 2 cm is real) and consider squat shoes.
- Use tests to track specific restrictions, not to predict injury; retest every 4–6 weeks and drop drills that are no longer needed.
