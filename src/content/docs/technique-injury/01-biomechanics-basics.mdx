## In one minute

Every barbell lift is a contest between the **moment** (turning effect) the bar creates around each joint and the moment the lifter's muscles can produce. A moment is force × moment arm, where the moment arm is the **horizontal distance** from the joint to the line of the bar's weight. Small changes in position change moment arms by a few centimetres, and a few centimetres under 200 kg is a large change in demand. Body proportions, bar position, stance and grip decide how that demand is shared between the hips, knees, back, shoulders and elbows. Technique is mostly the art of sharing it well: you can move load between joints, but you cannot make it disappear.

## Levers and moment arms

A joint is a hinge. The bone is the lever, the joint is the pivot, and two forces try to turn the lever in opposite directions:

- the **external force**: the bar's weight (plus the weight of the body segments above the joint), pulling straight down;
- the **internal force**: a muscle pulling on the bone through its tendon.

**Moment (torque) = force × moment arm.** The moment arm is the **perpendicular distance** from the joint to the line along which the force acts. For the bar's weight, which always acts straight down, that is simply the **horizontal distance** between the joint and the bar, seen from the side.

Two consequences follow:

1. **Only horizontal distance matters for the bar.** A bar can be 80 cm above the hip and still create no hip moment if it sits directly above it.
2. **Muscles work at a mechanical disadvantage.** Most muscles attach a few centimetres from the joint, while the load sits much farther away. The muscle must pull with a force many times larger than the load. This is the price of speed and range: a short muscle shortening a little moves the end of the limb a lot.

<Evidence level="practice" /> These are basic mechanics used throughout biomechanics research; the numbers in the worked examples below are illustrations, not measurements of a real lifter.

| Term | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Force | A push or pull; weight = mass × 9.81 | newtons (N) |
| Moment arm | Perpendicular (for the bar: horizontal) distance from joint to force | metres (m) |
| Moment, torque | Turning effect = force × moment arm | newton-metres (N·m) |
| External moment | Moment the bar and body create around a joint | N·m |
| Net joint moment | Moment the muscles must produce to control that joint | N·m |

:::tip[In plain words]
A joint is like a door hinge. The farther from the hinge you push, the easier the door turns. When the bar is far from a joint (measured sideways, not up and down), it pushes much harder on that joint, and your muscles must pull much harder to hold it.
:::

### Worked example 1: the squat, one position

Our intermediate lifter (squat 180 kg) is at the bottom of a squat.

- Bar weight: 180 kg × 9.81 ≈ **1,770 N**.
- Side view: the bar is **25 cm** in front of the hip joint and **10 cm** behind the knee joint.

| Joint | Moment arm | Moment from the bar alone |
|---|---|---|
| Hip | 0.25 m | 1,770 × 0.25 ≈ **440 N·m** |
| Knee | 0.10 m | 1,770 × 0.10 ≈ **177 N·m** |

The body's own mass above each joint adds to these numbers, and accelerating the bar adds more. The point is the ratio: in this position, the hips carry about 2.5 times the moment the knees do.

### Worked example 2: how much the muscle really pulls

If the quadriceps pull through the patellar tendon with a moment arm of about **4 cm** (an illustrative figure; it varies between people and with knee angle), holding that 177 N·m at the knee needs:

177 ÷ 0.04 ≈ **4,400 N** of quadriceps force, roughly the weight of 450 kg, to control a 180 kg bar. This is why muscle size matters so much for strength, and why tendons and joints need gradual loading.

### Worked example 3: a few centimetres matter

The same lifter lets the bar drift **5 cm** forward, so it now sits 30 cm in front of the hip:

1,770 × 0.30 ≈ **531 N·m**, about **90 N·m more** than before, a 20% jump in hip and back demand from a 5 cm drift. A useful rule: **for a 200 kg bar, every extra centimetre of moment arm adds about 20 N·m.**

:::tip[In plain words]
Your muscles pull several times harder than the weight on the bar, because they attach close to the joint. And a bar that drifts just a few centimetres forward can make the lift 20% harder for your hips and back. Small position errors cost a lot.
:::

## Moving load between joints, not removing it

A lifter balanced over the feet must keep the bar (and the combined centre of mass of lifter and bar) over the middle of the foot. If one joint's moment arm shrinks, another's usually grows.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 7 weight-trained men squatting to parallel with a barbell equal to body weight, blocking the knees from moving past the toes cut static knee torque from about 150 to 117 N·m but raised static hip torque from about 28 to 303 N·m, with more forward trunk lean (Fry et al., 2003).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 20 people squatting with light loads, unrestricted squats (knees allowed past the toes) produced higher knee moments and lower hip moments than restricted squats (Lorenzetti et al., 2012).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 male powerlifters squatting at 30–70% of 1RM, a narrow "traditional" squat (knees forward) produced the largest spine and ankle moments, while a wide-stance "powerlifting" squat with a more vertical shin produced the largest hip moments (Swinton et al., 2012).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 19 male powerlifters, a hexagonal (trap) bar, which lets the lifter stand inside the load, lowered peak moments at the lumbar spine, hip and ankle and raised the peak knee moment compared with a straight bar. Their 1RM was higher with the hexagonal bar (265 vs. 245 kg) (Swinton et al., 2011).

**Decision rule:** when you change technique, ask "which joint gets more, which gets less, and is that joint the stronger one for this lifter?"

:::tip[In plain words]
Changing your technique doesn't make a lift easier overall; it shifts the work from one joint to another. Keeping your knees back makes your hips and back work harder. Good technique puts the work on the joints and muscles that can handle it best.
:::

## Force–velocity: heavy means slow

Muscles produce **less force the faster they shorten**. This is the force–velocity relationship, first described mathematically in isolated muscle by Hill (1938).

- <Evidence level="practice" /> Consequences for powerlifting: a true 1RM moves slowly because it requires near-maximal force; lighter loads move faster because less force is needed. Bar speed therefore tracks how close a load is to maximal, which is the basis of velocity-based training ([Part 3 · Chapter 3](/lets-git-it/advanced/03-velocity-based-training/)).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> A slow, grinding rep is not a technique fault in itself; it is what maximal force looks like. Position breakdown during the grind is the fault.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> During the eccentric (lowering) phase, muscles can resist more load than they can lift. Lifters can control a descent with weights they could not press or stand up with.

:::tip[In plain words]
The heavier the weight, the slower it moves, because your muscles can't push hard and move fast at the same time. A slow max lift is normal. What matters is that your position holds while the bar is slow.
:::

## Length–tension: muscles have a best length

A muscle fibre produces most force at a middle length, where its contractile filaments overlap best, and less when very stretched or very shortened.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In single frog muscle fibres, isometric tension was highest on a plateau of sarcomere lengths and fell off at longer lengths and, more steeply, at very short lengths, which the sliding-filament theory explains (Gordon et al., 1966). This is basic physiology from isolated fibres, not from lifting.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> In a whole lift, strength at each point depends on **both** the muscle's length (length–tension) **and** its moment arm, which also changes with joint angle. Stretched muscles also store some elastic energy, which helps when the turn-around is quick.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 experienced male lifters bench pressing 95% of 1RM, the extra performance gained from the stretch on the way down faded as the pause got longer, with a half-life of about 0.85 s (Wilson et al., 1991). A competition pause removes most of this help.

:::tip[In plain words]
Muscles are strongest in the middle of their range and weaker when fully stretched or fully squeezed. Bouncing out of the bottom gives you a little free spring, but about half of it is gone within a second, and most of it within two. That's why a paused bench feels harder than a touch-and-go.
:::

## Sticking regions: where demand beats capacity

A **sticking region** is the part of a lift where the bar slows (sometimes to a near stop) after the initial push. Mechanically, it is the zone where the lifter's available joint moments are lowest relative to what the bar demands, and where any help from the stretch at the bottom has faded.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> A review describes the sticking point as arising from the combination of muscle force capacity and changing leverage through the range of motion (Kompf & Arandjelović, 2016). A second review compared sticking points in the three lifts and noted that they differ in where and why they occur (Kompf & Arandjelović, 2017).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 recreational lifters, every 1RM bench press showed a sticking period that began about 0.2 s after the press started and lasted about 0.9 s (van den Tillaar & Ettema, 2010).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 25 recreationally trained lifters doing 3RM squats, the sticking region coincided with more forward torso lean, a longer hip moment arm and a larger hip share of the total joint moment (Larsen et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 experienced male powerlifters, the bench press sticking region did **not** occur at the same elbow and shoulder angles across narrow, medium and wide grips; the authors suggested the moment arm of the bar about the elbow may explain it (Gomo & van den Tillaar, 2016).

Diagnosing sticking points and turning them into exercise choices is covered in [Part 3 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/advanced/05-weak-point-analysis/) and Part 5, Chapter 7 (From sticking point to accessory).

:::tip[In plain words]
Every lift has a hardest spot, usually a little way after you start pushing, when the spring from the bottom has worn off and your leverage is at its worst. That's where the bar slows down. Knowing why it slows there helps you pick the right fix.
:::

## Joint moments in the three lifts

### Squat

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> At 65% of 1RM, six national-class powerlifters squatting low-bar had peak hip moments of about 309–324 N·m and knee moments of about 92–139 N·m. Eight national-class weightlifters squatting high-bar had hip moments of about 216–230 N·m and knee moments of about 131–191 N·m. The powerlifters put relatively more load on the hip (Wretenberg et al., 1996). The two bar positions were done by different athletes, so bar position and athlete type are mixed together.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 10 strength-trained women squatting 50–90% of 1RM, the effort required from the knee extensors (relative to their maximal strength at each angle) rose with **depth but not load**, while the hip extensors' effort rose with **both depth and load**, and the ankle plantar flexors' effort rose with load (Bryanton et al., 2012). Depth is what makes the squat hard for the quads.

### Bench press

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 34 adults (12 powerlifters, 22 recreational lifters), powerlifters used a different bar path, had lower normalized peak elbow moments and a shorter range of motion. Powerlifters and men were more "shoulder dominant" than recreational lifters and women, who relied relatively more on the elbow (Mausehund & Krosshaug, 2023).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 14 bench-trained men, wide and medium grips produced greater horizontal shoulder moments than a narrow grip during the sticking region, and 1RM was about 104 kg with the narrow grip vs. about 109–110 kg with medium and wide (Larsen et al., 2021). Grip width is covered in [Chapter 3](/lets-git-it/technique-injury/03-bench-press-technique/).

### Deadlift

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 57 national-championship powerlifters (13 women, 44 men), estimated average compressive loads on the L4/L5 spinal segment reached about 17,200 N, and the highest average L4/L5 and hip moments were about 990 and 1,050 N·m. The sumo style had about 10% lower L4/L5 moments than conventional (Cholewicki et al., 1991).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 24 national-championship powerlifters (12 sumo, 12 conventional), conventional deadlifters had larger vertical bar distance and mechanical work than sumo deadlifters, with differences in knee and ankle moments; hip moments did not differ significantly (Escamilla et al., 2000). Stance choice is covered in [Chapter 4](/lets-git-it/technique-injury/04-deadlift-technique/).

| Lift | Joints with the largest demand | What shifts the balance |
|---|---|---|
| Squat | Hip and knee extensors; spinal extensors hold the torso | Bar position, stance width, knee travel, depth, footwear |
| Bench press | Shoulder (horizontal adduction) and elbow (extension) | Grip width, elbow position, touch point, arch |
| Deadlift | Hip and spinal extensors; knee extensors off the floor | Stance (sumo/conventional), start hip height, bar distance from shins |

:::tip[In plain words]
In the squat, deadlift and bench press, the bar puts its biggest load on just a few joints: hips and knees, hips and back, shoulders and elbows. Changing where the bar sits, how wide your feet or hands are, or how deep you go changes how the load is split between them.
:::

## Body proportions change the lift

Segment lengths set the moment arms a lifter has to work with. Two lifters with the same muscles can face different demands in the same lift.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 59 male classic powerlifters, those whose deadlift made up a larger share of their total tended to have longer lower legs and a greater reach relative to height. Reach relative to height positively predicted relative deadlift strength but negatively predicted relative bench strength. A bigger torso was the main predictor of absolute strength in all three lifts (Ferland et al., 2020).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 36 men, body mass correlated strongly with bench press 1RM, and total arm length was moderately associated with 1RM (Caruso et al., 2012).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 21 college-aged men and women, fat-free mass relative to height was the only statistically significant predictor of squat 1RM in the combined model, although biomechanical and anthropometric variables showed strong individual correlations (Vigotsky et al., 2019).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 18 resistance-trained adults, the combined maximal isometric moments of the shoulder and elbow had the largest effect on bench press 1RM, followed by lean body mass (Van Every et al., 2022).

**Bottom line:** proportions shape technique and which lift is "your" lift, but muscle mass and joint strength explain more of strength than limb lengths. Proportions are a reason to adjust technique, not an excuse.

| Proportion | Squat | Bench press | Deadlift |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long thighs, short torso | More forward lean needed to keep the bar over midfoot; larger hip moment | Little effect | Hips start higher and farther back; often suits sumo |
| Short thighs, long torso | Can stay upright; more knee moment | Little effect | Hips low; often comfortable conventional |
| Long arms | Wider grip may be needed for comfort | Longer bar travel; wider grip and arch shorten it | Shorter pull; hips start higher, an advantage |
| Short arms | Little effect | Short bar travel, an advantage | Longer pull; hips start lower |
| Large chest and torso | Stable bracing | Shorter bar travel | Bar must clear the thighs and belly |

<Evidence level="practice" /> The table follows from the moment-arm geometry above and from common coaching practice. Apart from the reach findings (Ferland et al., 2020), it has not been tested directly; use it to choose what to try, then judge by the lifter's results.

:::tip[In plain words]
Your arm, leg and body lengths make some lifts naturally easier or harder for you. Long arms help the deadlift and hurt the bench; long thighs make you lean forward more in the squat. But how much muscle you have matters more. Use your build to choose your technique, not to explain away a weak lift.
:::

## Mobility and range of motion

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 24 men (12 national-level powerlifters, 12 recreationally trained), powerlifters had less shoulder extension and horizontal abduction and less hip flexion, extension and adduction. In powerlifters, less shoulder extension and hip flexion went with a higher competition Wilks score. The authors suggested lifters keep enough shoulder extension and hip flexion to reach competition depth (Spence et al., 2023).

<Evidence level="practice" /> The practical takeaway is narrow: a lifter needs enough mobility to reach the competition positions safely and legally, not more. Mobility work is covered in Part 5, Chapter 8 (Mobility and movement preparation).

:::tip[In plain words]
You need enough flexibility to get into the competition positions: deep enough in the squat, and the bar down to your chest in the bench. Beyond that, extra flexibility doesn't seem to make you stronger.
:::

## Sample programs

These are practical analysis tools and a technique-trial block. The default lifter is intermediate, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. Loads use the RPE chart from [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/).

### Program 1: The side-view moment-arm audit

A 20-minute session you can run with a phone. Use it before changing anyone's technique.

| Step | What to do | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Set up | Phone at hip height, exactly side-on, 3–4 m away; lifter's joint centres visible (tight clothing, tape on hip, knee, ankle, shoulder) | Camera level and square, or the distances are wrong |
| 2. Film | Each lift at about RPE 7–8 for 2–3 reps, plus one heavier single if planned that day | Real loads; empty-bar technique differs |
| 3. Freeze frames | Pause at: start, bottom (or chest), sticking region, lockout | Same four frames every time |
| 4. Draw lines | Vertical line from the bar's centre; mark horizontal distance to hip, knee (squat, deadlift), shoulder and elbow (bench) | Which joint has the longest moment arm at the sticking region? |
| 5. Compare | Set the frames beside a previous video or the other bar position or stance | Did the moment arm move to a joint that is stronger or weaker for this lifter? |
| 6. Record | Note the numbers in the training log | Re-film every 4–6 weeks or after any technique change |

Free video apps can draw lines and estimate distances; the absolute numbers are rough, so compare like with like.

### Program 2: Technique starting points for three body types

<Evidence level="practice" /> These are starting points to test, built from the geometry above, not prescriptions.

| | Lifter A: long thighs, short torso, long arms | Lifter B: short thighs, long torso, short arms | Lifter C: average proportions, large chest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Squat bar position | Try low bar first; compare high bar if hips or back limit | High bar or low bar both reasonable; try high bar if the lifter is upright naturally | Either; choose by comfort and strength |
| Squat stance | Wider, toes turned out, so the knees travel out more than forward | Hip-width to shoulder-width | Shoulder-width or slightly wider |
| Squat footwear | Raised-heel shoe worth testing to stay more upright | Flat or raised heel | Either |
| Bench grip | Wide (near the 81 cm limit) to shorten bar travel | Medium | Medium to wide |
| Bench arch | Work on a strong, legal arch to shorten travel | Moderate arch | Moderate arch; touch point lower on the chest |
| Deadlift stance | Try both; long arms favour conventional, long thighs may favour sumo | Conventional often feels natural; test sumo | Sumo may help the bar clear the belly |
| Likely sticking points | Squat out of the hole (forward lean); bench off the chest | Squat mid-range; deadlift off the floor | Bench lockout |

### Program 3: A 4-week technique trial (squat stance)

Use this when the audit suggests a different squat stance (or bar position) might suit a lifter. Both options are trained in the same block, at the same loads, so the comparison is fair. Bench and deadlift continue as normal; deadlift sits on Thursday so heavy squat and heavy deadlift are never on consecutive days.

| Week | Monday (squat) | Saturday (squat) | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Current stance 3 × 3; new stance 3 × 3 | New stance 4 × 4 | 3s: RPE 7 (~84%, 150 kg); 4s: RPE 7 (~81%, 145 kg) |
| 2 | Current stance 3 × 3; new stance 3 × 3 | New stance 4 × 4 | Same loads; RPE should fall for the new stance as it is learned |
| 3 | Current stance 2 × 2; new stance 2 × 2 | New stance 3 × 3 | 2s: RPE 8 (~89%, 160 kg); 3s: RPE 7 (150 kg) |
| 4 | Current stance 1 × 1; new stance 1 × 1 | Rest from heavy squats (volume deload: 2 × 3 at 130 kg) | Singles: RPE 8 (~92%, 165 kg) |

**How to judge it at week 4:**

- Compare RPE, bar speed (if measured) and video at the same load for both stances.
- New stance wins only if it is **equal or better on RPE and clearly better on position** (depth consistency, bar over midfoot, less forward collapse at the sticking region).
- If unclear, keep the current stance. <Evidence level="practice" /> A new technique often needs several weeks to beat a well-practised one, so a tie early on can still be worth a longer trial in the off-season.

## Common mistakes

- **Judging moment arms from the front or at an angle.** Moment arms for the bar are horizontal distances seen exactly side-on.
- **Chasing one joint's numbers.** Reducing knee stress by keeping the shins vertical simply moves demand to the hips and back.
- **Treating the empty bar as the test.** Positions change under heavy load; analyse working sets.
- **Blaming proportions for weak lifts.** Muscle mass and joint strength explain more of strength than limb lengths.
- **Changing technique close to a meet.** Trial changes in the off-season with a structured block.
- **Reading single lab studies as rules.** Most biomechanics studies are small (often 6–20 people) and use submaximal loads.

## Key takeaways

- Moment = force × moment arm; for the bar, the moment arm is the horizontal distance to the joint.
- A few centimetres of bar drift can raise a joint's moment by 20% or more; the absolute increase grows with the load.
- Technique changes move demand between joints; they don't remove it.
- Heavy loads move slowly (force–velocity), and muscles have a best length (length–tension); together with leverage, these create sticking regions.
- Body proportions shape technique and lift strengths, but muscle mass and joint strength matter more.
- Test technique changes with video and a structured trial, not by feel alone.
