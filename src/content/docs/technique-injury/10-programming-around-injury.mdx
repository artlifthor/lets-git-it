## In one minute

Most lifters with pain can keep training if the coach changes **how** they train: the range of motion, tempo, exercise, load, volume or frequency. A simple **pain-monitoring model** (how much it hurts during training, the next morning, and from week to week) tells you whether the current dose is tolerable. Exercise is a first-line treatment for tendon pain and chronic low back pain, and is recommended for shoulder pain (though that evidence is weaker), so loading is usually part of the solution, not the enemy. Return to heavy lifting in **stages**, moving on when the lifter meets clear criteria rather than after a fixed number of days. Diagnosis and medical limits always come from a qualified health professional; the coach's job is to turn those limits into a sensible training plan.

## 1. Train what you can, change what hurts

<Evidence level="practice" /> In a survey of 104 Swedish sub-elite classic powerlifters, 70% reported a current injury affecting training, but only 16% of those had stopped training completely; most modified their training instead (Strömbäck et al., 2018). That matches what most coaches see.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Keeping a lifter training has clear benefits:

- They keep strength and skill in the lifts and body parts that don't hurt.
- They stay in the habit and stay connected to the team.
- Gradual loading of the sore area usually helps it recover (see section 4).
- Complete rest followed by a sudden return is a common way to re-aggravate a problem.

:::tip[In plain words]
An injury rarely means "stop lifting". It usually means "lift differently for a while". Keep training everything that doesn't hurt, and find a version of the painful lift that you can do.
:::

## 2. The pain-monitoring model

<Evidence level="emerging" /> In a randomised trial of 38 patients with Achilles tendon pain, one group kept running and jumping during rehabilitation, guided by a pain-monitoring model; the other group stopped those activities for 6 weeks. Both groups improved a lot over 12 months, with **no difference** between them. Continuing to load with pain monitoring caused no harm (Silbernagel et al., 2007). This was one tendon, in one small trial, not in lifters.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Coaches and clinicians commonly adapt this idea into three checks. The exact numbers below are a widely used **coaching rule of thumb**, not research-proven cut-offs. Rate pain from 0 (none) to 10 (worst imaginable).

| Check | Green: keep going | Amber: hold or reduce | Red: back off and review |
|---|---|---|---|
| **During the session** | 0–2/10, stable or easing as you warm up | 3–4/10, stable through the session | 5/10 or more, or rising set by set |
| **Next morning** | Same as usual or better | A little worse but settles within the day | Clearly worse, or still worse by the next session |
| **Week to week** | Same or better | No change for 2 weeks | Getting worse over the week |

**How to use it:**

- **All green:** progress the next session (add 2.5–5 kg, a set, or more range).
- **Any amber:** repeat the same dose next time; don't progress.
- **Any red:** go back to the last dose that was green. Two red responses in a row, or pain trending worse over a week: book a physiotherapist or doctor.

<Evidence level="practice" /> Ask about pain before the warm-up, after the first working set, and the next morning (a quick message works). Write the numbers in the training log next to the sets.

:::tip[In plain words]
Pain during training is allowed if it stays low, doesn't climb during the session, and isn't worse the next morning. If it's a little worse, hold steady. If it's clearly worse, go back a step. Write the numbers down so you can see the trend.
:::

## 3. Load-modification options

Change **one or two things at a time**, starting with what most likely triggered the pain. Each option below keeps training going while lowering the stress on the irritated area.

| Lever | Examples | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Range of motion** | Box squat to just above the painful depth; pin squat; board press or floor press; block or rack pulls | Pain at one end of the range (bottom of the squat or bench) |
| **Tempo and pauses** | 3-second lowering; pause just above the painful point; remove long pauses | Pain from bouncing or speed; lifters who lose position under load |
| **Exercise variation** | Safety-squat bar or high-bar instead of low-bar (shoulder or elbow pain); trap-bar deadlift; neutral-grip dumbbell press | Pain tied to one bar position, grip or stance |
| **Grip or stance** | Narrower bench grip; change squat stance width or toe angle; switch deadlift stance for a block | Pain tied to a recent change in setup |
| **Intensity (load)** | Cap sets at RPE 6–7 or 60–75% of 1RM | Pain that appears only with heavy loads |
| **Volume** | Cut sets by 30–50% on the painful lift | Pain that builds across a session or week |
| **Frequency** | Train the painful lift 1–2 times a week instead of 3 | Pain that never quite settles between sessions |
| **Implement** | Machines, cables, single-arm or single-leg work | Areas that need very controlled loading |
| **Train the other side** | Single-arm or single-leg work on the unaffected side | When one limb can't be trained at all |

**Training the uninjured side may help the other side keep some strength.** <Evidence level="moderate" /> A meta-analysis of 31 trials (785 people) found that training one limb increased strength in the **untrained** opposite limb by about 12% on average (about 9% for arms and 16% for legs), although the included studies had a high risk of bias (Manca et al., 2017). These trials were in healthy people, not injured limbs, so treat the benefit for an injured side as likely but untested. This "cross-education" makes single-limb training on the good side worthwhile when the other side must rest.

:::tip[In plain words]
You have lots of levers: less depth, slower reps, a different bar or grip, lighter weights, fewer sets, or fewer sessions. Change the one most likely to have caused the pain first. Even training only your good arm or leg may help the injured one hold on to some strength.
:::

## 4. What the evidence says about exercise for common problems

### Tendon pain (tendinopathy)

Tendon pain is common in the elbow, knee (below the kneecap), shoulder and hip. Loading programmes are the core of rehabilitation.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review (32 studies, of which 10 compared loading programmes directly) found only **limited (Achilles) and conflicting (patellar) evidence** that eccentric-only loading is better than other loading programmes. Heavy slow resistance (HSR) training had the stronger evidence for patellar tendon pain. Improved muscle performance (strength, endurance) was the factor most consistently linked with better outcomes. The authors noted that good-quality studies were scarce (Malliaras et al., 2013).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 58 patients with long-standing Achilles tendon pain, 12 weeks of **heavy slow resistance** and of **eccentric training** gave equally good, lasting improvements at 52 weeks. Patients attended more HSR sessions (92% vs 78%) (Beyer et al., 2015).

**Isometric holds for pain relief: mixed evidence.**

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 6 volleyball players with patellar tendon pain, a session of heavy isometric holds reduced pain right away and for at least 45 minutes, more than isotonic (moving) exercise did (Rio et al., 2015).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> But a later systematic review of 10 randomised trials found that isometric exercise was **not better** than isotonic exercise for chronic tendon pain, either immediately or over 12 weeks, and the response varied a lot between people. The evidence was limited (Clifford et al., 2020).

<Evidence level="practice" /> For lifters: use isometric holds (e.g., wall sits or Spanish squats for the knee, a held bench press just off the chest) if they help that lifter, but don't rely on them. Progress towards **slow, heavy, full-range** work and then back to the competition lift.

:::tip[In plain words]
Sore tendons usually get better with gradual, fairly heavy, slow strength work, not with rest. Holding a position still can ease pain for some people, but it's not a magic fix. The aim is to build back up to normal lifting.
:::

### Low back pain

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A Cochrane review of 249 trials found **moderate-certainty** evidence that exercise reduces pain in chronic low back pain compared with no treatment, usual care or placebo: about 15 points on a 0–100 scale at the earliest follow-up, a clinically important difference. The effect on function was smaller and did not reach the authors' threshold for clinical importance (Hayden et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="moderate" /> For acute low back pain, advice to **stay active** gave small benefits for pain and function compared with advice to rest in bed (Dahm et al., 2010).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Low back pain guidelines recommend reassurance, staying active, avoiding bed rest, and exercise therapy for chronic pain, with imaging only when serious disease is suspected (Oliveira et al., 2018).

<Evidence level="practice" /> No single type of exercise is clearly best for low back pain, and lifting can be part of the treatment. For a powerlifter, that often means keeping squats and hinges in the programme at a tolerable range and load, rather than replacing them with only "core exercises".

### Shoulder pain

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> A systematic review and meta-analysis of conservative treatments for shoulder impingement (pain at the front or side of the shoulder with arm movement) found exercise was better than non-exercise controls for pain, and **specific** shoulder exercises were better than general exercises. The authors rated the evidence as **very low quality** (Steuri et al., 2017).

<Evidence level="practice" /> In lifters, this usually means a period of reduced or modified bench pressing alongside progressive rotator cuff and upper-back strengthening, guided by a physiotherapist when pain lingers.

:::tip[In plain words]
For back pain, sensible exercise is one of the best-supported treatments. For shoulder pain it is recommended too, though the research is weaker. Keep moving, keep lifting what you tolerate, and build strength in the sore area gradually.
:::

## 5. Returning to heavy training: stages, not dates

<Evidence level="practice" /> An international consensus on return to sport recommends treating return as a **continuum** that runs alongside rehabilitation, not a single decision at the end. It notes that research evidence to support return decisions is scarce (Ardern et al., 2016). In practice, move between stages when the lifter meets **criteria**, not after a set number of days.

| Stage | Goal | Typical training | Move on when… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Settle** | Calm the area, keep training everything else | Pain-free variations (reduced range, different bar), other lifts as normal, walking, low-load exercises for the sore area | Daily life is comfortable; the chosen variation is green on the pain checks for 1–2 weeks |
| **2. Rebuild** | Restore range and capacity | Variations moving towards full range; 3–5 sets of 5–8 at RPE 6–7; slow tempos | Full-range variation at moderate load is green for 2 sessions in a row |
| **3. Reload the competition lift** | Re-expose to the exact lift | Competition lift at 60–80% of 1RM, sets of 3–5, volume gradually back to normal | Competition lift at RPE 7 is green; technique matches pre-injury videos |
| **4. Go heavy** | Return to normal heavy training | Top sets at RPE 8, then 8.5–9; normal programme | Heavy work is green for 2 weeks; lifter feels confident |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Two extra rules help:

- **Confidence counts.** A lifter who is pain-free but afraid of the lift is not ready for heavy singles. Give them more repeated, successful exposures at moderate loads first.
- **Build volume before intensity.** Return to normal set and rep numbers at moderate loads before chasing heavy singles.

:::tip[In plain words]
Come back step by step: first find something you can do without pain, then rebuild, then do the real lift lighter, then go heavy. Move up a step when your body says yes, not because a certain number of days have passed.
:::

## 6. Working with health professionals

<Evidence level="practice" /> Good communication stops the lifter getting mixed messages. Ask the lifter's permission before contacting their clinician.

**What the coach should share:**

- The training history before the pain started: recent changes in volume, load, exercises, grip or stance.
- Which lifts and positions hurt, and what pain scores the lifter reported.
- The upcoming schedule (meets, key sessions).
- A video of the lift, if useful.

**What to ask the clinician:**

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Are there any movements or loads to avoid completely, and for how long? | Sets the hard limits |
| What pain level during or after training is acceptable for this lifter? | Lets you use the pain checks with their numbers |
| Which signs should make us stop and contact you? | Agreed warning signs |
| Can we keep training [specific lifts or variations]? | Most lifters can keep much of their training |
| When should we review progress? | Avoids drifting for months |

**What the coach should not do:** name a diagnosis, contradict the clinician's limits, or promise the lifter a return date. If you disagree with advice (for example, "never squat again" for non-specific back pain), the lifter can ask for a second opinion, ideally from a clinician experienced with strength athletes.

:::tip[In plain words]
The physio or doctor decides what's wrong and sets the limits; the coach turns those limits into a training plan. Share the training history, ask clear questions, and agree on what pain level is OK and what should make you stop.
:::

## Sample programs

Example lifter: intermediate, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. Loads come from the RTS chart in [Part 1, Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg. Each plan assumes a health professional has ruled out anything serious, or there are no red flags. Apply the pain checks (section 2) every session: green, progress; amber, repeat; red, go back a week.

### Program 1: Training around a cranky shoulder (4 weeks)

**Situation:** front-of-shoulder pain at the bottom of the bench press after moving to a wider grip. Pain 4/10 on paused bench, 1/10 on a 2-board press. Squat and deadlift are pain-free, but holding a low-bar squat bar is uncomfortable.

**Why it's built this way:** go back to the previous grip, shorten the range first, then extend it as the checks stay green. Use a safety-squat bar (or high-bar) to take load off the shoulder while squatting. Bench drops from 3 to 2 days, and shoulder and upper-back work is added every session. Heavy squat (Monday) and heavy deadlift (Wednesday) are 48 hours apart.

| Week | Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mon | Safety-squat bar squat | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 |
| 1 | Mon | 2-board press (old, narrower grip) | 4 × 6 | RPE 6–7, pain ≤2 |
| 1 | Wed | Deadlift | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 (~170 kg) |
| 1 | Wed | Cable external rotation; band pull-apart | 3 × 15 each | Easy, pain ≤2 |
| 1 | Fri | Safety-squat bar squat | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 1 | Fri | Bench press, 1-s pause (old grip) | 3 × 5 | 60% (72.5 kg), test only |
| 1 | Fri | Chest-supported row | 4 × 10 | RPE 8 |
| 2 | Mon | Safety-squat bar squat | 4 × 4 | RPE 7.5 |
| 2 | Mon | Bench press, 1-s pause | 4 × 5 | 65% (77.5 kg) |
| 2 | Wed | Deadlift | 3 × 3 | RPE 7.5 (~177.5 kg) |
| 2 | Wed | External rotation; pull-apart; push-ups | 3 × 12–15 each | Pain ≤2 |
| 2 | Fri | Safety-squat bar squat | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 2 | Fri | 2-board press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Mon | Back squat (high-bar if comfortable; high-bar is usually a little weaker, so let RPE set the load) | 4 × 4 | RPE 7.5 (~147.5 kg) |
| 3 | Mon | Bench press, 1-s pause | 4 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) |
| 3 | Wed | Deadlift | 3 × 3 | RPE 7.5 (~177.5 kg) |
| 3 | Fri | Back squat | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) |
| 3 | Fri | Bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) |
| 4 | Mon | Back squat | 4 × 4 | RPE 7.5 (~147.5 kg) |
| 4 | Mon | Bench press, competition pause | 1 × 3, then 3 × 4 | RPE 8 (~102.5 kg), then RPE 7.5 (~100 kg) |
| 4 | Wed | Deadlift | 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~180 kg) |
| 4 | Fri | Back squat | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) |
| 4 | Fri | Bench press | 3 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) |

Keep the rotator cuff, pull-apart and row work in every week. If paused bench press is still amber in week 2, stay on 2-board presses for another week. If shoulder pain is no better after 2 weeks, or weakness or night pain appears, see a physiotherapist or doctor.

### Program 2: Training around low back pain (4 weeks)

**Situation:** non-specific low back pain, checked by a physiotherapist with no red flags. Pain 4/10 on floor deadlifts and the bottom of heavy squats, 1–2/10 on block pulls and box squats to parallel. Bench is fine.

**Why it's built this way:** keep squatting and hinging (exercise helps back pain, section 4), but start in a reduced range at moderate loads and rebuild to the floor and full depth. Walking every day keeps the lifter active. The heaviest squat (Monday) and deadlift (Thursday) are 72 hours apart.

| Week | Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mon | Box squat (parallel box) | 3 × 5 | 60% (107.5 kg) |
| 1 | Mon | Back extension (bodyweight) | 3 × 10 | Pain ≤2 |
| 1 | Tue | Bench press; row | Normal programme | — |
| 1 | Thu | Block pull (bar just below knee) | 3 × 5 | RPE 6, pain ≤2 |
| 1 | Thu | Goblet squat | 3 × 8 | Easy |
| 1 | Sat | Bench press; split squat | Normal; 3 × 8 | Split squat easy |
| 1 | Daily | Walk | 20–30 min | Easy pace |
| 2 | Mon | Box squat (lower box, just below parallel) | 4 × 5 | 65% (117.5 kg) |
| 2 | Mon | Back extension | 3 × 12 | Add a light plate if green |
| 2 | Thu | Block pull (bar at mid-shin) | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| 2 | Thu | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | 40% of deadlift (85 kg) |
| 3 | Mon | Back squat | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~145 kg) |
| 3 | Thu | Deadlift from the floor | 3 × 4 | 70% (147.5 kg) |
| 3 | Thu | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | 45% (95 kg) |
| 3 | Sat | Back squat | 3 × 5 | 65% (117.5 kg) |
| 4 | Mon | Back squat | 1 × 3, then 3 × 4 | RPE 8 (~155 kg), then RPE 7 (~145 kg) |
| 4 | Thu | Deadlift | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 (~175 kg) |
| 4 | Sat | Back squat | 3 × 5 | 65% (117.5 kg) |

Bench press and upper-body work continue unchanged on Tuesday and Saturday each week. Daily walking continues throughout. If floor deadlifts are amber in week 3, return to mid-shin block pulls for one more week before trying again.

### Program 3: A 6-week return to heavy deadlifts

**Situation:** the lifter has had a low back episode (or a hamstring strain) and has been cleared by a physiotherapist to return to deadlifting. Daily life is pain-free, and block pulls at moderate loads are green. Goal: back to normal heavy deadlift training in 6 weeks.

**Why it's built this way:** loads stay moderate in weeks 1–3 while the lifter rebuilds tolerance to the floor pull; intensity rises in weeks 4–6. Each week has clear criteria to move on. The lifter deadlifts once a week, plus one lighter hinge session. Squats continue on the lifter's normal plan, with the heavy squat at least 48–72 hours from the deadlift day.

| Week | Main deadlift day (Thu) | Second hinge day (Mon) | Move on if… |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Block pull (mid-shin) 4 × 5 @ 60% (125 kg) | Romanian deadlift 3 × 8 @ 40% (85 kg) | All pain checks green; positions match pre-injury video |
| 2 | Deadlift from the floor 4 × 5 @ 65% (137.5 kg) | RDL 3 × 8 @ 45% (95 kg) | Green; bar speed steady across sets |
| 3 | Deadlift 5 × 3 @ 75% (157.5 kg) | RDL 3 × 6 @ 50% (105 kg) | Green; lifter reports confidence |
| 4 | Deadlift 4 × 3 @ RPE 7 (~175 kg) | Paused deadlift (pause below knee) 3 × 3 @ 60% (125 kg) | Green; RPE matches target |
| 5 | Deadlift 1 × 2 @ RPE 8 (~187.5 kg), then 3 × 3 @ RPE 7 (~175 kg) | RDL 3 × 6 @ 50% (105 kg) | Green; top set technique solid |
| 6 | Deadlift 1 × 1 @ RPE 8 (~192.5 kg), then 3 × 2 @ RPE 7.5 (~185 kg) | Light pulls 3 × 3 @ 70% (147.5 kg) | Green: return to the normal programme, with RPE 9 work allowed from next week |

**If a week is amber:** repeat it. **If a week is red:** go back one week, and contact the physiotherapist if it happens twice. A 6-week plan can easily take 8 weeks; that's fine.

## Common mistakes

- **Diagnosing and treating.** Coaches modify training; health professionals diagnose and treat.
- **All-or-nothing thinking.** Either "push through it" or "stop lifting completely". The useful answer is almost always in between.
- **Changing everything at once.** Change one or two levers so you know what helped.
- **Testing the painful lift every session.** Repeatedly checking whether it still hurts keeps the area irritated and the lifter anxious. Test on purpose, at planned points.
- **Returning by the calendar.** Move to the next stage when the criteria are met, not on a fixed date.
- **Rushing intensity.** Going straight back to heavy singles before volume at moderate loads is tolerated.
- **Forgetting the rest of the body.** Training around one injury but letting other lifts and general fitness slide.
- **No record.** Not logging pain scores means you can't see the trend.

## Key takeaways

- Most lifters with pain can keep training with sensible changes to range, tempo, variation, load, volume or frequency.
- Use pain checks during the session, the next morning and week to week: green, progress; amber, hold; red, back off and review. The exact thresholds are coaching practice.
- Continuing to load with pain monitoring did no harm in a tendon trial, and exercise is a first-line treatment for tendon pain and chronic low back pain, and is recommended for shoulder pain, though that evidence is very low quality.
- Heavy slow resistance works at least as well as eccentric-only training for tendon pain; isometric holds help some lifters but aren't superior overall.
- Return to heavy lifting in stages and progress on criteria: settle, rebuild, reload the competition lift, then go heavy.
- The clinician sets the limits and diagnosis; the coach builds the plan and communicates.
