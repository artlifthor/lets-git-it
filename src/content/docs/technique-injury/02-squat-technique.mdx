## In one minute

A good competition squat keeps the bar balanced over the middle of the foot, reaches legal depth every time, and holds a braced trunk from the walkout to the "Rack" command. Bar position (high or low) and stance width change how the work is shared between hips and knees, but research shows smaller differences than gym arguments suggest, so the right choice is the one that lets the lifter hit depth consistently and lift the most. Letting the knees travel forward is normal and moves load off the hips and back. Depth, bracing and a repeatable setup matter more than any single cue. Variations such as pause, tempo, pin, front and safety-bar squats each change one thing, and should be chosen for what they fix.

## The competition standard

Summarised from the **IPF Technical Rulebook (2026 edition, effective 1 March 2026)**. These are rules, not evidence claims; the full text is the final word.

| Phase | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Bar position | Horizontal across the shoulders; the rulebook sets a lowest legal bar position (the 2026 change notes describe the aim as keeping the bar above the shoulder blades); hands, thumbs and fingers in contact with the bar |
| Start | After walking out, stand **motionless, erect, knees locked**; wait for the **"Squat"** command (with a downward arm signal). Not set within 5 s → "Replace" |
| Depth | Bend the knees and lower until the **top surface of the legs at the hip joint is lower than the top of the knees** |
| Descent | **One descent only.** The attempt starts when the knees unlock |
| Ascent | No double bounce, no downward movement on the way up |
| Finish | Upright, knees locked, motionless; wait for **"Rack"** (with a backward arm signal) |
| Feet | No stepping; rocking between heel and ball of the foot is allowed |
| Arms | Elbows or upper arms may not support on the legs (slight contact without support is allowed) |

<Evidence level="practice" /> The 2026 bar-position rule matters for very low low-bar squatters: check the rulebook diagrams and film from the side if the bar sits close to the lowest legal position. Part 4, Chapter 7 covers rules and commands in detail.

:::tip[In plain words]
In a meet, you walk the bar out, stand still with your knees locked, and wait for "Squat". Go down once until your hip crease is below the top of your knee, stand all the way up, and wait for "Rack". Your feet can't move, and the bar can't sit lower than the rulebook allows.
:::

## Setup and walkout

<Evidence level="practice" /> Horschig's *The Squat Bible* builds the squat from a stable foot (weight spread over heel, big-toe base and little-toe base, the "tripod"), knees tracking in line with the toes, a braced neutral trunk, and enough ankle and hip mobility to reach depth. The steps below follow that logic and common powerlifting practice.

| Step | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Rack height | Bar at about mid-chest to armpit height | Unrack with a small knee extension, no tiptoe or half squat |
| 2. Grip and bar | Hands as close as is comfortable for the shoulders, elbows and wrists; widen it if the elbows ache; pull the shoulder blades together; bar on the upper back (high or low position) | A tight upper back forms a stable shelf |
| 3. Brace | Big breath into the belly and sides, then brace before unracking | Trunk stiffness from the first moment the bar is loaded |
| 4. Unrack | Feet under the bar, stand straight up | Avoids a twisted or off-balance start |
| 5. Walkout | **Two or three steps, the same every time**: one back, one to set width, a small third to adjust if needed | Fewer steps save energy and keep the stance the same every time |
| 6. Set | Toes at the chosen angle, weight on the whole foot, knees locked, eyes steady | Required for the "Squat" command; repeatable start |

:::tip[In plain words]
Set up the same way every rep: bar at the right height, tight upper back, big breath, stand it up, two or three steps back, the same every time. Spread your weight over your whole foot. A repeatable setup makes every squat look the same, which is what you want on the platform.
:::

## Bar position: high bar vs. low bar

- **High bar:** the bar sits on top of the upper trapezius, just below the neck.
- **Low bar:** the bar sits lower, on the rear deltoids just above the shoulder blades; very low positions must be checked against the 2026 rule.

The lower bar position puts the bar closer to the hips along the torso, so the lifter leans further forward to keep the bar over midfoot.

- <Evidence level="practice" /> A narrative review found that the high-bar squat is characterised by more knee flexion, less hip flexion, a more upright torso and a deeper squat, while the low-bar squat has more hip flexion and forward lean. Vertical ground forces differed little. Low bar tended to show more erector, adductor and glute activity, and high bar more quadriceps activity (Glassbrook et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In international-level powerlifters (low bar, n = 6) and national-level weightlifters (high bar, n = 6) squatting up to 100% of 1RM, differences in joint angles and ground force were small to moderate and not statistically significant. The authors suggested low bar may suit lifting the greatest load (Glassbrook et al., 2019).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 recreationally trained men doing 3RM squats with the same stance width, high-bar and low-bar loads were almost the same (about 129 vs. 130 kg), and there were **no significant differences** in hip, knee or ankle moments or estimated muscle forces (Larsen et al., 2024).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 14 recreationally trained men and women doing 3RM squats, the most load was lifted low-bar, then high-bar, then with a safety bar (Kristiansen et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 experienced powerlifters, at 60% and 65% of 1RM, lowering-phase muscle activity was higher in the low-bar squat for all muscles measured (Murawa et al., 2020). In 10 powerlifters squatting 5RMs with matched depth and stance, bar and joint movement around the sticking region did not differ, though muscle activity did: high bar showed more rectus femoris, vastus medialis and lower-erector activity (van den Tillaar et al., 2020).

**What this means:** low bar is usually a little stronger for powerlifters and shifts slightly more work to the hips; high bar is more upright and slightly more quad-dominant. When stance is held constant, the differences are small. Muscle-activity findings are mixed between studies. Choose the position that lets the lifter reach depth consistently, feels stable, and is comfortable for the shoulders and elbows; use the other position as a variation.

| Choose low bar if… | Choose high bar if… |
|---|---|
| The lifter is strong through the hips and back | The lifter is naturally upright and quad-strong |
| Long thighs make an upright torso hard | Low bar causes shoulder, elbow or wrist pain |
| Testing shows a clearly higher squat | The lifter cannot keep a low bar above the legal limit |

:::tip[In plain words]
Low bar lets most powerlifters lift a little more and uses the hips a bit more. High bar keeps you more upright and uses the thighs a bit more. The difference is smaller than people argue about. Pick the one where you hit depth every time and lift the most, and use the other as an extra exercise.
:::

## Stance width and foot angle

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 9 male lifters squatting 60–75% of 1RM, stance width (75%, 100% and 140% of shoulder width) did not change quadriceps activity, but it did change activity of the adductors and glutes (McCaw & Melrose, 1999).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 10 experienced men, turning the feet straight or out 30° made no difference to muscle activity or knee forces. A wide squat stance produced more tibiofemoral and patellofemoral compression and PCL tension than a narrow one (Escamilla et al., 2001).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 powerlifters, a wide-stance "powerlifting" squat kept the shins more vertical than a narrow squat, giving smaller spine and ankle moments and larger hip moments (Swinton et al., 2012).

<Evidence level="practice" /> Practical guide: start around shoulder width with the toes turned out enough that the knees can follow the toes without the feet rolling. Wider stances suit lifters with long thighs or a hip-dominant style; narrower stances suit upright, quad-dominant squatters. Hip anatomy varies, so a few lifters need much wider or narrower stances than average to reach depth comfortably.

:::tip[In plain words]
Feet about shoulder-width apart, toes turned out a bit, and knees pointing the same way as your toes is a good start. A wider stance uses your hips and inner thighs more; a narrower one uses your thighs more. Find the width where you get deep comfortably and feel strong.
:::

## Depth

The IPF standard is **hip crease below the top of the knee**. For most lifters that is a little below "thighs parallel".

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 10 strength-trained women, the effort demanded of the knee extensors rose with squat depth but not with load (Bryanton et al., 2012). Depth is where the quadriceps are trained hardest.
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 17 male students over 12 weeks, deep squat training improved both deep and shallow squat 1RM, while shallow training transferred poorly to the deep squat (Bloomquist et al., 2013). In 17 men over 10 weeks, full squats grew the adductors and glutes more than half squats (Kubo et al., 2019).
- **Is deep squatting safe for the knees?** <Evidence level="practice" /> The reviews disagree on details. One concluded that knee forces rise with knee flexion and recommended parallel over deep squats for athletes with healthy knees because injury risk to some knee structures *may* rise in a deep squat (Escamilla, 2001). A later review concluded that, with good technique and progressive loading, deep squats do not increase injury risk to passive tissues, and that partial squats with very heavy loads may be the bigger long-term concern (Hartmann et al., 2013). Neither review is based on direct injury data from powerlifters. Powerlifters must squat to legal depth anyway; the practical question is building tolerance gradually.

**Getting depth right in training:**

- <Evidence level="practice" /> Film every top set from the side at hip height for the first months; lifters judge their own depth poorly.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Aim for depth that is clearly legal (a few centimetres below), not just legal. Referees see from angles, and fatigue makes depth shallower at heavy loads.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> If depth is lost only at heavy loads, it is usually a strength or confidence problem out of the bottom (pause squats, pin squats). If it is lost at all loads, look at stance, footwear and mobility.

:::tip[In plain words]
Squat so your hip crease ends up clearly below your knee, not just barely. Film yourself from the side, because almost everyone thinks they go deeper than they do. Deep squats build your inner thighs and glutes at least as well, and they carry over better to a legal competition squat.
:::

## Knee travel: knees past the toes is normal

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 7 weight-trained men, blocking the knees from passing the toes lowered knee torque (about 150 → 117 N·m) but raised hip torque sharply (about 28 → 303 N·m) and increased forward trunk lean (Fry et al., 2003).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 30 people squatting with light loads, unrestricted squats had smaller changes in thoracic spine curvature and less segmental trunk motion than squats with the knees held behind the toes; the authors advised coaches not to be overly strict about forward knee travel (List et al., 2013).

<Evidence level="practice" /> Let the knees travel forward and out in line with the toes as far as the lifter's ankles and style allow. What matters is that the **bar stays over midfoot** and the **knees track over the feet** instead of collapsing inward under load.

:::tip[In plain words]
It's fine, and normal, for your knees to go past your toes. Forcing them back just makes your hips and lower back work harder. Keep the bar over the middle of your foot and keep your knees pointing where your toes point.
:::

## Bracing, breathing and the belt

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> A systematic review of 16 studies found that the squat produced the highest intra-abdominal pressures of the exercises studied (over 200 mmHg), higher than deadlift, leg press and bench press (Blazek et al., 2019). Bracing with a held breath is part of how heavy squats are done.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Common practice: breathe in before each rep (at the top), brace the trunk against the held breath, and hold it through the sticking region; exhale at or near the top. Very long sets may need a small breath between reps at the top.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> A belt gives the trunk something to brace against. Most powerlifters use one for heavy sets; whether and how to use it is covered in Part 5, Chapter 11 (Equipment).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Holding the breath raises blood pressure sharply. Lifters with high blood pressure or heart conditions should get medical advice before heavy bracing.

:::tip[In plain words]
Before each rep, take a big breath into your belly and tighten your whole middle as if bracing for a punch. Hold it through the hard part, then breathe out at the top. If you have high blood pressure or a heart condition, check with a doctor first.
:::

## Descent and turnaround

- <Evidence level="practice" /> Lower under control at a speed you can repeat. Too slow wastes energy at heavy loads; too fast risks losing position or depth control.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> A single rebound out of the bottom is legal; a **double bounce** or any **downward movement on the way up** is not.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Drive up with the chest and hips rising together. If the hips shoot up first, the bar ends up far in front of the hips (a longer hip moment arm) right at the sticking region; see [Chapter 1](/lets-git-it/technique-injury/01-biomechanics-basics/).

:::tip[In plain words]
Go down under control, turn around once at the bottom, and stand up with your chest and hips rising together. Bouncing twice or letting the bar dip on the way up gets you red lights.
:::

## Footwear

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 32 participants, weightlifting shoes (raised heel) reduced ankle bending and increased knee bending compared with athletic shoes, and gave a more upright trunk in unloaded squats; experienced squatters used this effect more than novices (Legg et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Raised-heel shoes suit high-bar and upright squatters, and lifters with limited ankle mobility. Flat, firm shoes suit many low-bar, wide-stance squatters. Avoid soft running shoes for heavy squats. IPF rules limit shoe sole height; check the equipment rules in Part 4, Chapter 7.

:::tip[In plain words]
Squat shoes with a raised heel help you stay upright and get deeper, especially if your ankles are stiff. Flat, hard shoes work well for wide, low-bar squatters. Soft running shoes are a poor choice for heavy squats.
:::

## Variations and what they train

Exercise selection and the specificity ladder are covered in [Part 1 · Chapter 8](/lets-git-it/foundations/08-exercise-selection/). The table below focuses on technique: what each variation changes and the fault it is used for.

| Variation | What changes | Technique job | Evidence and notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pause squat** (2–3 s at depth) | Removes the rebound | Position and strength out of the hole; depth awareness | <Evidence level="practice" /> |
| **Tempo squat** (3–5 s down) | Slower lowering | Bar path, balance, staying braced on the way down | <Evidence level="practice" /> |
| **Pin squat** (start from pins at depth) | Concentric-only start | Starting strength at the bottom; honest depth | <Evidence level="practice" /> |
| **Box squat** (box at or just below legal depth) | Sit back to a box, shins vertical | Hip-dominant pattern, consistent depth | <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 powerlifters, the box squat produced the smallest spine and ankle moments of three squat styles (Swinton et al., 2012) |
| **Front squat** | Bar in front of the neck | Upright torso, quads, upper back | <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 12 participants at maximal loads, the back squat showed more trunk lean and the front squat more vastus medialis activity while rising (Yavuz et al., 2015). In 15 trained people, the front squat recruited muscles as effectively as the back squat with lower knee compressive forces (Gullett et al., 2009) |
| **Safety-bar squat** | Cambered yoke bar, hands in front | Upper back and upright position; shoulder-friendly | <Evidence level="emerging" /> Lifted the least load of three bar types but gave larger knee moments than low bar and more glute activity than high bar (Kristiansen et al., 2021) |
| **Other bar position** (high or low) | More knee or more hip | Adds volume with a slightly different stress | See the bar position section |
| **Belt squat, hack squat, leg press** | No bar on the back | Leg muscle without spinal loading | <Evidence level="practice" /> |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Horschig's *The Squat Bible* also uses simpler squats, such as the goblet squat and box squat, to teach the pattern before loading the back squat. They are useful for novices and for lifters returning from time off.

:::tip[In plain words]
Each squat variation changes one thing to work on one problem: pauses for the bottom, slow lowering for control, pins for starting strength, front and safety-bar squats for staying upright. Pick the one that matches what goes wrong in your squat.
:::

## Cues that work

<Evidence level="practice" /> Cues are coaching practice; use one or two per set, not five. Faults and fixes are covered in Part 5, Chapter 5 (Common technical faults).

| Problem | Cue | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Weight shifts to toes | "Whole foot" or "tripod foot" | Keeps balance over midfoot |
| Knees cave in | "Knees out over the toes" | Keeps knees tracking with the feet |
| Chest drops out of the hole | "Chest up into the bar" or "drive the back into the bar" | Hips and chest rise together |
| Loses tightness at the bottom | "Big breath, brace before you move" | Trunk stiffness before the descent |
| Rushes the descent | "Sit down between your heels, controlled" | Repeatable depth and position |

:::tip[In plain words]
Use one short cue at a time, matched to the one thing going wrong. If a cue doesn't change the lift within a couple of sets, try a different one.
:::

## Sample programs

Loads use the RPE chart from [Part 1 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/). Variations and light technique work are given as RPE only, because their maxes differ from the competition squat.

### Program 1: Learning the squat (novice, 6 weeks)

A new lifter, three full-body sessions a week (Monday, Wednesday, Friday), who can currently squat about 60 kg for 5. The aim is a repeatable, legal squat before chasing load. Bench press and deadlift are trained alongside at RPE ≤7.

| Weeks | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Goblet squat (2 s pause at the bottom) | 3 × 8 | Light dumbbell, RPE 5–6 | Depth, knees over toes, upright trunk |
| 1 | Box squat to a box at legal depth | 3 × 5 | Empty bar | Find depth, sit back, two- or three-step walkout |
| 2 | Back squat, tempo (3 s down) | 4 × 5 | Start at 40 kg, RPE ≤6 | Bracing and bar over midfoot |
| 3–4 | Back squat | 3 × 5 | Start at 50 kg; add 2.5–5 kg per session while RPE ≤7 | Same setup every rep; film the top set |
| 3–4 | Pause squat (Fridays) | 2 × 3 | RPE 6 | Depth awareness |
| 5–6 | Back squat | 3 × 5 | Keep adding 2.5–5 kg per session while RPE ≤7 and depth holds | Linear progression ([Part 1 · Chapter 7](/lets-git-it/foundations/07-novice-linear-progression/)) |
| 5–6 | Goblet squat (warm-up) | 1 × 8 | Light | Movement prep |

**Why it is built this way:** simple patterns first (goblet, box) teach depth and balance with little load; tempo and pauses teach control; then linear progression adds load as long as the technique holds. Any session where depth is lost, the load repeats.

### Program 2: Switching from high bar to low bar (intermediate, 6 weeks)

Intermediate lifter (current high-bar squat 180 kg) who wants to test low bar. Squat on Monday and Friday, deadlift on Wednesday, so heavy squats and heavy deadlifts are never on consecutive days.

| Week | Monday | Friday | Load notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | High-bar squat 4 × 4; then low-bar squat 3 × 5 (practice) | Low-bar squat 4 × 5 | High bar RPE 7 (~81%, 145 kg). Low bar: start at 110 kg, add 5–10 kg per session while RPE ≤7 |
| 3–4 | Low-bar squat 4 × 4 | High-bar squat 3 × 5 | Low bar by RPE 7. High bar RPE 7 (~79%, 140 kg) |
| 5 | Low-bar squat 3 × 3 | High-bar squat 3 × 4 | Low bar by RPE 8. High bar RPE 7 (145 kg) |
| 6 | Low-bar squat: work up to 1 × 1 at RPE 8, then 2 × 3 at ~90% of that single | High-bar squat 1 × 1 at RPE 8 (~92%, 165 kg), then 2 × 3 at 150 kg | Compare the two singles |

**How to decide:** estimate each 1RM from the RPE 8 single (single at RPE 8 ≈ 92% of 1RM). Keep low bar only if it is equal or stronger **and** depth is consistent **and** the shoulders and elbows tolerate it. <Evidence level="emerging" /> Research suggests low bar is often slightly stronger, but not always (Kristiansen et al., 2021; Larsen et al., 2024). Check on video that the bar position is legal under the current rulebook. Switching close to a meet is not advised.

### Program 3: Position and depth block (intermediate, 4 weeks)

Intermediate lifter (squat 180 kg) who loses position out of the hole and has had depth calls at meets. Squat on Monday and Saturday, deadlift on Thursday. Bench press on Tuesday and Thursday is unchanged.

| Week | Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mon | Squat | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~81%, 145 kg) |
| 1 | Mon | Pause squat (3 s) | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 |
| 1 | Sat | Tempo squat (4 s down) | 3 × 4 | RPE 6–7 |
| 1 | Sat | Squat | 3 × 2 | RPE 7 (~86%, 155 kg) |
| 2 | Mon | Squat | 4 × 4 | RPE 7.5 (~82%, 147.5 kg) |
| 2 | Mon | Pause squat (3 s) | 3 × 3 | RPE 7.5 |
| 2 | Sat | Pin squat (pins at legal depth) | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 |
| 2 | Sat | Squat | 3 × 2 | RPE 7 (155 kg) |
| 3 | Mon | Squat | 4 × 3 | RPE 8 (~86%, 155 kg) |
| 3 | Mon | Pause squat (3 s) | 3 × 3 | RPE 8 |
| 3 | Sat | Tempo squat (4 s down) | 3 × 4 | RPE 7 |
| 3 | Sat | Squat | 3 × 2 | RPE 7 (155 kg) |
| 4 | Mon | Squat | 2 × 3 | 140 kg (volume deload, RPE ≤6–7) |
| 4 | Mon | Pause squat (3 s) | 1 × 3 | RPE 6 |
| 4 | Sat | Tempo squat (4 s down) | 1 × 4 | RPE 6 |
| 4 | Sat | Squat | 2 × 2 | 145 kg |

**Why it is built this way:** every competition-squat set is filmed and judged for depth, with a "commands" rep (wait for a training partner's "Squat" and "Rack") on the last set each day. The pause and pin squats force honest depth and strength at the bottom, and the tempo squats rehearse bar path. Week 4 cuts squat sets from 13 to 6 at the same or slightly lighter loads. <Evidence level="practice" /> Whether these variations fix a lifter's specific fault is tested by re-filming the competition squat at the end of the block, as described in [Part 3 · Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/advanced/05-weak-point-analysis/).

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing bar position by internet argument.** Test both; the evidence shows small differences, and individual results decide.
- **Training to "almost" depth.** Squats that are borderline in training become red lights under meet fatigue.
- **Forcing vertical shins.** It shifts load to the hips and back; let the knees travel forward in line with the toes.
- **Too many cues at once.** Pick one cue per set.
- **A different walkout every time.** Use two or three steps, the same every time, so stance is identical.
- **Ignoring the 2026 bar-position rule.** Very low low-bar positions should be checked against the current rulebook.
- **Switching technique close to a meet.** Make changes in the off-season with a structured trial.

## Key takeaways

- Competition squat: motionless start, "Squat", hip crease below the top of the knee, one descent, lockout, "Rack"; bar position within the 2026 limit (IPF 2026).
- Low bar is usually slightly stronger and more hip-dominant, high bar more upright and quad-dominant, but differences are small when stance is matched.
- Stance width changes adductor and glute involvement more than quadriceps involvement.
- Depth trains the quads hardest and transfers best; film it.
- Knees past the toes is normal; restricting it moves load to the hips and back.
- Brace with a held breath; raised-heel shoes help upright squatters.
- Choose variations by the fault they fix, and test whether they worked.
