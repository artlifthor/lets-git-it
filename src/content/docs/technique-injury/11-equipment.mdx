## In one minute

Classic (raw) powerlifting allows a small set of personal equipment: a **belt**, **knee sleeves**, **wrist wraps**, **shoes**, a **singlet**, a **T-shirt**, socks and chalk. Equipped lifting adds supportive suits, bench shirts and knee wraps. Belts have the best evidence: they raise pressure inside the abdomen, help lifters move faster in small studies, and added about 5 kg to squat 1RM in one study of 25 lifters. Knee sleeves may add a little to a maximal squat; wrist wraps did not change bench press performance in the one study that tested them; heeled shoes let lifters squat with a more upright trunk and more knee bend. At IPF competitions, singlets, belts, knee sleeves and wrist wraps (and all equipped gear) must come from manufacturers on the IPF **Approved List**; check the current list for which items it covers. Every item must also meet the rulebook's size limits, so buy legal gear early and train in it.

## 1. What the rules allow

The **IPF Technical Rulebook** (edition effective 1 March 2026) sets what can be worn. <Evidence level="practice" /> The table summarises the main personal equipment rules; always check the current rulebook and the current **Approved List of Apparel and Equipment** before buying or competing, because both are updated.

| Item | Classic (raw) | Equipped | Key rules |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Singlet** | Required | Allowed (or a supportive suit) | Non-supportive, one piece, single thickness; from an approved manufacturer. Leg length 3–25 cm from the crotch; long-legged approved singlets allowed. Same singlet for all three lifts. |
| **T-shirt** | Required under the singlet in all three lifts | Supportive shirt may replace it | Fabric or synthetic textile, no rubberised/stretch material, pockets, buttons or reinforced seams; round neck; form-fitting sleeves ending below the deltoid and above the elbow (not rolled up). |
| **Belt** | Allowed | Allowed | Approved manufacturer; non-stretch leather, vinyl or similar; no padding. **Max width 10 cm, max thickness 13 mm.** Prong(s) or lever buckle. Worn outside the singlet or suit. |
| **Knee sleeves** | Allowed | Allowed instead of knee wraps (never both) | Approved manufacturer; single ply of neoprene (fabric cover allowed). **Max 7 mm thick, max 30 cm long.** No straps or Velcro; centred over the knee; not touching socks or a regular singlet. |
| **Wrist wraps** | Allowed | Allowed | Approved manufacturer (check the current list). **Max 1 m long (including Velcro and loop), 8 cm wide**; covering width max 12 cm: no more than 10 cm above or 2 cm below the centre of the wrist joint. The thumb loop must be off the thumb during the lift. Not combined with sweatbands. |
| **Knee wraps** | **Not allowed** | Allowed | **Max 2 m long, 8 cm wide**; only in competitions designated as equipped. |
| **Supportive suit and bench shirt** | Not allowed | Allowed | Must be on the Approved List. |
| **Shoes** | Required | Required | Indoor sports shoes, weightlifting/powerlifting shoes or deadlift slippers. **Sole max 5 cm thick** at any point; flat underside; loose insoles max 1 cm; properly fastened. |
| **Socks** | Allowed; **shin-length socks required for the deadlift** | Same | Must not touch knee sleeves or wraps. No full-length stockings or tights. |
| **Chalk and powder** | Allowed | Allowed | Baby powder, resin, talc or magnesium carbonate on the body and clothing only (not on wraps or the soles of shoes). Oil, grease and lubricants are forbidden. |
| **Medical tape** | Two layers around the thumbs | Same | Anywhere else needs official permission; tape may not help hold the bar. |

Equipment is inspected before the session, and lifting in illegal gear on the platform can lead to disqualification (see Part 4, [Chapter 7: Competition rules](/lets-git-it/peaking/07-competition-rules/)). Lifting straps and hooks are not part of the permitted competition equipment; they are training tools only.

:::tip[In plain words]
In a classic meet you can wear a belt, knee sleeves, wrist wraps, shoes, a singlet with a T-shirt under it, and long socks for the deadlift. Knee wraps, squat suits and bench shirts are only for equipped meets. Your singlet, belt, knee sleeves and wrist wraps must be from makers on the IPF approved list, and everything must fit the size rules.
:::

## 2. Belts

### What a belt does

A belt gives the abdominal muscles something firm to push against when the lifter braces. This raises **intra-abdominal pressure** (pressure inside the belly), which helps stiffen the trunk.

- <Evidence level="moderate" /> Several small laboratory studies found that wearing a belt **raised intra-abdominal pressure** during heavy lifting. In 9 people deadlifting 90% of 1RM, peak pressure was higher and rose earlier with a belt (Harman et al., 1989). In 6 people squatting 90% of 1RM, pressure was higher with a light or heavy belt and lower-back muscle activity was lower (Lander et al., 1990). In 5 people doing sets of 8, pressure was 25–40% higher with a belt (Lander et al., 1992).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> Not every study agrees. During maximal isometric (static) lifting efforts, a belt raised the pressure inside the back muscles and appeared to stiffen the trunk, but **did not change** peak intra-abdominal pressure or maximal lifting capacity (Miyamoto et al., 1999).

### Does it improve performance?

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 25 resistance-trained men and women, back squat 1RM was higher with a belt than without (119.2 vs 114.0 kg on average, about 5 kg more), and bar speed was faster at the same absolute loads (Huang et al., 2026).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 14 men squatting 90% of 1RM, reps were completed faster with a belt, with **no change** in leg or back muscle activity or joint angles (Zink et al., 2001).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 5 people doing sets of 8, belted reps were generally faster, especially later in the set (Lander et al., 1992).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In an observational study of 20 recreational male lifters, deadlifts were completed faster with a belt and wrist straps together, and lifters rated the effort as easier when they wore a belt, straps or both (Fong et al., 2022).

### Does it prevent injury?

<Evidence level="moderate" /> There is **no good evidence that belts prevent back injury**. A systematic review of back belts for workers who lift (10 studies) found conflicting results and no high-quality trials supporting belt use to prevent low back pain (Ammendolia et al., 2005). That evidence is from occupational back belts, not lifting belts used for maximal lifts. <Evidence level="practice" /> The reasons to use a belt in powerlifting are performance and confidence, not injury prevention.

:::tip[In plain words]
A belt gives your stomach muscles something to push against, so you can brace harder. One study found it helped people squat about 5 kg more, and small studies show it helps you lift a little faster. It won't stop you getting hurt, so you still need to learn to brace well without it.
:::

### Choosing and using a belt

| Choice | Options | Practical notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Thickness** | 10 mm or 13 mm (13 mm is the IPF maximum) | 10 mm is easier to break in and more comfortable for most lifters; 13 mm is stiffer. Neither is proven better. |
| **Width** | Up to 10 cm, usually the same width all the way round | A full 10 cm belt suits most lifters; shorter-torso lifters sometimes find it digs into the ribs or hips. |
| **Buckle** | Single prong, double prong or lever | Lever: fast, same tightness every time, but changing the setting needs a screwdriver. Prong: easy to adjust between lifts (e.g., looser for deadlift). Double prong is slower to fasten. |
| **Legal status** | Must be from a manufacturer on the IPF Approved List for IPF meets | Check the current list before buying. A non-approved belt is fine for training but not for the platform. |

<Evidence level="practice" /> **How to wear it:**

- **Position:** most lifters wear it around the belly button, covering the lower ribs to the top of the hips. Many deadlifters wear it slightly higher or looser so it doesn't block the start position.
- **Tightness:** tight enough to brace hard against, loose enough to take a big breath into the belly first. You should be able to slide a flat hand between belt and belly before bracing.
- **Brace into it:** breathe in, brace the whole trunk out against the belt, then lift. A belt worn loosely without bracing adds little.

<Evidence level="practice" /> **When to wear it in training:**

- Warm-ups and lighter sets: usually without a belt, to practise bracing on your own.
- Heavy sets of the competition squat and deadlift (roughly RPE 7.5+ or above about 80% of 1RM): belted, to train the way you will compete.
- Some lifters keep one beltless squat or deadlift session (or variation) per week to keep trunk strength and bracing skill without the belt.
- Bench press: a belt is optional; some lifters like it for stability.

## 3. Knee sleeves and knee wraps

### Knee sleeves (classic and equipped)

Knee sleeves are neoprene tubes. They keep the knee warm, give a feeling of support and compression, and store a small amount of elastic energy at the bottom of a squat.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 15 resistance-trained men who had never used knee sleeves, back squat 1RM was about 5 kg higher in neoprene sleeves (166 kg with either a normal-size or a one-size-smaller sleeve) than in a minimally supportive control sleeve (161 kg). The tighter sleeve gave **no extra benefit** over normal sizing, and sleeves did not change jump power or leg extension performance (Machek et al., 2021).
- <Evidence level="practice" /> Many lifters wear sleeves for comfort and warmth, especially with achy knees. There is no good evidence that sleeves prevent knee injuries.

:::tip[In plain words]
Knee sleeves keep your knees warm and feel supportive, and in one study they helped people squat about 5 kg more. Going a size smaller didn't help any further, so buy the size the maker recommends.
:::

### Knee wraps (equipped only)

Knee wraps are long elastic bandages wrapped tightly around the knee. They store much more elastic energy than sleeves.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 10 trained men squatting 80% of 1RM, knee wraps made the lowering phase 45% faster and increased the upward push (10% more vertical impulse, 10% more peak power). They also changed squat technique, with less forward-backward bar movement. The authors suggested this may shift which muscles do the work and may stress the knee joint (Lake et al., 2012).

<Evidence level="practice" /> Knee wraps are illegal in classic lifting. Equipped lifters use them on heavy squats; they need practice, as they change the squat's rhythm and position.

:::tip[In plain words]
Knee wraps are long, tight elastic wraps used only in equipped lifting. They are not allowed in classic meets. They store energy at the bottom of the squat and help you bounce out of it.
:::

### Choosing and using knee sleeves

| Choice | Guidance |
|---|---|
| **Thickness** | 7 mm (the IPF maximum) is standard for squatting; 5 mm is cooler and lighter for general training. |
| **Size** | Follow the manufacturer's chart (usually measured around the knee or thigh). Very tight sleeves take time and effort to get on and showed no extra benefit (Machek et al., 2021). |
| **Legal status** | Approved manufacturer only; max 7 mm × 30 cm; continuous tube, no straps or Velcro. |
| **Putting them on** | At IPF meets, a helper may assist by hand, and socks may be used to slide them on; plastic sliding devices and lubricants are not allowed. Practise your method in training. |
| **When to wear** | Squat working sets and heavy squat variations; optional for deadlifts (some sumo pullers like them); many lifters wear them for all squats if their knees feel better. |

## 4. Wrist wraps

Wrist wraps limit how far the wrist bends back under a heavy bar. Lifters use them for the bench press and, sometimes, low-bar squats.

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> A study in resistance-trained men and women reported that **wrist wraps did not affect bench press strength, power or endurance** (Harris et al., 2024). The indexed abstract does not give the sample size or effect sizes, so treat this as a single finding.
- <Evidence level="practice" /> The main reasons to wear them are comfort and keeping the wrist stacked over the forearm, especially for lifters with wrist pain or who lose wrist position under heavy loads.

| Choice | Guidance |
|---|---|
| **Stiffness** | Stiff wraps give more support for heavy bench; flexible wraps are more comfortable for squats and lighter work. |
| **Length** | Shorter wraps (roughly 30–60 cm) are common in classic lifting; the IPF maximum is 1 m including Velcro and loop. |
| **Legal status** | From a manufacturer on the IPF Approved List for IPF meets; check the current list. |
| **How to wrap** | Cover the wrist joint itself, not just the forearm. Stay within 10 cm above and 2 cm below the wrist centre for competition. Remove the thumb loop from the thumb before lifting. |
| **When to wear** | Heavy bench press sets (roughly RPE 7.5+); low-bar squats if the wrist hurts. Train without them on lighter sets if the wrists feel fine. |

:::tip[In plain words]
Wrist wraps stop your wrists from bending back under a heavy bar. They probably won't add kilos to your bench, but they can make heavy sets more comfortable. Wrap over the wrist joint, and take the loop off your thumb before you lift.
:::

## 5. Shoes

Shoe choice changes squat mechanics. The main choice is a **raised, solid heel** (weightlifting or "squat" shoes) versus a **flat, thin sole** (flat trainers, wrestling shoes or deadlift slippers).

- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 25 college-aged participants squatting 60% of 1RM, weightlifting shoes **reduced forward trunk lean** compared with running shoes; the thigh did not reach a deeper position (Sato et al., 2012).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 32 participants, weightlifting shoes reduced ankle bend and increased knee bend in loaded and unloaded squats, and gave a more upright trunk in unloaded squats. Experienced squatters made more use of this effect in unloaded squats than novices (Legg et al., 2017).
- <Evidence level="emerging" /> In 24 experienced weightlifters squatting 80% of 1RM, both shoe types produced larger **knee** extension demands than squatting barefoot, while barefoot squatting produced larger **hip** extension demands. Footwear redistributes the work between joints (Southwell et al., 2016).

<Evidence level="practice" /> What this means for lifters:

| Shoe type | Effect | Usually suits |
|---|---|---|
| **Raised heel (weightlifting/squat shoes)** | More upright trunk, more knee bend, less ankle bend needed | High-bar or narrow-stance squatters; lifters with limited ankle mobility; lifters who tip forward out of the hole |
| **Flat, thin sole** | More hip-dominant squat; shortest distance for the deadlift; stable, close to the floor | Low-bar or wide-stance squatters; deadlifts (conventional and sumo) |
| **Bench press** | Either; choose what lets you set your feet and drive with the legs | Personal preference; many lifters bench in their squat shoes |

<Evidence level="practice" /> Shoe rules at IPF meets: sole no thicker than 5 cm, a flat underside, no adhesives, and fastened laces or straps. Socks with rubber soles are not allowed. Never change shoes in the last few weeks before a meet.

:::tip[In plain words]
Shoes with a raised heel help you squat more upright with your knees further forward. Flat shoes suit low-bar squats and deadlifts. Pick what fits your squat style, and don't change shoes just before a meet.
:::

## 6. Chalk, singlets and the rest

| Item | What to know |
|---|---|
| **Chalk (magnesium carbonate)** | Improves grip by drying the hands. Use on hands for deadlifts and bench, and on the upper back for squats (if the gym allows). Never on the soles of shoes or on wraps at a meet. |
| **Baby powder / talc** | Some deadlifters put powder on the thighs to reduce bar friction. Allowed on the body and clothing at IPF meets; oil and lubricants are banned. Check your gym's rules first. |
| **Singlet** | Must be from an approved manufacturer. Train in it a few times before a meet to check fit and feel, especially if you plan a long-legged singlet. |
| **T-shirt** | Plain or with permitted logos, sleeves between the deltoid and elbow. Some lifters use a grippy cotton shirt to stop the bar sliding on the squat. |
| **Deadlift socks** | Shin-length socks are required for the deadlift; light shin guards between sock and shin are allowed. |
| **Lifting straps** | Training only. Useful for high-rep pulls and rows when grip fails before the target muscles; not permitted in IPF competition (the rulebook does not list them as allowed equipment, and even tape may not be used to help hold the bar), so keep grip training in the plan. |

:::tip[In plain words]
Chalk helps your grip. Bring a legal singlet and T-shirt, long socks for the deadlift, and practise in everything before the meet. Straps are fine for training but not allowed on the platform.
:::

## 7. Buying checklist

<Evidence level="practice" /> For a lifter planning to compete in IPF classic meets, buy in this order:

| Priority | Item | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Shoes suited to your squat (and flat shoes or slippers for deadlift if wanted) | Affects every squat session |
| 2 | Belt from an IPF-approved manufacturer | Used on every heavy squat and deadlift; buy a legal one once |
| 3 | Knee sleeves from an IPF-approved manufacturer | Comfort and a small squat benefit |
| 4 | Wrist wraps from an IPF-approved manufacturer | Inexpensive; comfort on heavy bench |
| 5 | Approved singlet, T-shirt, shin-length socks | Needed only for meets; buy a few months before the first one |
| — | Chalk | Cheap and useful from day one |

**Before buying any competition gear:** find the manufacturer on the current IPF Approved List, check the product meets the size limits, and check any extra requirements from your national federation (in the Philippines, PAP's meet announcements).

:::tip[In plain words]
Buy competition gear only from makers on the IPF approved list, and check that each item fits the size rules. Start with shoes and a belt, then add knee sleeves and wrist wraps.
:::

## Sample programs

Example lifter: intermediate, squat 180 kg, bench press 120 kg, deadlift 210 kg. Loads come from the RTS chart in [Part 1, Chapter 5](/lets-git-it/foundations/05-measuring-intensity/), rounded to 2.5 kg. The point here is the **Gear** column: when to wear what.

### Program 1: Off-season week (4 days)

**Why it's built this way:** heavy competition-lift sets are done in the gear you will compete in. Lighter, higher-rep work is done with less gear, to keep bracing and wrist strength without support. Heavy squat (Monday) and heavy deadlift (Thursday) are 72 hours apart.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load | Gear |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 1 × 3, then 3 × 5 | RPE 8 (~155 kg), then RPE 7 (~142.5 kg) | Squat shoes, sleeves, belt (warm-ups up to ~120 kg beltless) |
| Mon | Bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 (~92.5 kg) | Wrist wraps optional |
| Tue | Close-grip bench press | 4 × 6 | RPE 7 | No wraps |
| Tue | Romanian deadlift | 3 × 8 | RPE 7 | No belt; straps allowed if grip fails first |
| Tue | Chest-supported row | 4 × 10 | RPE 8 | Straps optional |
| Thu | Deadlift | 1 × 3, then 3 × 4 | RPE 8 (~180 kg), then 70% (147.5 kg) | Flat shoes, belt on the top set; back-off sets beltless, no straps |
| Thu | Overhead press | 3 × 8 | RPE 8 | — |
| Sat | Squat | 3 × 6 | 65% (117.5 kg) | Squat shoes; no belt; sleeves optional |
| Sat | Bench press | 3 × 5 | RPE 7 (~95 kg) | Wrist wraps on working sets |

### Program 2: Three weeks before a meet (full competition gear)

**Why it's built this way:** every heavy single is a dress rehearsal in the exact meet gear: approved singlet, T-shirt, belt and sleeves, meet shoes, shin-length socks and chalk. Equipment problems (a sleeve that is too tight, a belt notch that blocks the deadlift start) must show up now, not on meet day. Heavy squat (Monday) and heavy deadlift (Thursday) are 72 hours apart.

| Day | Exercise | Sets × reps | Load | Gear |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat | 1 × 1, then 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~165 kg), then RPE 7 (~150 kg) | Full meet gear for the single: singlet over T-shirt, belt, sleeves, meet squat shoes. Back-offs: belt and sleeves |
| Mon | Bench press (competition pause) | 1 × 1, then 3 × 3 | RPE 8 (~110 kg), then RPE 7 (~100 kg) | Meet wrist wraps, wrapped exactly as on the day; singlet still on |
| Wed | Bench press | 4 × 4 | RPE 7 (~97.5 kg) | Wraps optional |
| Wed | Row; triceps | 3 × 10 each | RPE 8 | — |
| Thu | Deadlift | 1 × 1, then 2 × 3 | RPE 8 (~192.5 kg), then RPE 7 (~175 kg) | Singlet, T-shirt, shin-length socks, meet deadlift shoes, belt at deadlift notch, chalk; no straps |
| Sat | Squat | 3 × 2 | RPE 7 (~155 kg) | Belt, sleeves, meet shoes |
| Sat | Bench press | 3 × 3 | RPE 7 (~100 kg) | Meet wrist wraps |

**Gear checklist from this week to the meet:** check every item against the current IPF Approved List and size rules; pack spares (second pair of wrist wraps, spare socks, spare shoelaces); don't buy anything new.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying gear that isn't approved.** A belt, sleeves or wrist wraps from a non-approved brand can't be used at an IPF meet. Check the list before you buy.
- **Wearing the belt for everything.** Belting every warm-up and light set means the lifter never practises bracing without it.
- **Never training in competition gear.** Sleeves, belt notch, shoes and singlet all change how the lifts feel. Rehearse in them.
- **Sizing sleeves down for "extra pop".** A smaller size gave no extra benefit in research and costs time and effort to put on.
- **Expecting gear to prevent injury.** There is no good evidence that belts or sleeves prevent injury; load management does that job.
- **Changing shoes close to a meet.** A new heel height changes squat mechanics; make shoe changes in the off-season.
- **Using knee wraps in classic training without a reason.** They change squat technique and are illegal in classic competition.
- **Forgetting the small items.** Long socks for the deadlift, a legal T-shirt and a spare pair of wraps are easy to forget and can cause problems at the meet.

## Key takeaways

- Classic lifting allows a belt, knee sleeves, wrist wraps, shoes, a singlet, a T-shirt, socks and chalk; knee wraps and supportive suits/shirts are for equipped lifting only.
- At IPF meets, singlets, belts, knee sleeves and wrist wraps (and all equipped gear) must come from manufacturers on the Approved List; check the current list for which items it covers. All gear must also meet the size limits (e.g., belt ≤10 cm × 13 mm; sleeves ≤7 mm × 30 cm; wrist wraps ≤1 m × 8 cm).
- Belts raise intra-abdominal pressure, in one study added about 5 kg to squat 1RM, and in several small studies increased bar speed; they are not proven to prevent injury.
- Knee sleeves added about 5 kg to squat 1RM in one study; tighter sizing gave no extra benefit. Wrist wraps did not change bench performance in one study but help comfort.
- Heeled shoes give a more upright, knee-forward squat; flat shoes suit low-bar squats and deadlifts.
- Wear full gear on heavy competition-lift sets, use less gear on lighter work, and rehearse in meet gear before competing.
